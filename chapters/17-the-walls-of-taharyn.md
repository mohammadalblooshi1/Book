# Chapter Seventeen
## The Walls of Taharyn

> *Behind every wall burns a lantern; behind every lantern, a fear.*
> — a saying of the lantern-keepers of Taharyn

---

Taharyn was red.

That's the first thing anyone notices, and the last thing they forget. The whole city was built from the same dark red stone, quarried from the cliffs above the oasis a thousand years ago, so that when the Fire came up in the morning the walls looked wet, as if the city had been carved out of a heart that was still beating.

It sat around the greatest water in the desert, a lake so still that the palms on the far side stood upside down in it. Along the top of the walls, every twenty paces, hung a lantern, burning day and night, because the people of Taharyn were rich, and rich people are afraid of the dark in a way that poor people can't afford to be.

We lay on our bellies at the top of the last dune and looked at it for a long time.

"It's bigger than I thought," said Zayn.

"Everything is bigger than you thought," I said. "You grew up in a village with one well and a goat that thought it was in charge."

He almost smiled. Almost. He'd been doing that more since the mountain caves, in small, rusty bursts, like a door that hadn't been opened in a while.

I want you to see him properly, because there isn't much time left to look. He was thinner than he'd been in Harrow's End, his hair bleached nearly white. Golden cracks ran along both his forearms and up the side of his neck, fading so slowly now that the old ones hadn't gone before the new ones came. Soren's broken sword was strapped across his back in grey cloth, with the letter inside the hilt. He touched it about every hundred breaths, the way he touched his mark.

Behind us, at the bottom of the dune, the pale camel shifted and groaned. Daruun stood beside it with his veil down around his neck, and I could feel him not saying things. He was very good at that. It ran in the family.

"I'll stay out here," he said at last. "The Unnamed won't come through the gates like honest men. They'll come out of the desert. When they do, I'll see them first."

Zayn nodded without turning round.

"And Zayn." His father's voice caught on it, the way it still did. He'd only been allowed to use that name for a few days. "Come back out to me. After. Promise me."

The boy was quiet for a long time.

You know why. You've been listening. He *couldn't* promise it. A promise is a kind of prediction, and the feather on his chest knew the future better than any of us. If he'd tried to say *I promise*, it would have burned like a coal.

"I'll try," he said instead.

My tail glowed. It was true. That was the cruel part: it was completely true, and it wasn't anything like enough.

Daruun looked down at the sand. Then he climbed onto the camel without another word and rode away along the hollow between the dunes, and the wind came behind him and smoothed out his tracks.

"He'll be all right," said Zayn.

I didn't answer. I'd made a rule for myself, after the City of Glass. I would not lie to that boy again. So I said nothing, which is what I'd been doing about a great many things.

---

There were guards on every gate, and every guard knew what to look for.

We'd heard it in the last oasis town, shouted by a crier in the market square for anyone with ears: *a pale-haired boy, a fox with odd eyes, a broken blade. The Prince-Killer. Reward in water tokens, enough to fill a cistern.* The story had run ahead of us across the whole desert the way stories do, faster than camels and twice as hungry.

So we didn't use the gates.

"He told me about it," Zayn said. We were crouched in the reeds at the south end of the lake, where the water slid out under the city wall through a low arch of red stone, black with moss. "By the fire, the second night. He said there was an old channel the water-carriers used before they dug the new cisterns. It runs under the city and comes up beneath the palace kitchens." He paused. "He used it to sneak out. And to sneak back in. He said it was the only part of the palace nobody ever guarded, because nobody important ever went there."

"What did he sneak back in *for*?"

Zayn did smile then, a real one, small and crooked and full of grief. "Honey cakes."

That was Soren. That was exactly Soren. A prince of the greatest city in the desert, and the most daring thing he ever did as a child was crawl through a sewer for cake.

The channel was dark and cold and waist-deep on Zayn, which meant it was considerably more than waist-deep on me. I rode on his shoulder with my tail held up out of the water, complaining about the smell, the cold, and the fact that I had outlived four kingdoms only to drown in a palace drain. Zayn let me. I think it helped him to have something to listen to that wasn't his own heart. His mark glowed through his wet shirt and gave us just enough light to see by.

After a very long time the channel ended in a row of slippery steps and a wooden hatch.

Zayn pushed it up an inch. Warm air came down through the gap, and with it a smell so wonderful I nearly fell off his shoulder: bread, onions, cinnamon, roasting meat, and honey. Lots of honey.

He climbed out into a storeroom full of sacks and jars and hanging garlic, and found himself face to face with a cleaver.

---

The woman holding it was the largest person I had seen in some centuries.

She was nearly as tall as Daruun and twice as wide, with forearms like a blacksmith's and a face as round and red as one of the city's own bricks. She held the cleaver the way other people hold a sword.

"Thief," she said. It wasn't a question. Her voice could have stripped paint. "Out of my drain like a rat. You've got a count of three to tell me why I shouldn't—"

Then she saw the sword.

The grey wrapping had come loose in the channel. The hilt stuck up over Zayn's shoulder, and the broken stub of blade beneath it, and the light from the kitchen doorway fell right across them.

The cleaver dropped an inch.

"Where," she said, in a completely different voice, "did you get that?"

Zayn could have run. He could have lied, or tried to, and taken the burn. Instead he stood there, dripping on her clean floor, and told her the truth, because that was the only thing he had ever known how to do.

"It was Soren's," he said. "He was my friend. I didn't kill him."

I sat on a sack of lentils and let my tail do the rest. It lit up gold, bright as a lamp, so bright the jars on the shelves threw shadows.

"His tail does that when someone's telling the truth," Zayn said, a little apologetically.

"It also does it when it's warm and dry," I said. "Which it isn't. Thank you for asking."

The woman shrieked. Then she clapped a hand over her own mouth, and stood there with her eyes going wide and then wet, staring from me to the boy to the broken sword. The cleaver clattered onto the stone.

"I knew it," she whispered through her fingers. "I *knew* it. I told them. I told every one of them, and nobody listened to the cook."

She was Mother Yusa. She had run the palace kitchens for thirty years and half-raised the king's two sons, because the king was busy being a king and somebody had to make sure the boys ate. She told us this very fast, while she sat Zayn on a crate and threw a sack over his shoulders and put lentils and a plate of honey cakes in front of him, all without seeming to breathe.

"That boy," she said, pointing a floury finger at the hilt as if Soren himself were inside it, "stole honey cakes from my kitchen for ten years. Ten years! Came up out of that drain smelling like a dead fish, every single time, and thought I didn't know." Her chin wobbled. She steadied it. "A boy like that doesn't kill anyone. And neither do you. Eat."

Zayn ate.

He ate the way he'd eaten in Harrow's End when Auntie Sabba's bundles came, slowly and carefully, like a boy who'd learned that good things might be taken away if he looked too pleased about them. Mother Yusa watched every mouthful. When he got to the honey cakes, he stopped, and held one in his palm for a long time without eating it.

"He told me about these," he said quietly. "He said nobody in the world made them like you."

Mother Yusa turned round and went to stir a pot that didn't need stirring, and her big shoulders shook for a while.

---

She hid us in the back storeroom, behind the oil jars, with blankets and a saucer of cream. And she told us about the palace.

The king did not eat. That was the first thing she said, as if it were the worst. Since the news of Soren's death, King Absalon had sent back every plate, and the only person he would listen to was his vizier.

"Orrin," said Zayn.

She spat into the fire. I liked her very much at that moment.

"Grey eyes. Soft voice. You never hear him coming, that one. He's brought the king Soren's ring. Says he took it off the body of the Prince-Killer's victim to bring it home." She looked at Zayn sideways. "The king wears it on a chain round his neck now. Over his heart."

Zayn had gone very still. His hand had gone to the hilt.

"And Idan," Mother Yusa went on, more quietly. "The little one. He's barely said a word since. Comes down here some nights when he can't sleep." She wiped her clean hands on her apron. "He was going to be like his brother. Now he's just quiet."

Then she asked the question I'd been waiting for since she picked up the cleaver.

"How did he die?" she said. "Our Soren. Was it… did it hurt him?"

Zayn opened his mouth and closed it again. He didn't know. That was the truth, and it was the whole truth, and it had been cutting him every night for a turn of the Ash. He had walked away. He had been somewhere else in the dark when it happened.

"I wasn't there," he said. "I should have been."

My tail glowed. I looked at the floor.

Mother Yusa looked at me. I don't know what she saw. Something in the way I sat, perhaps, or the way I didn't. She was a woman who had spent thirty years knowing which kitchen boy had broken a jar just by the set of his shoulders.

She didn't ask me anything. I was grateful. I was ashamed of being grateful.

I'll tell you about that night soon, little one. Not yet. Let me be a coward a few pages longer.

---

Idan came down after midnight.

We heard the hatch at the top of the back stairs, and soft bare feet, and then the sound of someone very carefully lifting the lid off the honey-cake jar in exactly the way that someone else had probably taught him to.

Zayn stood up behind the oil jars. The mark on his chest glowed.

The boy froze with a cake halfway to his mouth.

He was ten. Small for it, with his brother's dark eyes and none of his brother's swagger. He stared at the glow, and at the hilt over Zayn's shoulder, and the cake fell out of his hand.

"That's Soren's," he whispered.

"Yes," said Zayn.

"They said you killed him."

"I didn't."

Gold, steady and warm, from the tip of my tail.

Idan looked at it. Then he looked at me. "The fox's tail is glowing," he said.

"It does that," I said. "Your brother tried to eat a scorpion once, did you know? Was sick for a whole day. Looked like a green lizard. I've never been prouder of anyone."

I don't know why I said it. I'm not good with children. I've told you that. But Idan made a sound, a small, cracked, astonished sound, and it took me a moment to understand that it was a laugh, and that it was probably the first one in a turn of the Ash.

Then he sat down on the floor and cried, and Zayn sat down beside him, and neither of them said anything for a long time. Zayn didn't hug him. He didn't know how, yet. He just sat close enough that their shoulders touched, the way you'd sit beside someone at a fire to share the warmth.

It was the right thing. It's almost always the right thing.

Later, when the crying had worn itself out, Zayn unwrapped the sword and twisted the pommel, and the hilt came open, and he took out the letter. It was creased soft from handling. The seal on it was dark red wax, pressed with a circle that had nothing inside it.

"Can you read?" Idan asked.

"Yes," said Zayn. And then, because the truth always came with him whether he wanted it or not: "Your brother taught me. In the sand. With a stick." He touched the letters on the page, one by one, as if they were still warm. "He was a bad teacher. He kept laughing."

Idan read the letter by the light of Zayn's mark. I watched his face change as he got to the end. *On the Night of Embers, when the lanterns are lit. The king first. Then the city.*

"The Night of Embers is tomorrow," Idan said.

"I know."

"And the Ash will be full."

Zayn didn't say anything. He touched his mark. I watched his fingers press it, once, the way you press a bruise.

"Father holds petitions in the morning," Idan said. "Before the festival. Anyone can come and speak, if they're brave enough." He swallowed. "I could take you in."

---

The throne hall of Taharyn had a ceiling so high that the lanterns hanging from it looked like Embers. At the far end, seven steps rose to a chair of black rock, and in the chair sat the saddest man I have ever seen.

King Absalon had been tall and broad once. You could see the shape of it, the way you can see the shape of a house after the roof has fallen in. Around his neck, on a thin gold chain, a heavy ring rested against his chest, and every few moments his hand drifted up to touch it, the same way Zayn touched his mark.

Beside the throne, one step below it, stood Orrin.

He looked exactly as he had in the oasis town. Silver hair, smooth face, soft robes the colour of sand. His faded grey eyes moved across the hall and found Zayn the very instant the boy stepped out from behind Idan, and something happened in his face that I didn't understand until later.

He smiled.

Not the wide smile of a man who's won. A small one. Satisfied. The smile of a man who sets a trap for a fox and comes back in the morning to find the fox exactly where he expected.

"The Prince-Killer!" someone shouted, and then everyone was shouting. Guards ran. Spears came down. A courtier in a blue robe shrieked "*Monster!*" in a voice like a goose, and I saw Zayn's jaw tighten, and I saw the gold on his skin flicker grey for a single breath.

"*Stop!*" Idan had run forward and planted himself in front of Zayn with his arms out. He was ten years old and shaking and he didn't move. "He has something. Father, he has something for you. You have to *listen*."

It was the first time anyone in that hall had heard the younger prince raise his voice in a turn of the Ash. Even the guards hesitated.

The king looked at his son for a long moment. Then he lifted one tired hand, and the spears went up.

So Zayn walked down the long red floor between the columns, with the whole court of Taharyn staring at him, and climbed to the bottom step of the throne, and held up the letter.

He read it aloud. Every word. His voice didn't shake. Soren had made him practise until it didn't.

When he finished, the hall was so quiet I could hear the lanterns hissing.

"That," said Zayn, "is Vizier Orrin's seal. Soren took this letter from one of the Unnamed. He hid it in his sword. They killed him before he could bring it home."

The king took the letter in his hands and looked at the seal for a long time. Then he looked at Orrin.

And Orrin sighed, very gently, like a teacher whose favourite pupil has made a small mistake.

"Your Majesty will remember," he said, "that I reported my seal stolen on the night of the prince's exile. It is written in the palace records." He spread his soft hands. "I did not wish to believe Soren had taken it. But here it is. A forgery in a broken sword, carried by the boy who killed him, on the very eve of the Night of Embers." He shook his head sorrowfully. "It is clever. I'll grant them that."

You see? Long before Soren ever found that letter, Orrin had made sure that if it was found, it would look like a lie.

I hated him then more than I have hated anything in a long time.

"Did you kill Soren?" said Zayn.

He hadn't meant to ask. It came out of him the way the Light did, without permission.

Orrin looked down at him with those faded grey eyes, soft and patient and sad. "I loved that boy," he said, "as if he were my own son."

From the cord at Zayn's belt, Hesset's little bronze truth bell rang.

It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. In that silent red hall it sounded like a stone dropped down a well. Every head turned toward it.

"A desert trinket," said Orrin, without looking at it. His voice didn't change at all. "Bells ring when boys shake them, Majesty."

"He didn't shake it," said Idan.

"I didn't kill your son," Zayn said to the king. His voice was very low. "I left him alone that night. We argued, and I walked away, and when I came back he was gone. That's what I did. That's the worst thing I've ever done. But I didn't kill him."

The mark on his chest didn't burn. I sat at his feet, and my tail blazed gold, so bright that the courtiers nearest us stepped back and shaded their eyes.

The king looked at me. Then at the boy. Then at Orrin, at the man who had stood one step below his throne for twenty years.

I watched him waver. I watched it happen in his face like a dune shifting in the night. He wanted to believe us. I think, underneath everything, he had always known something was wrong. But believing us meant believing that he had exiled his son for a lie, and mourned him for a lie, and worn his ring over his heart because the man who killed him had handed it to him. No father can look straight at a thing like that. Not all at once.

His hand went to the ring on his chest.

"Hold the boy," said King Absalon at last, so quietly that the guards had to lean in. "Not in the cells. In the east tower, under guard. After the Night of Embers, I will hear all of this again. Properly. From both of you." He looked at Orrin. "Both."

"Of course, Majesty," said Orrin, and bowed. "Shall we cancel the festival? For safety?"

The king closed his eyes. "No. The people need their lanterns."

"As you wish."

And as the guards took Zayn's arms, I saw the vizier's face, just for an instant, when he thought no one was watching.

He was still smiling.

---

The tower room was small and round and had a window that looked east.

They had taken the sword. They'd taken the bell. They hadn't taken me, because nobody had quite worked out how you arrest a fox, and I wasn't going to help them.

Zayn sat on the floor with his back to the wall and watched the light change.

Below us, all over the red city, people were hanging lanterns. They strung them across the lanes and set them in windows and on rooftops, and down at the palace steps, servants were carrying out eleven great lanterns of bronze and coloured glass, one for each of the lost bearers. Eleven lanterns for eleven children whose names nobody remembered. I'd known every one of them.

The Fire went down. The Ash came up over the eastern dunes. It was nearly full. One more night and it would be perfectly round.

There was a soft sound at the door. Not a guard. Guards stamp.

Orrin's face appeared at the little barred window in the wood. In the dark, his eyes looked almost white.

"Thank you," he said gently, "for coming."

Zayn didn't move.

"I've wondered for a turn of the Ash how to bring you here," Orrin went on. "Inside the walls. On the Night of Embers. Under the full Ash. And you came by yourself, through the kitchen drain, with a letter in your hand." He sounded almost fond. "The desert really does write the sands, doesn't it?"

My tail glowed.

It glowed bright gold in that dark little room, and Zayn saw it, and I saw him understand. Everything Orrin had said was true. That was what made it so terrible.

The vizier's footsteps went away down the stair.

For a long time neither of us spoke. Then Zayn got up and went to the window, and I hopped up onto the sill beside him.

Below us, the city was full of soft, waiting light. And on the roof of the building opposite, just at the edge of the lantern-glow, a figure in a grey robe crouched with two grey blades across his knees. His face was a smooth white mask. There was a crack running down the middle of it, from brow to chin.

He was looking up at our window.

And very faintly, under the wind, so faint I might have been the only one in the world who could hear it, he was humming.

---

*End of Chapter Seventeen*
