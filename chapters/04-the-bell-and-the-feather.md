# Chapter Four
## The Bell and the Feather

> *The bell rings for the liar, and the liar is often you.*
> — words stamped around the rim of an old healer's bell

---

His tent was gone.

Not burned. I'd half expected burning, and so had he, I think. But the people of Harrow's End were too frightened that night even for fire. Fire makes light, and light, they had just learned, brought things out of the dark.

So they had simply torn it down. Somebody had pulled up the stakes and slashed the patched walls in nine colours, and then trampled the whole thing flat into the sand, the way you'd stamp on a scorpion you weren't brave enough to pick up. His blanket was gone. His water skin was gone. The little clay jar where he kept his water tokens had been smashed, and the tokens were gone too, of course. Fear doesn't stop people from being practical.

Zayn stood looking at it for a while in the dark.

"Well," I said. "At least you won't have to pack."

He didn't laugh. I hadn't really expected him to. He knelt in the wreckage and began to dig through it with his hands, quietly, methodically, the way he dug through the ruins. A scavenger to the last.

He found the twist of waxed paper first. Four dates rolled in honey, the ones he'd been saving since the day before all this began. Somebody had stepped on them. They were squashed flat and full of sand. He brushed them off carefully and put them inside his shirt with Auntie Sabba's bread.

Then he found the cloth.

It had been buried under the collapsed tent pole, half sunk in the sand. It was a square of grey fabric, softer than anything else he owned, woven with a pattern of small strange marks that no weaver in Harrow's End had ever been able to name. The desert had wrapped him in it fifteen years ago, when it left him on the edge of the village. He'd slept with it under his head every night of his life.

He held it against his face for a moment. Then he folded it small and tucked it away with the rest.

"That's everything," he said.

It wasn't much. A knife, a cloth, some bread, four ruined dates. Fifteen years in Harrow's End, and that was everything.

---

Hesset was waiting for us.

Of course he was. The lamp was still burning when we reached his tent, the bells turning slowly on their threads in the warm air that spilled out through the flap. There was tea already poured. Two cups. Hesset sat cross-legged on his worn rug, his milky eyes fixed on nothing, his white beard falling over his folded hands.

"Sit," he said, before Zayn had even ducked through the doorway. "You're bleeding on my herbs."

Zayn didn't sit.

He stood in the doorway with the lamplight on his face, and the golden cracks along his jaw were faded now, almost gone, just a faint shimmer like the trail a snail leaves on stone. His shirt was torn at the back and stiff with blood. He was shaking. Not from cold.

"You knew," he said.

Hesset turned his face toward the sound. For a long moment he said nothing.

Then he said, "Sit down, child. Let me see to your back. And then you can shout at me, and I promise to listen to every word."

The bells were silent. Every word of it was true.

---

He sat, in the end. I think he was too tired not to.

Hesset's hands were old and dry and very gentle. He peeled the torn shirt away from Zayn's back and washed the wound with water from a clay bowl, and then he packed it with a paste that smelled of bitterleaf and smoke. Zayn hissed through his teeth. He didn't cry out.

"Close to the old one," Hesset murmured. His fingers moved lightly over the pale, puckered scar under Zayn's left shoulder blade, the one from when he was six. "The sand does like to write in the same place twice."

He turned his blind face toward the corner of the tent, where I was sitting with my tail curled over my paws.

"And you," he said. "You've finally decided to talk, I hear."

"News travels fast," I said.

"Garrem screams very loudly." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's been a long time, fox."

"Not long enough."

Zayn twisted round to stare at us both. "You *know* each other?"

Hesset and I said nothing at the same moment, which I suppose was its own kind of answer.

I'll tell you this much, since you're listening and he wasn't: yes. We knew each other. We had known each other for a very long time, longer than the boy could have guessed, and the last time we'd been in the same place it had ended badly. That's Hesset's story, and he never told it to the boy. So I won't tell it to you. Not yet.

"Drink your tea," said Hesset, and pressed the cup into Zayn's hands.

Zayn drank it. Smoke and mint, bitter as always. He hated it as always. I think it was the first thing that whole night that felt like home.

---

"What am I?" he said at last.

The tea was finished. The lamp had burned down a little. Outside, the wind had come up and was whispering along the walls of the tent, and somewhere far off a dune was groaning as it moved in the dark.

"You know the story," said Hesset.

"The Phoenix-Born." Zayn's voice was flat. "The Weaver puts a piece of its light inside a child. The child carries it across the desert to the highest dune in the world and sets it free and drives the darkness away."

"Yes."

"That's a story. It's just a story you tell."

Hesset said nothing.

"I'm not one of them," said Zayn. "I'm not—"

And he stopped. His face twisted. His hand went to his chest, to the feather mark, and pressed hard, and I saw him bite down on the inside of his cheek.

It was burning.

He'd been about to say *I'm not one of the Phoenix-Born.* He'd wanted it to be true so badly he'd nearly said it. And the mark on his chest had caught the words before they reached his lips and set them on fire.

He sat there with his hand pressed to his chest and his eyes squeezed shut, breathing through his nose, waiting for the pain to fade.

"Ah," said Hesset softly. "Yes. That's the trouble with you, isn't it? You can't even lie to yourself."

---

Hesset rose. It took him a while. His knees cracked like dry sticks. He felt his way along the wall of the tent, past the hanging bitterleaf and the bunches of sand-mint, to an old wooden chest in the far corner. Zayn had seen that chest a thousand times. He'd never once seen it open.

Hesset opened it now. He reached inside without hesitating and brought out a small bundle wrapped in oiled leather.

"When the desert left you on the edge of this village," he said, "you were wrapped in that grey cloth you carry. And tucked inside the cloth, against your chest, was this."

He put the bundle in Zayn's hands.

"Oszkar's father would have sold it. He'd have got a hundred water tokens for it, perhaps more. So I took it before anyone else saw, and I kept it. I've been keeping it for you for fifteen years. Open it."

Zayn opened it.

It was a feather.

It was made of gold, or something like gold. It was as long as his hand and so fine that you could see the lamplight through the edges of it. Every barb was perfect, every tiny line, exactly like the feather of a real bird. But it was broken. It had been snapped clean across the middle, the two halves still clinging together by a few bent barbs, and many of the barbs along one side were twisted and crushed, as if something had tried to tear it apart.

And it was warm.

Zayn held it in both palms and felt the warmth soak into his skin. It was the same warmth as the mark on his chest. The same shape. The same fine lines. He looked from the feather to the mark and back again, and he didn't say anything for a long time.

"It's broken," he said finally.

"Yes," said Hesset.

"Can it be fixed?"

The old man was quiet a moment. "Some things mend themselves," he said, "when they're ready."

That was true too. The bells didn't stir. My tail stayed bright.

I've thought about that feather a great deal since. I watched it, all through the journey that followed. I'll tell you now, because it doesn't spoil anything: it did mend. Slowly. One barb at a time. Every time the boy learned something true about himself, something hard and painful, the feather grew a little more whole.

Every time, I hated it a little more. You'll understand why, eventually.

---

There was a second gift.

Hesset reached up without looking and unhooked one of the bells from its thread, the smallest one, no bigger than the tip of Zayn's thumb. It was bronze, worn smooth with age, and strung on a cord of braided leather.

"This one," Hesset said, "is for you."

He put it around Zayn's neck himself, his old fingers fumbling at the knot.

"You cannot lie. You know that by now. The mark won't let you. But the rest of the world can, and the rest of the world will. This bell will ring when someone lies to you. Not loudly. Not always in time. But it will ring."

Zayn touched it. It lay against his chest just above the feather mark, cool against his skin.

"How do I know it works?"

Hesset lifted his cup and smiled. "My tea," he said, "is the finest in the whole desert."

The bell rang.

It was a small, clear, sweet sound, like a single drop of water falling into a silver cup. And at exactly the same moment, my tail dimmed, the glow going out of it like a breath blown across a lamp, and then coming slowly back.

Zayn looked at the bell. Then he looked at my tail. Then he looked at me.

"Your tail," he said slowly. "When you lie, it…"

"Yes, yes. Glows for the truth, goes dark for a lie. Anybody's lie, not just mine. Very embarrassing. Imagine trying to play cards." I curled the offending thing tighter around my paws. "Now you've got two ways of knowing when somebody's lying to you. Most people don't get one. Try not to waste them."

"And when *you* lie?"

"I don't," I said.

My tail glowed.

He was still staring at it. I could see him thinking back over everything I'd said to him that night, every word, trying to remember whether it had glowed.

It had. All of it.

Later, much later, that would matter more than either of us knew.

---

"Would you die for them?" Hesset asked.

He said it quietly. He'd sat back down on his rug, and he was turning his empty teacup slowly between his palms, and he didn't raise his voice at all. He might have been asking whether Zayn wanted more tea.

Zayn looked up. "What?"

"The people of this village. Oszkar, who called you Sandborn. Garrem, who cheated you every week of your life. The children who threw stones when you were six, and the men who threw them tonight. Bram." He paused. "Would you die for them?"

The tent was very quiet.

Zayn opened his mouth.

I watched him. I think I'll always see it. He opened his mouth to say *yes*, because it was what a hero would say, because it was what the Phoenix-Born said in every story Hesset had ever told him. The feather burned before the word was out. He flinched.

He closed his mouth, and opened it again, to say *no*. Because they had stoned him. Because they had called him monster and torn down his tent and stolen his tokens. Because he didn't owe them anything at all.

That burned too.

He sat there with his hand pressed hard against his chest, his eyes wet, his mouth half open. Neither word would come. Both of them were lies, or both of them were too true to say. I don't know which. I'm not sure he did either.

"I don't know," he whispered finally.

My tail glowed. The bell was silent.

Hesset nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd expected.

"Good," he said. "That's honest. Hold on to it. It won't be your answer forever."

"Why are you asking me that?"

Hesset didn't answer. He turned the empty cup in his hands, round and round.

---

It was almost dawn when Zayn asked the question that mattered.

"Where do I go?"

"The City of Glass," said Hesset.

Zayn blinked. "That's a story too."

"Most things are, until you walk into them. It's real. It lies deep in the desert, farther than anyone from Harrow's End has ever gone. It's the place where the first bearer said no to the Light, and the sand around him melted into glass." His blind eyes moved, as though he were looking at something a very long way off. "The walls there remember. Everything you need to know about what you are is written on them. Older than me. Older than the fox, almost."

"Steady on," I said.

"How do I find it?"

"You don't," said Hesset. "You walk, and the dunes find it for you. They'll open a way if you're meant to reach it. You've lived beside the desert all your life. You know how it moves."

Zayn turned the broken feather over in his hands. "Will you come with me?"

Something passed across the old man's face then. It was quick, and it was gone almost before I saw it, but I've known Hesset a long time, and I knew what it was. It was shame.

"I don't leave the edge of the desert," he said. "I can't. The desert and I have an agreement. It's an old one, and I made it a long time ago, and I'm still paying for it."

True. The bell didn't stir.

Zayn was quiet. Then he asked it. The same question he'd asked two nights ago, when everything had still been ordinary and his tent had still been standing.

"What happens to them? The Phoenix-Born. When they get to the dune, and they set the light free, and the darkness goes away. What happens to them, after?"

I held my breath.

And Hesset, who had been the oldest and wisest man in Harrow's End for longer than anyone could remember, who had sat among his truth bells for more years than the boy could have guessed and never told a lie that mattered, looked at the boy with his blind eyes and said:

"Go to the City of Glass. The answers there are older than mine, and truer. You'll understand it better when you see it written."

The bell didn't ring.

Of course it didn't. Every word was true. My tail stayed gold. There was nothing in what he'd said for either of us to catch.

But I looked at Hesset across that tent, and Hesset, who could not see me, turned his face toward me anyway. And we both knew.

He had not lied to the boy. He had done something worse. He'd stood beside the truth with a cloth over it and let the boy walk past.

I've told you there's a gap between *the whole truth* and *true*. That night, in that tent, an old man I had once loved like a son pushed a fifteen-year-old boy into that gap, and smiled at him, and wished him well.

I didn't say anything either. Remember that, when you're judging him. I was sitting right there, and I could have said it, and I didn't.

---

"May the sand remember you," said Hesset at the tent flap.

The Embers were fading. The sky in the east was turning from black to the deep, bruised blue that comes just before the Fire. Somewhere behind us, beyond the clay houses of Harrow's End, the grey lay low and flat and patient along the edge of the world. It hadn't moved in the night. It hadn't needed to.

"Will it follow me?" Zayn asked. He was looking toward it. "If I go. Will it leave them alone?"

"I don't know," said Hesset.

The bell was silent. He truly didn't.

Zayn nodded. Then he did something I'd never seen him do in fifteen years. He stepped forward and put his arms around the old man, quickly and clumsily, like someone who's never been taught how. Hesset went very still. Then his dry old hands came up and rested on the boy's back, just for a moment, either side of the new wound.

"Go," Hesset said roughly. "Before I make you drink more tea."

We went.

---

We walked north, into the deep desert, as the dunes were finishing their night's work.

If you have never seen the Destiny Desert move, I'm sorry for you. It's the most beautiful thing in the world, and one of the most frightening. All night the dunes had been shifting, and now, in that last grey hour before dawn, they were settling into their new places. Whole hills of sand slid slowly sideways with a sound like a vast animal breathing. Ridges folded. Hollows opened. Paths that had been there yesterday were gone, swallowed, and new ones lay smooth and fresh between the dunes, untouched by any foot.

One of those new paths opened right in front of us.

It ran straight out from the edge of Harrow's End, between two great golden slopes, as smooth as a swept floor. It hadn't been there an hour ago. Zayn knew every inch of the ground around the village, and he stopped at the start of it and stared.

"It's for me," he said.

"Apparently," I said.

He looked back once.

Harrow's End sat behind us in the blue light, small and dark and huddled around its well. He could just make out the shape of Auntie Sabba's oven, and the flattened patch of sand where his tent had been. At the far edge, a thin thread of smoke rose from Hesset's tent and went straight up into the still air.

And along the eastern side of the village, grey.

He touched the feather mark. He touched the bell. He touched the folded grey cloth inside his shirt, where the broken feather now lay wrapped against Auntie Sabba's bread.

"The sand remembers," he whispered.

Then he turned his back on the only home he'd ever had and walked out into the desert, and the dunes closed softly behind us like a door.

---

About an hour later, as the Fire came up red over the eastern dunes, I found a scorpion.

It was a fat one, the colour of old copper, scuttling out from under a stone to warm itself in the first light. I caught it neatly behind the stinger and sat down in the middle of the path to eat it.

I took my time about it. I chewed with my mouth open. The shell made a wonderful noise, a wet, splintering crunch that echoed off the dunes on either side, and I made sure to crunch every single leg individually.

Zayn stopped walking and turned around.

"Do you have to?" he said.

"I do, actually," I said, with my mouth full. "It's very nutritious. You should try one."

"That's disgusting."

"*You're* disgusting. You smell like bitterleaf and old blood. At least my breakfast was alive this morning."

He stared at me. And for a moment, just a moment, something happened at the very corner of his mouth. It wasn't a smile, not yet. He wouldn't truly laugh for a long time, not until he met someone who could make him. But it was the place a smile would go, one day, if things went well.

He turned and kept walking. I finished my scorpion, and licked my whiskers, and trotted after him.

My tail was glowing. I'll let you work out why.

---

*End of Chapter Four*
