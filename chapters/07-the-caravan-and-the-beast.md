# Chapter Seven
## The Caravan and the Beast

> *When the sky goes dark at noon, pray to the Fire or run.*
> — a caravan saying, older than any caravan still on the road

---

You smell a caravan long before you see it.

Camel, mostly. Camel is a smell that gets into the back of your throat and sets up house there. Then dung smoke, and spice, and the sour tang of lizard, and sweat, and a hundred people who haven't washed in a month because water is money and money is for drinking.

To Zayn, it smelled like a city. He'd never seen one.

We found the road in the middle of the morning, a long line of hard-packed ground marked every few hundred paces with a cairn of piled stones, running roughly north and west between the dunes. And on it, crawling along like a great brown caterpillar, was the caravan of Master Dagan.

Forty camels, roped nose to tail, groaning under bales and water jars. A dozen wagons on wide wooden wheels. Six sand lizards, long and low and armoured in scales the colour of dust, with riders perched on high saddles behind their heads, ranging out along the flanks of the column like dogs around a flock. Falcons rode on hooded perches above the lead wagon, a row of them, hunched and patient. Every now and then one was unhooded and thrown into the air with a little roll of paper tied to its leg, and it went climbing away over the dunes, carrying news to the oasis towns ahead.

Zayn stood on the ridge above the road and watched it pass with his mouth slightly open.

"Close your mouth," I said. "You'll swallow a fly. Or a camel."

---

Master Dagan was a broad, bald, sunburnt man with a beard dyed orange with henna and a voice that could have cracked rock. He looked Zayn up and down the way he'd look at a camel he suspected of being lame.

"Name?"

Zayn hesitated. Then he said the only true thing he could. "In my village they called me Sandborn."

"That's not a name, that's a weather report." Dagan grunted. "Doesn't matter. One of my boys ran off at the last well and I'm short a pair of hands. You can load, you can dig, you can shovel dung?"

"Yes."

"You'll work for water and food and a place by the fire. No tokens. You don't like it, the desert's that way." He jerked a thumb at the horizon, then squinted down at me. "What's that?"

"A fox," said Zayn.

"It's *my* fox," I said, before I could stop myself. "And it has a name, thank you."

There was a long pause.

Dagan looked at me. I looked at Dagan. Somewhere behind him, a camel made a noise like a drain.

"Huh," said Master Dagan at last. "Talking fox. Saw a two-headed goat in the salt towns once." He turned away, already shouting at someone about a loose rope. "Keep it out of the food stores."

That was it. That was all. Caravan men have seen everything, or claim to have. It's one of the things I like about them.

---

Zayn worked.

He was good at it. He'd been doing harder work than this since he was small, alone, for one token a day. He loaded bales at dawn and dug wagon wheels out of drifted sand at noon and walked along the column at dusk with a shovel and a sack. He didn't complain. He didn't talk much either. He kept his scarf wound high around his throat and his shirt tied close across his chest, so nobody would see the faint glow of the feather mark after dark.

He was happy. I don't think he knew it. But I'd watched him for fifteen years, and I knew what his face looked like when nobody was throwing anything at it.

On the second night, a boy came and sat beside him at the fire.

His name was Pell. He was a lizard rider, the youngest in the caravan, perhaps sixteen or seventeen, with a gap between his front teeth and a long scar down one forearm where his lizard had bitten him the first week. He rode a big female called Dustmother, and he talked about her the way some people talk about their grandmothers.

"She'll go three days without water," he told Zayn. "Three! A camel only does that if it's got nowhere better to be. Dustmother does it for spite."

He'd brought dates. Plain ones, not honeyed, but he held out the cloth to Zayn without being asked, and after a moment Zayn took one.

"You're from the edge villages," Pell said. "I can tell. You walk like the sand's going to bite you."

"It does, sometimes."

Pell laughed. It was an easy laugh, loud and unworried. Zayn looked at him as if he'd never heard one up close before.

They talked late into the night. Or Pell talked, and Zayn listened. About the oasis town where Pell's family kept lizards, and the falcons and how you trained them, and the rumours along the road. There was a lot of grey out east this season, Pell said. Whole villages gone quiet. And up in Taharyn, the great red city, the old king had thrown his own eldest son out into the desert for dark magic. Hiding a Hollow-touched boy in the palace, people said, and trying to cure him.

"Cure him!" Pell shook his head. "You can't cure the grey. Everybody knows that."

Zayn said nothing. His hand had gone to his chest.

I lay by the fire with my eyes half closed and said nothing either. My tail stayed very still.

---

On the third day, at noon, the falcons wouldn't fly.

That was the first sign. Dagan's falconer unhooded the lead bird and threw it up into the air, and it went up three wingbeats and came straight back down to its perch, and sat there with its feathers flattened against its body, shivering. He tried another. The same.

The camels started groaning. All of them, all down the line, a long low miserable sound. Then, one by one, without being told, they began to kneel.

"Kiri," Zayn said quietly.

"I know," I said.

The wind had stopped.

Out here, the wind never stops. Not completely. There's always something, even on the stillest day: a breath along the dune crests, a whisper of sand. Now there was nothing. The air hung heavy and hot and dead. The Fire was high and white above us, and the sky was clear blue in every direction.

Except to the east.

To the east, low on the horizon, there was a line of black.

At first it looked like a sandstorm. Every caravan man in that column had seen a hundred sandstorms, and I saw them relax a little, the way you relax when a strange noise in the night turns out to be the wind. A storm they knew. A storm they could handle. Dagan was already bellowing orders. Circle the wagons, rope the camels down, cover the water.

But it wasn't moving like a storm.

Storms run *with* the wind. This was coming against it. Coming out of the dead, still air, rising higher and higher as it came, and it wasn't brown or yellow like sand. It was black. A deep, rolling, swallowing black, like the inside of a closed fist.

And it was getting dark.

That was the thing I'll never forget. Noon, the Fire at its height, and the light was going out of the world. Not slowly, the way it does at dusk. Quickly. As if something were drinking it.

"When the sky goes dark at noon," said Pell beside us, very softly. All the laughter had gone out of his voice. "Pray to the Fire or run."

"Run," I said. "Run now. All of you. Leave the wagons, cut the camels loose, *run*—"

Nobody listened. Nobody ever does.

---

It hit us like the end of the world.

The wind came first, a wall of it, so hard it knocked Zayn flat. Sand came with it, screaming sideways through the air, scouring skin, filling eyes. The wagons rocked. A camel broke its rope and went bolting past us into the dark, bellowing. Somebody was shouting, and then somebody else, and then it was impossible to hear anything over the roar.

Then the darkness came down over the caravan like a lid.

And inside it, something moved.

I've lived a very long time. I've seen most of the creatures of the Hollow. Husks and Hounds and wraiths, and things that crawl and things that fly and things I won't tell you about because you're too young. I've seen nothing like the Veilbeast. Nobody has, really. Nobody who's seen it all at once and remembered.

It was the storm. That's the only way I can tell you. It was the storm and it was something *inside* the storm, and you couldn't tell where one stopped and the other began. It was vast. Bigger than the caravan. Bigger than Harrow's End. Its body was the black wind itself, coiling and folding, and in the folds of it were shapes. Limbs, perhaps. Or the suggestion of limbs, huge and slow, reaching. High up, so high you had to tip your head back to see, there were lights in the dark. Red ones. Dozens of them, scattered across the black like coals blown out of a fire.

Eyes.

And beneath them, in the very heart of the storm, a hole. A great round silent place where even the darkness was darker. Where the roaring wind went in and didn't come out.

It lowered itself over the caravan.

---

I'm going to tell you what happened next, and I'm going to tell it fast, because I don't like to remember it slowly.

The front of the caravan went first. The lead wagon, the falcons on their perches, Dagan's own camel. The dark simply closed over them, and when it lifted they weren't there. No crash. No scream. There was only a smooth patch of sand where they'd been.

Then the camels behind, one after another, roped nose to tail. The dark took the rope and pulled them in like beads off a string.

The people screamed. I heard them. And then I heard the screams cut off, one by one, not in pain but in *forgetting*, as if each of them had stopped halfway through and couldn't remember why they'd started.

That's what the Veilbeast does. It doesn't eat you. It swallows you, and inside it is the Hollow, all of it at once, deep and total. What comes out the other side, if anything does, doesn't know its own face.

Zayn was on his feet. I don't know how. The wind should have kept him down. His scarf had torn loose, and the feather mark was blazing through his shirt, so bright it lit the sand around him.

And the Veilbeast's red eyes, high up in the storm, turned toward it.

All of them. At once.

"*Down*," I screamed at him. "Get *down*, it can see you, it can *see* you—"

The light in his hands came grey.

It came grey and thin and terrified, and I knew it wouldn't hold, not against that. Nothing would have held against that. Zayn stood in the middle of the screaming dark with a grey candle in his hands and the eyes of the Veilbeast fixed on him, and I watched the fear go all the way into him, deeper than it had ever gone.

Then, through the sand, I saw Pell.

His lizard was down. Dustmother lay on her side in the sand, kicking, and Pell was pinned beneath her, one leg trapped under her weight. He was screaming Zayn's name. Not his name. *Sandborn.* It was the only name he knew.

And the edge of the dark was coming toward him.

Zayn ran.

He ran straight into the wind, toward the boy who had shared dates with him, and somewhere in those ten or twelve steps, the light changed. I saw it change. Grey to gold, all at once, like a coal catching. He threw himself down over Pell and the struggling lizard, his arms spread wide, and the light came out of him in a dome. A shell of gold, a bubble of it, closing over the three of them like cupped hands.

The darkness came down on top of it.

---

I was outside the light. I want you to know that. I wasn't brave. I'd gone flat in the lee of an overturned wagon, my eyes shut, my paws over my ears, and I stayed there.

But I could see him through the sand. A small gold glow in the middle of a world gone black. The Veilbeast pressed down on it, and the gold bent, and buckled, and flickered. It held.

It held for a long time. Longer than anything should.

And then, slowly, like a tide going out, the darkness lifted.

The wind dropped. The roar faded. The black wall of the storm went rolling away west across the dunes, still against the wind, its red eyes winking out one by one into the distance. The light came back into the world. The Fire was still high. It was still noon. It had all taken less time than it takes to eat a meal.

I got up.

The caravan of Master Dagan was gone.

Forty camels, a dozen wagons, six sand lizards, the falcons, the water, and somewhere between sixty and seventy people. All gone. The road was smooth and empty and swept clean. There were no bodies. There was no blood. A few scattered bales lay half buried in the sand, and a single wagon wheel, still spinning slowly on its side.

Far off across the dunes, small grey figures were walking away. Not running. Walking, slow and aimless, in every direction, the way Husks walk. One of them had a beard dyed orange with henna. He didn't look back.

In the middle of that great swept emptiness, a small golden glow went out.

---

Zayn was lying in a drift of sand with his arms still spread. The golden cracks were everywhere: hands, arms, throat, face, running across his cheeks like the glaze on an old pot. He was shaking so hard his teeth clattered.

Of Pell, there was only a hand sticking up out of the sand.

The storm had buried him, him and the lizard both. Zayn dug. He had nothing left, and he dug anyway, with his bare cracked hands, flinging sand back between his knees like a dog, until he found a shoulder, and a face, and dragged the boy out by the collar.

Dustmother was dead beneath him. She'd died with her head turned toward her rider.

Pell was alive. Mostly.

His leg was broken. Badly. And one side of him, his left arm and shoulder and the left side of his face, had gone grey. The edge of the dark had touched him, just for a moment, before the gold closed over. His left eye had gone white. He was mumbling. Bits of words. Names of people, half finished, trailing off into nothing.

Zayn dragged himself up onto his knees. He looked at Pell for a long time.

"Don't," I said. "Look at you. You've nothing left. You do this now and it'll bring every Hound for a day's ride."

He laid his hands on Pell's face anyway.

---

It came gold this time without being asked. Just warmth, flowing out of him and into the boy, and the grey began to lift.

He saw Pell's life. A small boy at an oasis town, tossing scraps to a clutch of baby lizards the size of his hand. A grandmother laughing. A first ride, a first fall, a first bite. The long scar on his forearm, new and raw, and Pell refusing to cry about it. Then, forward: Pell older, grey at the temples, sitting by a fire with children around him, telling a story. Zayn couldn't hear what story. He could only see Pell's hands moving, describing something huge.

And then the other vision.

Longer, now. Clearer. The full Ash above him, so vast and white it seemed to be falling. The Crown Dune under his bare feet. He knew the name of it now, though nobody had ever told him. He felt the wind up there, cold and clean. He felt his arms rise. He felt the fire start in his chest and spread, and this time it didn't stop at a flash. It went on. He felt it crack him open. He felt himself coming apart into light.

Below him, someone was shouting a name.

He could almost hear it. Almost. It was right there, on the edge of the wind, and it was one he didn't know.

Then it was gone.

---

Pell opened his eyes. Both of them brown.

The grey was gone from his face and arm. His leg was straight. He took a breath, a huge shuddering one, and looked up.

At Zayn, kneeling over him, cracked all over with glowing gold like something about to shatter, light still leaking from his eyes. At the fox beside him with one silver eye. At the swept, silent, empty desert all around, where an hour before there had been a caravan and everyone he knew.

I watched him put it together. I watched him do the sum, the way frightened people always do, quick and wrong.

"It looked at you," Pell whispered. "I saw it. It came, and it looked straight at *you*."

Zayn didn't answer.

"It came for you." Pell was scrambling backward through the sand, away from him, his newly healed leg kicking. "Didn't it? You brought it. You're the thing it was looking for. Everyone's gone, Dagan's gone, they're all *gone*, and you—"

His face twisted.

"*Monster.*"

Zayn flinched. Like a slap.

"I'm not a monster," he said.

My tail glowed. It glowed bright and steady and true. But I watched it closely, all the while Pell was talking, and here's the thing I've never been able to forgive.

When Pell said *it came for you*, my tail didn't dim.

Not even a flicker.

Pell got to his feet. He stood swaying on the leg Zayn had mended, staring at him. Then he turned and ran, limping, stumbling, out across the empty sand toward the west, and he didn't look back. Not once.

Zayn knelt where he'd left him and watched him go.

---

The ground began to move under us.

Slowly at first. A trembling. Then a long, slow sliding, the sand around us beginning to sink and turn, like water going down a drain. In the middle of the swept place where the caravan had been, a hollow was opening. It was getting wider. And deeper.

Far off to the west, at the edge of the sky, the black storm had stopped moving.

Its red eyes had turned back toward us.

"*Up*," I said. "Zayn. Up. *Now*."

He tried. His legs wouldn't hold him. He'd given everything he had to the dome and the rest to Pell, and there was nothing left. He got halfway up and the sand slid out from under him and he went down on his side, and the sand was pouring now, pulling him toward the centre of that widening hollow, and he couldn't get his feet under him.

I grabbed his collar in my teeth. I pulled. I'm a small fox. I felt the silver half of me straining, aching, wanting to come out, wanting to *grow*, and I almost let it. I almost did.

The sand was at his waist. At his chest.

Then came the hoofbeats.

---

The pale camel came over the edge of the hollow at a dead run, its feet sliding in the loose sand, and the rider in grey leaned so far out of the saddle that I was sure he'd fall. A wrapped hand closed on Zayn's arm. Then a heave, a grunt, and Zayn came up out of the sand like a cork from a bottle and was thrown across the saddle face down.

"The *fox*," Zayn gasped. "Kiri—"

The rider didn't hesitate. He scooped me up by the scruff with the butt of the long spear, a trick I'd like to say I appreciated, and dropped me on top of the boy, and the camel was already turning, already running, out of the hollow and away from the storm.

Behind us, the Veilbeast was coming back.

I saw it over the rider's shoulder, rising black against the sky. And on the ridge between us and it, for just a moment, I saw something else. A grey robe, flapping. A smooth white mask.

Brother Seven was standing in our path.

He drew one grey blade as the camel thundered toward him. He swung it, as we passed, at the boy slung across the saddle. A clean, fast, perfect stroke. It went past Zayn's throat a finger's width away and cut nothing but air.

Brother Seven didn't miss. Not by accident. I've seen him fight. He stood there on the ridge and watched us go, the blade hanging at his side, and the storm came down on the place where he stood and he didn't move.

Then I lost sight of him in the sand.

---

The rider didn't stop until the Fire was going down.

By then, the storm was a smudge on the horizon behind us, and the dunes were turning red and long-shadowed, and the camel was blowing hard. The rider slid Zayn off the saddle onto firm ground at the foot of a dune and set me down beside him. He crouched over Zayn for a moment. He didn't touch him. He looked at the golden cracks on the boy's face for a long, long time.

Then he stood up and pointed with the long spear. North and west.

"Wait," Zayn croaked from the sand. "Please. Who are you? Why do you keep—"

The rider mounted. The pale camel turned. And in a few moments they were gone into the dusk, the way they'd gone the first time, without a word.

"That's twice," Zayn whispered.

"Yes," I said.

"Kiri, who *is* he?"

I didn't answer that. You know by now that I didn't.

---

We walked the way the spear had pointed. We had nothing else.

Almost nothing. When I went to sniff at the place where the rider had crouched, I found a water skin lying in the sand. Half full. He hadn't said a word about it. He'd simply left it there, the way Auntie Sabba used to leave her bundles.

Zayn made it last as long as he could. It wasn't long enough. By the middle of the next afternoon the skin hung flat and empty against his hip, and his lips had split, and every few hundred steps he stumbled. The golden lines on his skin faded slowly to a dull shimmer. Near dusk he took out the twist of waxed paper from Harrow's End, the squashed dates rolled in sand and honey, and ate one. Just one. He held it in his mouth a long time.

He didn't say anything about Pell. He didn't say anything about the caravan, or Dagan's orange beard walking away into the dunes, or my tail when Pell said *it came for you*. He'd seen it. I know he'd seen it. He didn't say a word.

The Ash came up late that night. It was thin, thinner than I'd seen it since we left Harrow's End, a pale scratch of white low over the dunes. It hardly gave any light at all.

And somewhere ahead of us, so faint that only a fox could have felt it, something was humming.

I felt it first in my teeth. Then in the pads of my feet. A deep, low, steady note rising out of the dark to the north and west, exactly where the spear had pointed, as if the desert were singing itself to sleep.

I knew that sound. I knew what it was, and what it did to people who drank from it. I knew, too, that the rider had not pointed us at it by accident.

I didn't tell Zayn. Not yet. He was walking with his head down and his hand pressed flat to the feather mark, putting one foot in front of the other, and he hadn't heard it. In a little while he would.

You'd think, after eleven bearers, I'd know what was waiting at the bottom of every dune. I don't. Not always. That night I didn't know that the desert had saved one more thing for him, out there in the dark by the singing water. Something that wasn't a monster, or a vision, or a stranger in grey who rode away without a word.

If I had known, I'd have made him walk faster.

---

*End of Chapter Seven*
