# Chapter Eight
## The Singing Wells

> *Drink from the wells, and the past will drink from you.*
> — a saying of the water-finders, older than any town that uses it

---

The Ash was thin the night we found the Singing Wells.

Thin as the smile of a trader who has just cheated you and would like you to come back tomorrow. It hung low over the western dunes, a pale scratch on the dark, and gave almost no light at all.

That suited Zayn. He'd had enough of being seen.

He had barely spoken since the beast came down out of the black sky and the caravan was simply *gone*, camels and lizards and all. Since he'd dug one man out of the sand with his bare hands and poured the Light into him until his own arms cracked gold, and the man had opened his eyes and looked at those glowing hands and said the word.

You know the word. I don't need to say it again.

The cracks from that healing were still fading on his forearms, thin lines of dull honey under the skin. Every so often he'd look down at them and then look away fast.

"You can talk to me, you know," I said. "I'm told I'm an excellent listener."

"Who told you that?"

"No one. No one has ever said it, in all of history. I was hoping you'd be the first."

The corner of his mouth moved. It wasn't a smile. It was the place where a smile would have gone, if he'd had the strength to put one there. I'll take it, I thought. I've worked with less.

His water skin had been empty since the afternoon before. His lips had split. His feet dragged. He kept his hand pressed to the feather mark, as though it were the only thing holding him upright.

Then he stopped.

"Do you hear that?" he said.

I did. I'd been hearing it for some time and hoping he wouldn't.

It was a hum. Low and steady and very large, as if the whole desert were humming to itself with its mouth closed. When Zayn stood still you could feel it coming up through the soles of your feet and into your teeth.

"Water," Zayn whispered.

"Yes," I said. "Unfortunately."

---

You'll want to know why a fox would be unhappy about water in the middle of the Destiny Desert. It's a fair question. I'll answer it.

The Singing Wells are older than Veimore, older than the glass city, older than most of the names on the map. Seven stone well-mouths sit in a ring among date palms, and the water in them hums. The water-finders will tell you it's the voice of the Weaver, or the ghosts of everyone who ever drowned in them. Water-finders will tell you anything if you buy them a drink.

What I know is this. The water is sweet and cold, and it remembers. Drink from it, and it will hand you back something you had forgotten so completely that you didn't know there was a hole where it used to be.

People think that sounds like a gift.

People have never had their own past drink them back.

"Zayn," I said. "Before you go running in there with your tongue out—"

But he'd stopped again, and this time it wasn't the hum.

From somewhere ahead, beyond the dark shapes of the palms, came a snarl. Then a clang of steel. Then a sound I had not expected to hear anywhere within three days' walk of the Hollow.

Someone was laughing.

---

We came over the last low dune and looked down into the grove.

The palms stood black against the Embers. The seven well-mouths glowed faintly between them, a soft silver light rising off the water like breath on a cold morning. And in the middle of all that quiet beauty, a young man was fighting five Hollow Hounds by himself.

I've described the Husks to you. Let me tell you about the Hounds.

They are wolves, or the memory of wolves, made of shadow the way smoke is made of fire: solid in the middle and fraying at the edges. Their eyes are embers. Not the gentle Embers in the sky; the angry red coals at the bottom of a fire that's burned too long. They make one sound only, a low, wet snarl like tearing cloth.

They hunt the Light. That's what they are for.

The young man didn't seem to care.

He was tall, a few years older than Zayn, with dark hair tied back and a jaw that had been badly cut once and healed into a pale seam. He wore a plain, dusty desert robe. But he held his sword the way I've only ever seen kings' guards hold them, high and easy, the point never wandering. Or the half of the point he had left. The sword was broken. Snapped clean a hand's width above the guard and a little more, so that what remained was barely longer than a long knife, with a jagged end like a broken tooth.

He moved like water poured from a jug.

"No, not you," he was saying, sidestepping a lunge. "You, the ugly one. Yes. You know who you are."

He spun. The broken blade went through the nearest Hound's throat. It didn't bleed. It just burst, the way a dropped sack of flour bursts, into a cloud of fine black ash that drifted down onto the sand.

"Four," said the young man cheerfully. "Who's next? Don't all volunteer at once."

---

Zayn had dropped flat on his belly behind the crest of the dune. His hand was on his knife.

"Not our business," I said.

"He's alone."

"He seems to be enjoying it."

That was true. My tail glowed with it. But the young man was also bleeding from a long scratch on his left shoulder, and he was breathing harder than his voice let on, and one of the four remaining Hounds had begun, very quietly, to circle around behind him while the others kept him busy.

"They're not here for him, you know," I said. I said it before I could stop myself. "Hounds hunt the Light. They smelled you coming a day ago. They were waiting at the water for you. He just got in the way."

Zayn turned and looked at me.

I wish I hadn't said it. I've wished that about a great many things I've said. But it was true, and he had a right to know it, and my tail was glowing so bright it lit the sand between us.

He got up.

---

Here's the thing you need to understand about Zayn and the Light by this point in the story. He didn't trust it.

It had saved Bram. It had pulled Bram's little brother out of the grey. It had saved a man from the sand after the beast. And every time, the people it saved had looked at him afterwards with the same face, and that face had a word in it, and the word had driven him out of his village with stones at his back.

So when he ran down that dune toward the Hounds, the first thing he felt wasn't courage. It was a cold, sick little voice in his head saying: *He'll see. He'll see what you are, and then he'll look at you like they all do.*

The light came, and it came grey.

It flickered out of his fists, thin and dim as dirty water, and the Hound that was circling behind the young man stopped. It turned its ember eyes toward Zayn. It lowered its head and grinned the way only shadows can grin.

Fear does that. I've told you. It turns the Light into something the dark can chew.

The Hound sprang at him. Zayn threw himself sideways into the sand and felt its teeth snap shut on the air beside his ear. He rolled. He came up on his knees. And over the Hound's smoking back he saw the young man stagger as a second Hound caught his wounded shoulder and dragged him half off his feet.

The young man didn't cry out. He just grunted, and set his teeth, and swung the broken sword one-handed. He kept fighting. He kept standing up.

And Zayn thought, with no words at all, *Not him too.*

The light went gold.

---

It didn't blaze the way it had in Harrow's End. He was too worn through for that. But it came up out of his chest warm and steady, like the Fire climbing over a dune, and it ran down his arm and into the rusted blade of his scavenged knife until the metal shone like it was made of honey.

The Hound in front of him flinched.

Zayn didn't give it time to recover. He lunged and slashed, and for the first time in his life his knife went *into* a creature of the Hollow instead of sliding off. The Hound burst into ash. Zayn ran through the falling cloud of it without slowing down, and slammed his shoulder into the Hound that had the young man's arm, and the light went through it like lamp-oil going up.

Then there was a back against his back.

A warm, solid weight, someone else's shoulder blades pressed against his own. The last two Hounds paced in a slow circle around them, snarling their tearing-cloth snarl, and the young man shifted his feet so the two of them could turn together, like the hands of a water clock.

The young man glanced back over his shoulder. He looked at Zayn's face, and Zayn's arms, and the gold pouring out of Zayn's skin and lighting up the palms, the wells and the falling ash.

"You glow," he said.

A pause, while a Hound feinted and drew back.

"That's weird."

"I know," said Zayn.

"I didn't say it was *bad*." The young man turned forward again. "Duck."

Zayn ducked. The broken sword whistled through the air where his head had been and took the leaping Hound clean across the chest. Ash rained down on both of them. The last Hound looked at the young man, then at the boy made of light, and seemed to do some quick arithmetic. It turned to run.

Zayn's light reached it first.

---

The grove went quiet. Only the wells were still humming, low and patient, the way they'd been humming for a thousand years.

The gold drained back into Zayn's chest. It left him swaying on his feet with new cracks running up both forearms, brighter than the old ones, and that was when it came: the thing that always came.

The full Ash, white and enormous.

The Crown Dune, so high its top was lost in the sky.

Fire, rising up through him. His own arms, opening.

And this time, something new. A sound, far below him, at the foot of the dune. Someone shouting. Someone shouting a *name*, over and over, in a voice cracked down the middle, and he couldn't hear what the name was. He leaned toward it with everything in him, and he couldn't hear it.

"Hey. *Hey.*"

A hand on his shoulder. Zayn blinked, and the desert came back: palms, wells, Embers, the thin scratch of Ash in the west. He was sitting in the sand. He didn't remember sitting down. The young man was crouched in front of him, his broken sword laid across his knees, looking at him with frank, dark-eyed interest.

"You went somewhere," the young man said. "Your eyes did a thing."

Zayn looked up at the sky, at the Ash. It was thin. It was so very thin. He let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

I didn't tell him how fast an Ash fills up. It seemed unkind.

"Does it hurt?" the young man asked.

Zayn stared at him.

He nodded at the golden cracks on Zayn's arms. As if it were a perfectly normal question to ask someone who had just turned into a lamp in front of you.

In fifteen years of being Zayn, no one had ever asked him that.

"Yes," said Zayn.

My tail glowed. The young man glanced at it, then back at Zayn, and nodded slowly.

"Thought so," he said. "Your face looked like mine does when I've been punched." He stood up and held out a hand, and when Zayn took it he pulled him to his feet in one easy motion. "Right. Excuse me a moment. I need to collect my wages."

---

He took a clay jar from a pack lying by the nearest palm, knelt in the sand, and began to scoop up Hound-ash with a flat wooden spoon.

Zayn watched him do it for a long time.

"What," he said finally, "are you doing?"

"Making a living." The young man didn't look up. "Glassblowers in the free towns pay a water token a pinch for this. It makes black glass that won't break." He tapped the jar. "A good Hound's worth a week of water. You just doubled my income, glowing stranger. I'd thank you, but I'm going to have to give you half, and I'm still sad about it."

"You can keep it."

"Absolutely not. I have principles." He considered that. "I have *some* principles. One. It's that one."

He sealed the jar, wiped his spoon on his robe, and sat back on his heels. In the silver light from the wells, I saw the ring on his right hand for the first time. It was heavy and gold, too heavy and too gold for a dusty young man who scraped up monster-ash for a living. It was carved with a crest I had seen before, on banners and on great red city gates.

I kept that to myself. I keep a lot of things to myself. You may have noticed.

"Soren," said the young man, and pointed at his own chest.

He said it out loud. Right into the open night, with the wind moving in the palm fronds over his head.

Zayn flinched as if he'd dropped a lamp. "You shouldn't— The wind—"

"The wind and I have an understanding," said Soren. "It's never done me a single favour, and I've never asked it for one." He raised his eyebrows. "You've got one too, I assume. A name. Or do I keep calling you glowing stranger? I don't mind. It's got a ring to it."

Zayn's hand went to his chest. He pressed the mark under his shirt. He looked at the wind moving in the palms.

Then he said, so quietly that I barely heard it, "Zayn."

"Zayn," Soren repeated, at a perfectly normal volume. "Good. Short. Easy to shout when you're about to be eaten." He looked down. "And who's this?"

That was me. I had trotted down from the dune and was sitting by the nearest well, washing ash off my front paw with what I felt was considerable dignity.

"Kiri," I said. "And before you ask, yes, I talk. No, it isn't a trick. No, I won't do it on command for your friends. Yes, I've heard every joke."

Soren looked at me. He looked at Zayn. He looked back at me.

"Your fox talks," he said to Zayn.

"He's not my fox," said Zayn.

"I'm not his fox," I said, at exactly the same time.

Soren took this in. Then he shrugged. "Fine," he said. "I've had a strange year."

He didn't scream. He didn't make the sign against evil, which is what a man two hundred years ago did right before he fell into his own well. He just went back to tying up his shoulder with a strip of cloth, one end held in his teeth.

I decided I liked him. I didn't say so. I have standards.

---

Zayn's knees chose that moment to remember that he hadn't had any water for a day and a half. He sat down rather suddenly beside the nearest well.

The water inside was black and still, and the hum was so loud this close that the stone lip shivered under his hand. His throat clenched.

"Careful," said Soren. He'd stopped tying his bandage. "You know what these are?"

"The Singing Wells," said Zayn. "Kiri says they remember."

"Kiri's right." Soren came and crouched beside him. For the first time his voice wasn't joking. "First drink is the worst. It'll hand you something back. Something you didn't know you'd lost." He looked down into the black water. "I drank once. My first night out here. I saw my mother's face. She died when I was three. I didn't know I remembered her at all." He was quiet a moment. "Now I strain it through cloth and boil it, and it still hums at me a bit. But it doesn't sing."

Zayn looked at the water.

I knew what he was thinking. A boy found alone in the sand had nothing back there to remember: no face, no voice, nothing but a fox sitting a little way off, watching. Whatever the well handed him, it couldn't be worse than the emptiness he already had.

"Zayn," I said.

He looked at me.

I could have stopped him. One word would have done it.

I didn't say it. I've thought about why, many times, and I'll let you decide whether it was kindness.

He cupped his hands, dipped them into the Singing Well, and drank.

---

It was cold enough to ache in his teeth, and sweeter than any water he'd ever bought with a clay token. He swallowed, and the humming came inside him, filling his chest and his skull until it wasn't humming anymore.

It was singing.

And the desert went away.

He was small. Smaller than he had ever been, wrapped tight in something soft, warm all the way through for the first time in his life. It was dark, and he wasn't afraid of the dark. He couldn't see faces, only the orange flicker of a fire somewhere close.

But he could feel a hand.

It was a child's hand. Small, a little sticky, very warm, and it had hold of his entire fist. It wasn't letting go. It squeezed, gently and then tighter, the way you'd squeeze something to make sure it was real, the way he himself pressed the feather mark on his chest when he was afraid. Someone was holding on to him. Someone small had decided he was worth holding on to.

And above him, somewhere in the warm dark, a man was singing.

His voice was low and rough and a little out of tune. It was a song about fire, and about wings, and about something that falls into the ashes and rises up out of them again. Zayn didn't know the words. He couldn't hold on to them. They slid away like water through his fingers. But the tune went into him and stayed. It went into him like a hook.

Because he knew it.

He'd heard it before. Not long ago. He'd heard it hummed, out in the dunes, behind a smooth white mask, by a man with twin grey blades who had cornered him in the dark and then, for no reason at all, let him go.

---

Zayn came up out of the water gasping.

He was on his knees by the well, his face wet, and not all of it was well-water. He pressed both hands to his chest as if he could keep something in there.

Soren didn't say anything.

That was the second thing he did that night that no one had ever done for Zayn. He didn't ask questions. He didn't laugh, or stare, or tell him to stop. He just sat down in the sand beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the palms, and waited.

After a long time he said, "Good one or bad one?"

Zayn wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"I don't know," he said.

My tail glowed. It was the truest thing he could have said.

Later, when Soren had built a fire between two of the wells and was boiling water in a battered tin pot, Zayn came and sat by me. He didn't look at me. He kept his eyes on the flames.

"Kiri," he said softly. "That song. Do you know it?"

The hum of the wells filled up the silence.

"Lots of people sing about fire," I said at last. "It's the desert. There isn't much else to sing about."

My tail glowed, because every word of that was true.

That's the trouble with the truth. You can hide a whole lie in the spaces between it, and no tail in the world will ever give you away.

---

Soren shared his flatbread, hard as roof tiles, and the boiled, strained water from the tin pot. It only hummed a little.

"You can stay, if you like," Soren said, poking the fire with the end of his broken sword. He said it lightly, as though it didn't matter. It mattered a great deal. I could hear it in the spaces between the words. "Fire's big enough for two." He glanced over at me. "Three."

"I eat scorpions," I said. "I won't be touching your bread."

"Then you're cheap company. Even better."

Zayn lay down on the far side of the fire with his patched cloak pulled over him. I watched him do something I had never once seen him do in fifteen years. He fell asleep with his knife lying in the sand beside him, instead of held in his hand.

Soren sat up a while longer, turning the heavy gold ring on his finger, looking west at nothing. Then he lay down too.

I stayed awake. Someone has to.

The wells hummed. The Embers turned. And low over the western dunes, the thin Ash hung like a white thread, like the first stitch in something that would take exactly one turn of the sky to finish.

I didn't count the nights that first night. I should have. If I'd started counting from the beginning, I'd have known exactly how many they had, and I could have told you every one.

I started the next night instead.

I've never been able to stop.

---

*End of Chapter Eight*
