# Chapter Fourteen
## The Ringing of the Bell

> *The bell does not lie, but it can be too late.*
> — a saying of the healers at the desert's edge

---

The town was called Thornwell, and it was the kind of place you could love if nobody in it was trying to kill you.

It sat in a shallow bowl between three dunes, around a spring so old that the stones of its well had been worn into the shape of hands. Nine date palms stood over it. Somebody, long ago, had decided nine was lucky, so the people of Thornwell never cut one down and never planted a tenth. There was a market of striped awnings, a dyer's yard hung with red and saffron cloth, and a hundred flat-roofed mud-brick houses. A free town, like most oasis towns in the desert. No king. No walls. Just water, and people who'd decided to stay near it.

Orrin's house stood on the edge of the market. It had blue shutters and a courtyard with a fig tree and a fountain that actually worked, which in the desert is about as close to showing off as you can get.

"Animals sleep in the yard," said the steward at the gate, looking down at me.

"I'm not an animal," I said. "I'm a guest with fur."

The steward went pale, the way people always do the first time, and looked at Orrin.

Orrin smiled his soft smile. "The fox may go where he likes," he said. "Outside."

So I slept on the roof.

---

They gave Zayn the top room. It had a real bed with a real blanket, a basin of clean water, and a window that looked out over the nine palms. He stood in the doorway looking at it the way you'd look at a meal you suspected was a trap.

Then he took off the broken sword and laid it on the bed. He put his bag beside it. And for the first time since the full Ash, he slept through a whole night without the sword in his arms.

I watched him through the window from the roof's edge, lying in the cool dark with my burned side pressed against the warm bricks.

I didn't sleep.

---

You have to understand how good Orrin was at it.

I've known a great many liars, and most of them are bad at it. They talk too much. They hurry. Orrin never hurried.

On the first evening, there were dates on the table. Dates rolled in honey, on a blue clay plate.

Zayn stopped in the doorway and stared at them.

"Soren told me you liked them," said Orrin gently. "Sit. Eat."

The bell was upstairs, in the bag on the bed. I was on the roof, with my ear pressed to a crack in the shutter, and my tail went dim as a dying coal. Nobody saw.

Zayn sat. He ate one date, slowly. Then another. He didn't touch the little twist of waxed paper in his pocket, the one he'd been carrying all the way from Harrow's End. He ate Orrin's dates instead, and his eyes filled with tears, and Orrin pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in days.

That's how it's done, you see. Not with chains. With dates, and a real bed, and an old man who knows exactly when to look away.

---

Orrin wrote letters in the evenings.

He sat at a low table by the lamp with paper and ink and a little stone seal he wore on a chain around his neck. When each letter was done he folded it, dripped grey wax onto the fold, and pressed the seal into it. A falcon waited on a perch in the corner, and every night it flew out of the window with a letter tied to its leg.

On the second evening, Zayn sat across from him and watched the pen move.

"*Water,*" he said quietly. "That word. Upside down. It says *water*."

The pen stopped.

Orrin looked up. For a moment, only a moment, something moved behind those faded grey eyes, quick and cold as a fish in a dark pool.

"You can read," he said.

"A little," said Zayn. "Soren taught me."

"Did he." Orrin set the pen down. "He was a patient teacher?"

Zayn almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen anything like it since the full Ash. "No. He was terrible. He laughed every time I wrote an R. He said they looked like camels falling over."

"Then you must be a very good student," said Orrin, and went back to his letter, and folded it, and sealed it.

I remember the seal. The grey wax, and the way he pressed it down with his thumb, and the little curling mark it left behind. I didn't know why I was remembering it. Some part of me must have known. The part that always knows things a little too late.

---

On the third evening, he talked about his son.

The lamp had burned low, and the way conversations wander after dark, they had come round to families.

"I had a son once," said Orrin.

He said it to the lamp, not to Zayn. His voice was very quiet.

"And a brother. A long time ago, before I came to the palace. There was a sickness that year, in the town where I was born. It came through like a wind. My brother first. Then my boy." He turned his cup slowly in his long fingers. "He was six. He used to sit on my knee while I wrote, and try to catch the pen."

My tail glowed gold on the roof. Every word of it was true.

The truly dangerous liars don't lie very often. They tell you the truth, and the truth is sad and real, and it opens you up like a key in a lock. Then, once the door is open, they walk in.

"I'm sorry," said Zayn.

"So am I." Orrin smiled at him, tired and gentle. "I've spent a long time trying to forget them. It doesn't work, you know. Not the way you'd hope."

Zayn was quiet for a while. Then he said, "Tell me about my father."

Orrin set the cup down.

"He carried what you carry," he said. "The Light. Before you. The old murals have a name for him. You've seen them, I think. In the glass city."

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

"*The Runner*," he whispered.

"The Runner," said Orrin. "He was afraid of what the Light would ask of him. So he ran." He paused. "He's alive, Zayn."

Zayn's breath went out of him as if he'd been struck.

"Where?" His voice cracked. "Where is he?"

"When you're stronger," said Orrin, and reached across the table and laid a hand over Zayn's, very lightly. "I promise. When you're stronger, I'll take you to him."

It's a strange thing to sit on a roof and watch a boy's heart get caught on a hook. He leaned toward that old man the way a plant leans toward a window.

He trusted him.

For the first time since Soren, Zayn trusted someone.

---

That night he climbed up to the roof.

He didn't say anything at first. He just sat down beside me with his knees pulled up, looking out over the nine palms and the dark dunes beyond them. The Ash was thinner now. Down to a half, and still shrinking.

"He's alive," Zayn said at last. "My father."

"I heard."

"He said he'd take me to him."

"I heard that too."

He turned his head and looked at me. "You don't believe him."

My tail stayed exactly as it was. I didn't say anything.

"Kiri."

"Wear the bell," I said.

He frowned. "What?"

"Hesset's bell. The one in your bag. Take it out and wear it." I looked at the palms. "Humour an old fox."

"Why?"

I didn't answer.

"Why?" he said again, sharper. "If you know something, *tell* me. If he's lying, tell me what he's lying about."

There it was. The door, wide open, and all I had to do was walk through it.

*Don't trust him.* Three words. That's all it would have taken. And he would have asked, *Why not?* And I would have had to tell him where I'd been on the night of the full Ash, and what I'd been, and everything that came after. I wasn't ready to say that out loud. Not to him. Not even to you, yet. And I had no proof. I had nothing but my own word, and my own word had gone dark at the City of Glass.

I'm not going to make excuses. I've told you I won't. I'll only tell you what I did.

I said nothing.

Zayn looked at me for a long time. Then he made a small, tired sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Where were you?" he said. "That night."

I said nothing.

"That's what I thought," he said, and went back down the stairs.

---

In the morning, he wore the bell.

I think he did it out of spite, to prove me wrong, the way you'd taste a dish someone said was poisoned just to show them. Or maybe he did it for Hesset. He'd been angry at the old man ever since the murals in the City of Glass told him what Hesset hadn't. Maybe, in that soft blue house, he'd started to forgive him a little. You can't always tell why people do things. Sometimes they can't either.

But he tied it to his belt, on a short thread, under the fold of his scarf where it wouldn't show. A small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

It didn't ring all morning. Nobody lied to him all morning. Orrin was in his room, writing.

I went down into the market to find something to eat.

---

That's where I saw Bram.

I almost didn't recognise him. The boy I'd last seen in Harrow's End had been broad and loud and well-fed, almost a prince. This boy was thin as a stick. His clothes were rags. His feet were bound in strips of cloth, black with dirt, and his lips were split from thirst. He was standing at the old well with his hands cupped, begging for water, and nobody was giving him any.

There was a grey streak across one side of his face. Not the old one, from the Husk in the lane. A new one, running from his temple down into his hair, as if someone had dragged a dirty thumb across him.

I went very still in the shadow of an awning.

He didn't see me. He was watching the house with the blue shutters. He watched it the way a hungry dog watches a butcher's door. And after a while, when the market got busy around noon, he turned and slipped off down a narrow alley between the dyer's yard and the wall.

I followed him.

The alley turned, and turned again, and ended in a little dead square full of broken pots, where the red and saffron cloths from the dyer's yard hung over the wall like curtains.

Someone was waiting there.

Grey robe. Smooth white mask, with no face on it at all.

I flattened myself against the wall behind a cracked water jar and stopped breathing.

---

"Well?" said the mask. Its voice was flat and dry, like sand poured from a cup.

"He's here," said Bram. His voice shook. "You were right. He's in the house with the blue shutters. The top room, the one that looks over the palms. He sleeps by the window. He's got a sword, a broken one. He keeps it on the bed."

"You're certain it's him."

"I'd know him anywhere." Bram swallowed. "I've known him all my life."

"Good," said the mask. "Tonight, then."

"And Tobin?" Bram took a step forward. "You said. You *promised*. When you found us in the desert, you said you'd keep him safe from the grey. You said if I found the glowing boy for you, you'd give my brother back."

"When we have the bearer," said the mask, "the child goes free."

My tail went dark.

It went completely dark, like a lamp with the oil run out. I wanted to scream at Bram, *Look at me! Can't you see?* But he wasn't looking at me. He was looking at that blank white face as if it were the only door left in the world.

"Everyone's gone grey," he said. His voice cracked in half. "Do you understand? All of them. My father sits by the well all day and he doesn't know my name. Oszkar. Garrem. Everyone. I took Tobin and I ran, and we walked for days, and then you came, and—" He stopped. He pressed his hands over his face. "He's seven. He's only seven."

"Tonight," said the mask, and turned away.

Bram stood alone in the dead square for a long time, his hands over his face, his shoulders shaking. Then he went back up the alley without looking round.

---

I've thought about that conversation a great deal.

At first, it made no sense to me. Why would the Unnamed need a starving boy to tell them where Zayn slept? Their master was the one who'd *put* him there. Orrin had chosen the room. Orrin had chosen the bed.

I understood it later. A man like Orrin never lets his own hands be seen. If anything went wrong, if the bearer escaped, if he lived long enough to wonder how the masks had found him, it would be Bram's face he remembered. The boy from his village. The boy who'd thrown the first stone. Not the kind old man with the dates and the falcon, who'd tried so hard to keep him safe.

It was a clever plan. It would have worked, too.

Except for a small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

---

I ran.

I ran faster than I'd moved since the storm, my burned side tearing and smoking, across the market and under a camel and through a basket of figs.

The gate of the blue-shuttered house was shut.

I found a spot where the bricks were rough, and climbed. I'm not built for climbing. I'm built for running and digging and being rude. It took far too long. By the time I dragged myself over the top of the wall and dropped into the courtyard, the Fire was going down red behind the palms.

The giant was standing by the fountain.

He turned his head and looked at me. That was all. He simply stood between me and the door, and when I tried to go round him, he was there. And when I tried the other way, he was there too.

I could have changed. I've told you that before, and I'll tell you again before this story's done. I could have let the dark half out, and maybe I'd have got past him, and maybe I wouldn't.

I was still standing there, shaking, deciding, when I heard the sound upstairs.

---

They were sitting at supper. That was the strange thing. Zayn told me later. It was such an ordinary evening.

There was bread and lentils and a little bowl of dates. Orrin had been telling him why nobody in Thornwell planted a tenth palm, and for one evening Zayn had almost forgotten to be sad.

Then there were footsteps on the stairs. Many footsteps, soft and quick.

The door opened.

Grey robes. White masks. Five of them, then eight, filling the doorway and spilling into the room, and in their hands were blades. Long and thin, the colour of old ash, and they seemed to drink the lamplight instead of reflecting it.

Zayn was on his feet. The table went over. His hand went to his belt for the knife, and the knife wasn't there; it was upstairs, in his bag, with the broken sword, on the bed by the window, exactly where Bram had said it would be.

And Orrin rose.

He rose smoothly from his cushion, the old man in his blue robes, and stepped between Zayn and the masks. He put one arm out behind him, shielding the boy, the way a father shields a child.

"Stay behind me, child," he said. His voice was low and calm and full of love. "You're safe. No one will harm you while I live."

And the bell rang.

---

It wasn't loud. That's what I always remember. I heard it from the courtyard, through the shutters, a single clear note, high and sweet as a drop of water falling into a well. A small sound. A tiny sound.

It filled the whole room.

Zayn went completely still.

He looked down at his belt. At the bell, swinging on its thread under the fold of his scarf, still shivering with the note. Then he looked up at Orrin's back. At the silver hair. At the outstretched arm.

Orrin turned his head. The soft face didn't change at all. But the eyes did. Those faded grey eyes moved down to the bell and rested there, and something in them went flat and cold and very, very tired.

"Ah," he said quietly. "Hesset."

Zayn's lips were white. "The ring," he whispered. "Did he give you the ring?"

"Of course he did," said Orrin.

The bell rang again.

And then, like a lamp being lit in a dark room, all of it came at once. Zayn told me he could feel the pieces falling into place, one after another, each one a little stone hitting him in the back. The bag on the camel. The dates on the plate. *Soren told me you liked them.* The ring that had not been on Soren's hand.

*He gave me this.*

"No," said Zayn. It came out of him like something tearing. "*No.*"

Orrin sighed. He lowered his arm.

"I should have searched you myself," he said, and stepped aside.

---

The masks came in.

Zayn fought. I want you to know that. He had no knife and no sword and no friend, and he fought anyway: with his fists, with the overturned table, with a lamp he swung in a burning arc that sent two of the masks reeling back.

And the light came.

But it came *grey*.

Thin and dim and sputtering, the colour of the Hollow, the colour of fear and fury. It crawled out of his fists like smoke and did nothing at all. A mask stepped in close and laid a grey blade flat against his forearm, almost gently.

Zayn screamed.

It didn't cut him. There was no blood. But where the blade touched his skin, the grey light went *into* it, sucked out of him like water into dry sand, and he felt something leave him. A memory. A small one. He'd tell me later he never knew which. Only that it had been warm, and now there was a cold place where it used to be.

That was when I came through the shutters.

I don't remember how I got past the giant. Then I was in the room in a spray of broken wood, and I went for the nearest mask with every tooth I had.

I got one ankle. That's all. Then a hand like the side of a house closed around my middle, right over the burn, and the giant lifted me into the air, and I made a sound I'm not proud of, and he threw me.

I hit the wall. The world went white and then grey and then very far away.

---

The last thing Zayn saw, before the sack came down over his head, was the doorway.

There was someone standing in it. Behind the masks, in the shadow at the top of the stairs. A thin boy in rags, with his feet wrapped in cloth and a grey streak across his face.

Bram.

They looked at each other across the wrecked room. The boy who'd thrown the first stone, and the boy it had hit.

"They've got Tobin," Bram said. His voice was barely there at all. "I'm sorry. Zayn, I'm sorry."

He had never once, in fifteen years, called him by his name. I don't think he'd even known it, until the masks told him.

The bell on Zayn's belt hung silent. Not a whisper. Every word was true.

Then the sack came down, and the grey blades came with it, and the room went dark.

---

I woke alone on the floor of a room that smelled of lamp oil and spilled lentils.

The masks were gone. The old man was gone. The broken sword was gone from the bed upstairs, and the bag with it. Out in the courtyard, the fountain was still running, as if nothing had happened at all.

And on the floor, near the overturned table, there was a scrap of thread. Snapped. A little bronze bell lay beside it on the tiles, on its side, silent.

I picked it up in my teeth.

I didn't know then where they'd taken him. I didn't know whether there'd be anything left of him when I found him. I only knew that the bell had rung, and that it had rung too late, and that I had been standing in a courtyard when it did, with the truth in my mouth, saying nothing.

Bram told me, many years later, that he never forgave himself for that night.

Neither did I.

---

*End of Chapter Fourteen*
