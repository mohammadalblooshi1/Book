# Chapter Twenty
## Ash into Gold

> *Ash into gold and gold into flame —*
> *The end is the start, and the start is the same.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born; the last verse, which is also the first

---

The gold fell all night.

It came down soft and slow, like the first warm rain after a long drought, and everywhere it touched, the grey let go.

I watched from the top of the Crown Dune. I couldn't have moved if I'd wanted to. Down at the foot of the dune, the great ring of Husks stood with their faces turned up into the falling light, and one by one, the white went out of their eyes. Brown came back. Black. Green. An old herder looked down at his own hands and began to cry. A woman spun round and round in the sand, calling a child's name, and somewhere in the crowd a child's voice called back.

Not all of them. I promised you no more lies, so I'll tell you. Some of them woke screaming, because the gold gave them back everything, including what their hands had done while they were empty. And some didn't wake at all. The gold touched them, and they lay down in the sand, still smiling, and didn't get up.

Then there was *sound*. Thousands of people talking and weeping and shouting names. The whole desert was saying them.

Far off, over the walls of Veimore, a light came on. Then another. Someone was lighting the lanterns again. And on the shrine walls, I'm told, the names of the dead came back in gold, and stayed.

The grey went out of the desert like a tide going out.

That's what everyone says. But I have one silver eye that sees what the Hollow sees, and I'll tell you what I watched. It didn't go *away*. It went *down*. It drained into the dunes the way water drains into a dry riverbed, sinking between the grains, until there was nothing on the surface but gold.

The tide goes out, little one. It doesn't stop being the sea.

---

The gold fell on Malek too.

The small grey shepherd boy was standing alone at the edge of the crest, and he didn't run. He stood still and let it land on him, the way you'd let snow land on you if you had never seen snow.

And the grey came off him like dust.

Under it was a boy. Brown and thin and barefoot, with a crook of ordinary wood and a scab on one knee and sheep's wool caught in his sleeve. His eyes weren't black anymore. They were brown, warm brown, the eyes I'd looked into at a thousand fires before the world was old.

"Kiri?" he said, in a small voice.

I couldn't speak. It was the second time in one night.

He looked at his own hands, then back at me, and his eyes filled.

"I remember my name," he said.

My tail lit up so bright it hurt. It was true. He remembered his name.

"I know," I said, when I could. "I never forgot it. Not once."

He smiled. And I have spent every night since trying to decide what kind of smile it was. A boy's smile, healed and sorry and tired. Or the smile of someone who has finally put down a very heavy thing, and doesn't care where it lands, or who picks it up next.

He lifted his face to the last of the falling gold and came apart into light. It rose off the Crown Dune and went away east toward the coming Fire.

But his shadow didn't go with him.

It lay on the sand where he'd stood, grey and boy-shaped, for one breath after the rest of him was gone. Then it sank. Into the dune. Down, with the rest of the grey, the way a stone sinks into still water, and the sand closed over it without a ripple.

My tail did nothing at all.

I've hated Malek for more years than I can count. I'll miss him for longer. And I still don't know whether I watched him healed that night, or only watched him let go.

---

Brother Seven was kneeling at the very end of the crest, where the boy had stood.

The sand there had turned gold. Not gold-coloured. *Gold.* You know the old stories: sand that holds a bearer's ash turns gold and never moves again. It lay in a small, perfect circle on the top of the highest dune in the world. The wind went across it and didn't move a single grain. In the middle of it lay Hesset's golden feather, the one found with the baby in the sand. It was whole. Every barb.

Tamir had put down his blades. As I watched, his white mask split along its crack and fell away in two halves. It took skin with it. Fifteen years it had been on. There were raw red lines down his cheeks where its edges had grown into him.

Under it was a young man's face. Twenty, pale-eyed, thin, with his father's jaw and his brother's watchful look.

He opened his mouth. He hadn't spoken a word of his own in fifteen years.

"Aeron," said Tamir.

Daruun turned round.

He crossed the crest faster than I've ever seen a man move and caught his elder son in his arms. For a moment, a single moment, it was what it should have been.

Then Tamir began to remember.

Not the tent and the song and the baby. That came first, and it was sweet, and it lasted perhaps three breaths. Then the rest came. Fifteen years of it, all at once, the way the gold had given it back to the Husks. Every child he had carried into the hold of the Buried Ship. Every name he had watched burn. The exact moment each child had stopped crying. The things the grey blades had done in his hands, which he had not been able to feel at the time and could feel now, every one, all together.

He screamed.

He tore himself out of his father's arms and clawed at his own face, at the raw lines where the mask had been, as though he could put it back. Daruun caught his wrists. Tamir fought him, and screamed, and then stopped screaming and simply shook, and Daruun held on, the way he had held on to Zayn's burning hands. He didn't let go. He'd learned something about letting go, and something about not.

And after a long time, in the middle of it, through his teeth, Tamir began to hum. The phoenix song. Over and over. As if it were the only rope left and he was hanging off the edge of the world by it.

Idan stood beside them with Soren's ring clutched in his fist, looking out across the desert.

"Kiri," he said. "Look."

The Fire was coming up.

And below us, across the whole Destiny Desert, the dunes had turned gold in great sweeping lines, miles long. They weren't moving anymore. They never would again. Five letters, written from one edge of the sky to the other, so large you could have read them from the walls of Veimore, so large you can read them still.

*AERON.*

---

Old Hesset was lying on his back in the sand.

He'd been there all night. I'd forgotten him. Everyone had. That shames me now. I limped over, and he turned his face toward the sound of me. The dust was running out of the cracks in his hands like sand from a split sack.

"Fox," he said. His voice was barely there. "Is it morning?"

"Nearly."

"Did he forgive me? The boy?"

"He held up your bell," I said. "In front of everyone. He kept it the whole way."

He smiled, the smile I've never been able to describe, fond and sad at once, the way a man smiles at an old letter.

"The sand remembers," he whispered.

Then the Fire came up over the edge of the world, and its first light fell across his milky eyes.

And the milk cleared out of them.

They went clear and dark and wet, the eyes of a young man, and they *saw*. He looked up at the sky going pink and gold. He looked out at the great golden letters lying across the dunes. The last thing he had seen, three hundred years before, was the top of this dune in the dark. The first thing he saw now was the dawn.

"Oh," said Hesset. "Oh, *look* at it."

Then his eyes moved. Past the letters. Past the dunes. Out to the far rim of the desert, where the sand meets the sky, out where no one has ever come back from.

His face changed.

I have tried, every night since, to decide what was in it. Wonder. Terror. Both. The face of a man who has been in the dark three hundred years and, in the first light, sees the thing that has been standing beside him the whole time.

"Kiri," he whispered. "Kiri, at the edge. There's something— it's getting *up*. Fox, it's so big, it's been lying there all along, and it's—"

That was all.

His eyes stayed open, fixed on the edge of the world. His debt was paid, and he was gone. The truth bells at his belt were silent.

Then one of them rang. Once. Very softly. Though nobody had said a word.

I looked where he'd been looking, with both my eyes. I saw the far dunes, and the heat beginning to shimmer on them, and nothing else.

That isn't the same as there being nothing else.

We gave him to the dunes on the slope of the Crown Dune, facing east, so he'd always have the dawn. I closed his eyes. They stayed shut. I was glad of that. I didn't want them looking.

---

They went down at noon. Daruun, and Tamir, and Idan between them, back toward Veimore, where every lantern was burning again and the people were painting a new name on the shrine wall of the palace court. Idan would be king. He was ten.

I stayed with the gold. I sat beside that small glittering circle through the long afternoon and into the night, and I didn't eat, and I didn't sleep. There was nothing to guard. The gold would never move. Nothing in the desert would ever touch it.

I guarded it anyway.

Some time after dark, I put my ear to the sand beside the circle.

Something was breathing down there.

Very deep. Very slow. Not the way the catacombs of Veimore breathed, in and out. It went in, and then it didn't come out. It held. Like breath held under water, by something that can hold it a very long time, and is patient, and is listening to the footsteps going by overhead.

The Hollow wasn't gone. It was sleeping. And it had pulled the gold over itself like a blanket.

When the Ash came up that night, just past full, I stood on the top of the highest dune in the world and shouted at the sky.

"*Not this one!*"

My voice went out across the whole desert. I didn't care who heard.

"You hear me? Not this one! *Not again!* I have walked beside twelve of them. I have watched nine of them burn on this sand. And they're children, every time, *children*, you only ever want the *children*— He never had anything. Nobody ever gave him a single thing he didn't have to scavenge for, and he gave you everything, and you *took* it, and it isn't even *finished*, is it? It's never finished. You'll want another—"

The Weaver didn't answer. It never does. It only waits.

The wind came up from the east, gentle, and went over the golden circle without stirring a grain. Then it went over me and smoothed down my fur, the way you'd smooth a frightened child's hair.

Or the way you'd smooth the fur of an animal you mean to keep.

I lay down beside the gold with my nose on my paws. And I cried, little one. Foxes do.

---

There's something I have to tell you now.

I've been putting it off the whole story. You'll have noticed. You're a great deal cleverer than you look, all pink and wrinkled like that.

I saw it happen.

The night of the full Ash, one turn before the end, when they argued and Zayn walked away into the dark. I wasn't by the fire. I was out in the dunes in my other shape, hunting the Hounds that were tracking his light. I didn't want him to see me like that. Not after the storm, when his light burned me. Not after the way he'd looked at me.

So I was out in the dark being a monster, and that's why I was too far away.

I heard voices from the camp. Soren's. And another one, soft, that I didn't know yet.

By the time I came over the ridge, it was almost done.

Orrin was kneeling by the fire. He'd come alone, in plain robes, no mask. Soren had known him his whole life: the man who'd sat behind his father's throne every day he'd been alive. So when Orrin held out a flask and said *you must be thirsty, the boy has left you, come home*, Soren drank.

There was ash in that water. Bearer's ash, stolen from a grave. False light. That's why he went grey, little one. That's why the golden cracks. I watched them open across his face like a dropped plate, and I watched him not understand what was happening to him.

I was a moment too late. One moment. If I'd let one Hound go. If I'd turned at the first voice instead of the second.

Orrin saw me coming over the ridge, huge and black with my ember eyes, and he ran. He had Soren's ring in his hand. He'd pulled it off while the boy was still falling.

I reached Soren. He was already gone. His eyes were open. He'd fallen facing the way Zayn had walked.

I tried to close them. I had to shrink back into a fox even to try, and a fox's paws aren't made for it. They wouldn't stay shut. They kept opening, looking the way Zayn had gone. In the morning the boy closed them himself.

And then I said nothing.

I've told myself a hundred reasons. No proof. Who'd believe a half-Hollow beast against the king's vizier? If the boy had known too early, he'd have gone after Orrin with hate, and his light would have gone grey. All true, more or less.

But it isn't *why*.

The why is that I was ashamed. Ashamed of what I'd been out there, and of being slow, and that the boy I loved most would have to know I watched his only friend die and couldn't stop it.

So I let him carry it alone. For the rest of his life he thought it was his fault for walking away. I heard him whisper *I'm sorry* into the dark a hundred times, and I lay a few paces off with my eyes shut and pretended to be asleep.

He would have forgiven me. That's the worst of it. He'd have looked at me with those too-light eyes and said *it's all right, Kiri*, and his mark wouldn't have burned at all.

That's my confession. I'm telling you because you're the only one I've never lied to.

Not yet, anyway. Give me time.

---

Twelve years went by. Desert years. Long ones.

The grey never came back up. The villages filled again with people who remembered their names. Harrow's End was rebuilt around the same deep well. And there was a new lantern on the Night of Embers.

Auntie Sabba lit it.

She was so old by then she had to be helped to the patch of hard ground between the last house and the first dune, where a patched tent had once stood. Nobody had built there since. Nobody would.

She held the taper in both shaking hands. The lantern was clay, plain and small, with a feather scratched into its side.

"For the boy," she said, thin as paper, "who I should have spoken up for. Zayn. Who was Aeron."

She lit it.

The flame caught, and then it leaned. There was no wind. It leaned anyway, away from her, out toward the open desert, east, toward the far edge, the way a dog's head turns toward a sound you can't hear. For a breath it burned blue-grey, the colour of a Husk's skin. Then it straightened and went gold again, and Sabba blinked at it, and said nothing, and nobody else said anything either.

From it, one by one, the whole village lit theirs. Then they hung up the other eleven and said the names. Not the titles. The names. I'd told them every one. I'd decided someone ought to know they were children.

Bram lit his last, with Tobin, nineteen now and taller than him, carrying the taper. Bram had grown into a broad, quiet man with early grey in his beard who kept his father's well. He never talked about the oasis town. Every Night of Embers he stood at the twelfth lantern longer than anyone, until his wife came and took his hand.

Her name was Talia. She was round as the Ash that year.

The baby came that same night, after the lanterns were lit. A boy. Small and furious and very loud.

Talia was holding him against her in the dark of the little clay house when she went still.

There, just under his collarbone, was a mark the colour of honey. Shaped like a feather.

It was glowing.

She didn't call out. She didn't smile. She put her hand over it. Flat, hard, the way you'd cover a lamp in a house where something is looking in at the windows. She'd grown up on the story, every child in Harrow's End had, and she'd listened to it closely, and she knew exactly what that mark was for.

"No," she whispered. "No, no. Not him. Bram. *Bram.* Make it stop."

Bram looked at it a long time. Then he went down on his knees beside the bed and cried like a boy. And when he could speak again, he didn't ask anyone. There was no point. It was written across the whole desert in letters miles high, and the desert doesn't take requests.

"Aeron," he said, and his voice broke in the middle of it. "His name is Aeron."

My tail glowed. I was sitting in the doorway. I'd been sitting in that doorway since the Ash had turned nine times. Talia had stopped asking why. She looked at me now, over her son's head, and I saw her understand, and I saw her hate me for it. She was right to.

---

They help, Daruun and Tamir.

They built a house where Hesset's tent used to be and hung it with herbs and little bronze bells. Daruun is an old man now, older than his years. Every night, when the Fire goes down, he takes his long hunting spear and sits at the edge of the well-keeper's house, facing the dunes, and he doesn't go in until morning. He's been wrong once about what was coming. He doesn't mean to be wrong again.

Tamir doesn't sleep. I don't think he has slept a full night in twelve years. He walks the edge of the village in the dark, and sometimes I hear him reciting numbers under his breath, the ones he gave the children in the ship, as if counting them could bring them back. And every night, at the darkest hour, he sits under the baby's window and hums the phoenix song, and the baby stops crying to listen.

---

I'll tell you one more thing, and then I'll stop.

The dunes out at the far edge of the desert have started writing again. Out where no one has ever come back from. Out where Hesset was looking.

The first year, I could only see the shapes from the top of the Crown Dune. They aren't letters I know. I have walked this desert since its first grain, and I can't read a word.

The fifth year, I could see them from the walls of Veimore.

This spring, I climbed up on the roof of the well-house and saw them, very faint, on the rim of the world. They're coming closer. Every season, a little. The way the grey once came, low and flat and not in any hurry. It doesn't need to hurry.

And under the gold, when I put my ear to it, something is still holding its breath.

The Light doesn't come back for no reason, little one. It comes back because it's going to be needed. Because something is hungry. On one side, or the other. Or both. I've stopped being sure there's a difference.

But not tonight.

Tonight the lanterns are burning along the edge of the desert, and your mother is asleep with her hand over your chest, and your father is asleep in the chair beside her. Old Daruun is out at the edge of the house with his spear. Tamir is humming under the window. And I'm curled up beside your cradle with my nose on my paws, where I'll be every night from now on, whether anyone likes it or not.

You've been listening all this time. I know you have. Your mark has been glowing brighter at every part where he was brave. It's warm against my fur. Warmer every night.

So let me start again, the way I started it. The way I'll start it for you every night, until you're old enough to tell me to stop.

Let me tell you about a boy who had no name…

He was the one I loved most. Don't tell the others. Don't tell yourself, either. Not yet.

The end is the beginning, you see. It always has been. That's what the knot means. That's what I'm for. I was made to walk beside you, and to love you, and when it's time, to be the one who wakes you. I've done it twelve times. I've never once been able to stop.

And out there in the dark, past the lanterns and the golden dunes and the far edge of the world, something is already waiting for you.

The Fire is coming up.

Wake up, little one.

---

*End of Chapter Twenty*
