# Chapter Nineteen
## The Crown Dune

> *He'll walk where the dunes write the words of the sky,*
> *And the light will be born on the day he will die.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born; the verse mothers in the desert villages will not sing aloud

---

Mother Yusa found us on the palace stairs as we were leaving.

She'd come up out of the kitchens with a ladle in one hand like a club, flour and grey dust in her hair and a long red scratch down her cheek where something had reached for her in the dark. She saw the king's ring on the cord at Zayn's neck and understood everything at once. She didn't cry. She didn't ask. She pushed a small cloth bundle into his hands, and then she took his face between her two enormous palms and kissed his forehead, hard, the way you'd stamp a seal into wax.

She pulled back with her lips blistered. His skin was that hot. She didn't say a word about it.

Then she went back down into the dark to look for Idan's nurse, and I never saw her again. I don't know whether she found her. I don't know whether anything found Mother Yusa first. I have never asked. Some questions I have decided to leave in the ground.

Inside the bundle were dates. Rolled in honey.

---

We rode out through the eastern gate, the three of us on the pale camel, Daruun in front and Zayn behind him and me in the boy's lap, bleeding quietly into his shirt.

The streets were silent behind us. The Husks still stood where they'd stopped, every one facing east. We rode right past them. They didn't reach for us. They didn't turn their heads. As we passed, they began to say their last words again, very softly, all together, so that we went out of the city through a long low murmur of *bar the door* and *Mama, I'm here* and *I'll be home soon*. Then, one by one, as we went by, they stopped speaking and only smiled.

Zayn unwrapped the bundle and held the dates in his palm.

He'd always saved them. You remember. Ever since he was a small boy in a patched tent, he had saved dates and honey for the hardest days, as if you could store sweetness the way you store water.

He ate one. Then he held one out over Daruun's shoulder, and after a moment his father took it. Then he gave one to me, and I ate it, though I prefer scorpions and always will. The honey had half-melted against his hand.

"This is the hardest day," Zayn said. "I think. I don't think there's a harder one."

My tail glowed.

They rode in silence a while. The Crown Dune rose ahead of us, higher with every step, until it filled half the sky, and its flanks went on moving. Up. Pause. Down. The camel didn't want to go near it. Daruun had to beat it.

"Father," said Zayn.

It was the first time he'd called him that. I felt Daruun go rigid in the saddle.

"I forgive you."

Daruun didn't turn round. His shoulders shook. "You can't. I ran. If I hadn't run, none of this— your brother, the Hollow, all of it. You can't forgive that."

"I just did," said Zayn. "And I can't lie. So."

My tail lit up so bright it threw our shadow out across the sand ahead of us, long and gold, like a road.

Daruun made a sound I won't describe. He reached back with one hand without looking, and Zayn took it, and they rode like that, holding on, all the way to the foot of the dune. Zayn's hand was so hot that Daruun's palm blistered. He didn't let go.

---

The Husks were waiting.

Not only the ones from Veimore. All of them. They had come from the whole desert in the night, from the grey wastes and the emptied villages, from Harrow's End and the caravan roads and the oasis towns, and they stood in a ring around the foot of the Crown Dune a hundred deep. Herders. Traders. Old women. Children. Thousands of white, blank faces, all turned up toward the top of the dune, all smiling the same smile.

And all of them, together, very low, were humming the cult's two notes. The sound came up through the sand and into the camel's legs and into ours. It was like standing on the skin of a drum.

They parted for us like water.

I looked for faces I knew. I couldn't help it. I saw Garrem from the trading post. I saw a boy from the caravan. I saw, I think, the old headman Oszkar, with his beard full of sand. I didn't see Bram. I didn't know yet whether that was mercy.

We climbed. The camel couldn't, so we left it. The sand slid under our feet with every step, and under the sand, very faintly, something moved with the breathing, so that every step went down a little further than it should. Zayn went first, and Daruun came behind with his spear, and I came last, limping. Halfway up, Zayn turned and picked me up without a word and carried me the rest of the way, the way he'd carried me from the buried ship. His arms burned my fur. I let him. I was too tired to be proud.

And then we were at the top, and I looked up, and it was the vision.

I had seen it in his eyes every time he healed. Twelve times. Twenty. I had stopped counting. And here it was, every piece of it falling into place like the last stones of a tomb.

The Ash hung full and white above the Crown Dune, so close it looked like you could reach up and smudge it with your thumb. The top of the dune was a long, flat crest of pale sand, cold under our feet, so cold after his skin that it ached. Below us, on every side, the desert went away silver and grey to the edge of the world.

Orrin waited at the far end of the crest, with Idan.

Around him stood the Unnamed, perhaps twenty, their white masks shining like small moons. And a little apart from them, twin blades in his hands, stood Brother Seven, with the crack in his mask grown so wide that half his real face showed through, and the skin around the crack red and raw where he'd been tearing at it.

Idan stood quite still in Orrin's grip. His eyes were open. He was humming.

And between us and them, the grey was gathering.

---

It rose out of the sand like smoke from a fire put out with water. It thickened. It took a shape.

A boy.

A small boy, smaller than Zayn, barefoot and thin, in a shepherd's tunic, with a shepherd's crook. Everything about him was grey: skin, hair, ragged clothes. Only his eyes were different. Black, completely black, like two holes cut into the night, and when you looked into them too long you could see things moving far down, small and pale, like faces at the bottom of a well.

I knew that face. I had known it before there were cities in this desert. I'd sat beside it at a thousand fires when it was brown and laughing and smelled of sheep.

"Hello, Malek," I said.

"Hello, little fox." A child's voice, and under it a thousand others, a beat behind, the way the throne hall had echoed. "You've come to watch another one burn."

"I've come to watch one *choose*. It's different. You'd know, if you'd done it."

The grey boy smiled at me. The smile went a little too far back on both sides. It was the worst thing I have ever seen, and I have seen the inside of a Hound.

Then he turned his black eyes on Zayn.

"Look at them," Malek said softly, pointing his crook down at the ring of Husks. "Those are the people you're going to die for. That one threw stones at you when you were six. Those ones drove you out of your village. Those ones called you monster, and watched you starve. And you'd burn for them?"

Zayn's light flickered. It went grey at the edges.

"And here's what the fox won't tell you," said Malek, and his voice dropped, gentle as sand. "Ask him how many. Not bearers. *Children*. Ask him how old the Weaver's Daughter was. Ask him why, every time, it's a child. Ask him what is waiting on the other side of the light, with its mouth open. It isn't me, Zayn. I'm only the one who said no."

Zayn looked at me.

My tail glowed. Gold. Steady. I couldn't stop it. I've never been able to stop it.

I saw him see it. I saw it go into him.

"And after," said Malek, "they'll light a lantern. One more lantern in a row of eleven. In a hundred years no one will remember what it was for. No one remembers the bearers. Their names come off the walls like the names in Veimore. You'll be forgotten." A whisper. "You'll be forgotten, Zayn. Just like you always were."

My tail went dark. Pitch dark, cold as a dead coal.

But I could see the boy's face, and I could see it didn't matter what my tail said, because it was the oldest fear he had. The fear he'd been carrying since he was a baby crying in the sand. *Nobody will remember me.* And now, beside it, a new one I had put there: *and something is hungry for me, and always has been.*

His light went grey all the way through.

"There's a way out," said Orrin.

He stepped forward with one hand on Idan's shoulder and the thin grey blade in the other. His soft voice carried along the crest the way it had always filled the throne hall.

"Give me your name," he said. "That's all. Say it to the wind. Your true name, the one in your blood. Malek will take it, and the Light with it, and then it's over. No burning. You'll simply forget. And then everyone will forget, everyone in the whole desert, every loss, every grief, every dead son." Something moved in his faded eyes, far down. "I promise you, child, it doesn't hurt. I've done it. I burned my own name to stop remembering. Forgetting is the only mercy there is."

My tail flickered. Not dark. Not bright. Somewhere in between.

He believed it. That's the worst kind of lie, little one: the kind the liar has told himself so long it sounds like truth.

"Give me your name," said Orrin.

And Zayn, grey and shaking, opened his mouth.

---

Bells.

Small bronze bells, a great many of them, ringing faintly somewhere below on the slope.

Every head turned. Even the Husks' hum faltered.

Someone was climbing the Crown Dune. Slowly. On hands and knees through the sliding sand. He had a stick, but he wasn't using it any more. Around his waist hung a belt of tiny truth bells that chimed with every movement, and behind him, all the way down the slope, the Husks had turned to watch him pass, and none of them had touched him.

Old Hesset.

He should not have been there. He was bound to the edge of the desert, to Harrow's End, and had been for three hundred years, the price of the night he ran. To leave was to break the bond. To break the bond was to die.

He had walked across the whole Destiny Desert anyway.

He was dying as he climbed. You could see it happen. Every step was taking years off him that he didn't have. His beard was thin as smoke. His skin had gone papery and was splitting at the knuckles, and what came out of the splits wasn't blood but a fine pale dust. His milky eyes were fixed on nothing, and he climbed toward our voices as if they were a fire on a cold night.

He reached the crest and knelt there, gasping. Malek turned to him.

"Hesset," said the grey boy, warmly. "My old friend."

Every bell on Hesset's belt rang at once.

"I was never your friend," said Hesset. "You only told me what I wanted to hear, so I'd run. I've had three hundred years in the dark to work that out." He turned his blind face toward Zayn. "Boy. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"I asked you once, in my tent. *Would you die for them?* You couldn't answer." The old man swallowed. "I never told you someone asked me the same, on this sand, three hundred years ago. I said no. I said no and I ran, and the last thing I ever saw was the top of this dune. I've been in the dark since." His voice broke. "I hid that bearers die, because I was ashamed. And I hid something else. I hid that I *heard* it, child. Up here. When I said no. I heard it be disappointed." His hands clenched in the sand. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

Not a single bell rang.

"Don't answer for me," whispered Hesset. "Don't answer for them. Don't answer for whatever's listening. Answer for you."

Zayn stood very still on the top of the Crown Dune.

He looked at Malek. He looked at me, and at my tail, which had told him a thing about the Light that he would never now be able to stop knowing. He looked down at the thousands of Husks, and somewhere among them, maybe, was a boy who'd thrown a stone at him once. He looked at Idan, humming in Orrin's grip, forgetting his own name a little more with every breath. He looked at his father.

Then he untied Hesset's little truth bell from his belt and held it up in front of him, so everyone could see.

"Yes," he said.

The bell didn't ring.

And the grey went out of his light like a shadow when the Fire comes up. It turned gold. Not the gold of Harrow's End, or the City of Glass, or the stairs of Veimore. A deeper gold, the gold of honey held up to the light. It was beautiful. I want you to know that. It was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.

It was also the colour of a coal just before it falls apart.

Malek took a step backward.

---

"Brother Seven," said Orrin sharply. "Cut him."

Tamir raised his twin grey blades.

They were the only things in the world that could cut the Light. He stood between Zayn and Orrin, and the blades were shaking, and through the great crack in his mask one pale eye stared, wet and wide.

He was humming. Not the chant. The phoenix song. He didn't know he was doing it.

Zayn walked toward him.

He didn't raise his sword. He walked straight up to his brother until the grey blades were a finger's width from his chest, close enough that the steel began to frost and smoke at once where the Light met it.

"You held me," he said. "When I was born. I saw it. You were five, and you held me, and you were scared you'd drop me."

The blades trembled.

Then, slowly, Tamir lowered them.

Zayn walked past him to Orrin. The vizier backed away, dragging Idan, and laid the thin grey blade against the boy's throat. Zayn didn't stop. He put his hand over Orrin's hand, over the grip, and the gold went into it.

Orrin cried out and let go. His hand was grey to the wrist where the Light had touched it, and the grey was crazed with fine cracks, like a glaze. The blade fell into the sand.

Idan stopped humming. He blinked. He said, in a small, astonished voice, "Idan," as if he'd found it in a pocket. Then he ran.

He ran straight past Zayn to Daruun, and Daruun caught him, and the boy buried his face in the grey wrappings and shook. After a moment Zayn knelt before him. He untied Soren's ring from beside the infinity knot and pressed it into Idan's hand. It left a red mark on the small palm. Idan closed his fingers on it anyway.

"Your father wanted you to have this. And so would Soren."

Idan looked at him. He was ten. He wasn't stupid.

"Are you going to die?"

"Yes."

"Like Soren?"

Zayn thought about it. "No," he said. "Soren didn't get to choose. I do."

---

Daruun let go of Idan and took his son's hands, and didn't flinch at the heat.

"Let me," he said.

It was not the voice of the Veiled Rider who had saved us three times out of the dark. It was the voice of a man begging.

"Let me do it. It was mine before it was yours, and I ran. Let me pay. *Please.* Feed it me. I'm old, I'm nothing, let it have me instead. I ran so you could live. Don't make that for nothing. Not you. Please, son. Not you."

Zayn shook his head. He couldn't speak for a moment.

"It doesn't want you," he said, very gently. "It wants the Light. And the Light's in me." He tightened his grip. "And it wasn't for nothing. You ran, and you left me in the sand, and the desert kept me. I grew up alone, so I know what it's like to be the one nobody wants. That's why I can do this. That's why it's me. It all meant something. Even the bad parts. *Especially* the bad parts."

My tail was so bright I couldn't look at it.

I'll tell you what I thought, little one, because I promised you no more lies. I thought: *it meant something. And I don't know any more whether that's a comfort or the worst thing in the world.*

Daruun bowed his head over their joined hands. His whole body shook. I have seen men face Hounds and Veilbeasts and armies, and I have never seen anything as brave as what that man did next.

He let go.

He did it one finger at a time, slowly, like a man lowering something precious into deep water. The skin of his palms came away blistered and stuck to his son's hands, and he let go anyway. When the last finger slid free, he stepped back beside Idan and Hesset and didn't move again.

The hardest choice of his life. He made it.

---

Zayn turned to me last.

I was sitting in the sand, too tired to stand, my fur dark with my own blood, my silver eye aching as it always aches near the end. He knelt so our faces were level. Heat came off him like an open oven.

"You lied to me once," he said.

"I know."

"Tell me the truth now. Will it hurt?"

"Yes," I said. My tail glowed. "It will hurt more than anything has ever hurt."

"Will it matter?"

"Yes."

"Is Malek right? Is something waiting?"

I could have said nothing. I'd had a great deal of practice.

"I don't know," I said. And my tail, for the first time in all my life, did nothing at all. Neither gold nor dark. Because it was true that I didn't know, and it was true that I was afraid, and some truths are too big even for a fox's tail.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he smiled, the small, crooked, rusty smile he never wasted, and he put his forehead against mine. It singed the fur between my ears. I'll carry the mark of it until the world ends.

"Look after them," he said.

I couldn't answer. For the first time in all my long life, I couldn't think of a single word.

He stood. He walked to the very end of the crest, where the dune fell away on every side into the silver dark, and stood there under the full Ash with his back to us all.

"I always wanted to be a hero," he said quietly. Not to us. To the boy in the patched tent, maybe. "Like in the stories."

He took a breath.

"I am the hero."

It wasn't a boast. It was only true. My tail had never burned so bright.

Then he lifted his face to the Ash, and to whatever was beyond it, and he said the last words he would ever say.

"I'm coming, Soren."

---

And here I have to tell it the way I told it at the very beginning, because I have seen this moment so many times since that it has never stopped happening. It's happening now. It's always happening, somewhere behind my eyes, and I can still smell it.

He is burning.

He stands at the top of the Crown Dune with his arms open, as if he's about to catch something falling from the sky. The fire doesn't start at his hands. It starts in his chest, right where the mark is, the small golden feather that has glowed in the dark his whole life. It cracks open like a seed. I hear it crack. Light pours through the cracks in his skin, through every golden line that every healing left on him, and the lines split wider, and his skin curls back from them like paper from a flame. It runs down his arms and up his throat and out of his eyes.

His hair goes first. Then his shirt. The smell reaches me, and I will not describe it, and I will never stop smelling it.

And Orrin lunges.

Grey robes, grey hand, grey eyes wide and starving. He's spent his whole life waiting for this light, and he throws himself into it with both hands outstretched, grabbing, as if he could tear it out of the boy's chest and keep it.

"*Mine,*" he says.

It isn't. It never could be. The Light can't be taken. It can only be given.

It turns grey in his hands. The gold he's grasping goes dull, then ashen, and the grey runs up his fingers and wrists and arms like frost climbing a window, cracking as it climbs. He looks down at himself. For a single moment his face changes. He sees something in the stolen light: a small boy, and a brother, and a name he burned a very long time ago. His mouth opens to say it.

"Ez—"

His jaw comes off.

It doesn't fall. It crumbles, like an old grey statue that has stood in the wind too long, and then the rest of him follows, from the face down, still kneeling, still reaching. The last of him to go is his hands, clenched in the light, trying to hold on to it. The wind lifts the dust off the top of the Crown Dune and carries it away into the dark, the way it carries away a name that has been spoken carelessly.

There is nothing left of him at all. Not even the name. He never finished it.

And the boy doesn't scream.

That's the part I always remember. He doesn't scream. He is burning down to the bone and he doesn't scream, and I have never in all my life understood how, and I have never wanted to.

He looks down. Below him, all across the desert, the dunes are moving. Under the full Ash, in the silver dark, they shift and fold and rise, writing something across the sand in letters miles high. The same shapes they wrote for him on the first nights after he left Harrow's End, when he couldn't read a word and I wouldn't tell him what they said.

He can read them now.

A boy with a broken sword taught him, by a fire, with a stick in the sand, laughing the whole time.

*A*, the dunes write. *E. R. O. N.*

*Aeron.* In the old tongue, it means *the one who rises.*

It's his name. His true name, the one the Weaver wrote for him before he was born, the one the wind could never find, because the desert kept it safe for him all this time. The way you keep something safe that you mean to use. He reads it, the last thing he will ever read, with eyes that are already more light than eye, and I see what's left of his mouth smile.

Then he isn't looking at the dunes. He's looking at something only he can see. But I see it too, for a moment, in the light pouring out of him.

The Singing Wells, under a thin Ash. The water humming. A young man on the edge of the well with half a sword across his knees, a scarred jaw and a crooked grin and dark eyes, laughing. Laughing at him, the way he always did. Holding out his hand.

*You took your time,* says Soren.

And behind Soren, in the dark beyond the well, standing in a row as if they've been waiting there a very long time, I see the others. Small figures. Eight of them, the eight who gave themselves. Some barely taller than the well's edge. A girl of nine. A boy with salt-white hair. A smith's apprentice with burned hands. They're all looking at Zayn. None of them are smiling. Their faces are patient and gentle and very, very tired, the faces of children who have stood in a queue too long.

Then Zayn laughs, the way he laughed the first time, by a fire, when a prince tried to eat a scorpion. And he reaches out and takes the hand. And the row of children opens to let him in, and he takes his place at the end of it.

There's a space beside him. An empty space, at the very end of the row, the width of one small child.

I see it every time. I have never once been able to look away from it.

The light keeps growing until it isn't a boy anymore. It's a shape, two great wings of gold folding out across the dark, wide enough to cover the dunes from one edge of the sky to the other. The heat of it blisters the sand to glass at my feet.

Then it falls.

Gold rains down over the desert, and everywhere it lands, the grey goes away.

Somewhere below, someone is shouting a name.

It's his father. On his knees in the sand a little way down the crest, his arms around a crying prince, his face turned up into the falling gold, the skin of his hands still raw from letting go. Shouting it over and over, the only name he was ever allowed to call his son.

"*Zayn! Zayn!*"

And the wind takes it, gently, and carries it out over the whole desert. Not to the dark this time.

There isn't any dark left.

That's what I believed, that night. I want you to know I believed it. For one whole night, I did.

---

*End of Chapter Nineteen*
