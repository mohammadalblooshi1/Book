# Chapter Seven
## The Caravan and the Beast

> *When the sky goes dark at noon, pray to the Fire or run. If you hear your mother calling from inside the dark, do not turn around.*
> — a caravan saying, older than any caravan still on the road

---

You smell a caravan long before you see it.

Camel, mostly. Then dung smoke, and spice, and sixty people who haven't washed in a month because water is money. It's the smell of the living. After a valley full of ghosts, it smelled like hope.

To Zayn, it smelled like a city. He'd never seen one.

We found the road in the middle of the morning, a line of hard-packed ground marked with stone cairns, running north and west between the dunes. And on it, crawling along like a great brown caterpillar, was the caravan of Master Dagan.

Forty camels, roped nose to tail, groaning under bales and water jars. A dozen wagons on wide wooden wheels. Six sand lizards scaled the colour of dust, ranging along the flanks like dogs around a flock. Hooded falcons on perches above the lead wagon.

Zayn stood on the ridge above the road and watched it pass with his mouth slightly open.

"Close your mouth," I said. "You'll swallow a fly. Or a camel."

---

Master Dagan was a broad, bald, sunburnt man with a beard dyed orange with henna and a voice that could have cracked rock. He looked Zayn up and down the way he'd look at a camel he suspected of being lame.

"Name?"

Zayn hesitated. Then he said the only true thing he could. "In my village they called me Sandborn."

"That's not a name, that's a weather report." Dagan grunted. "Doesn't matter. One of my boys ran off at the last well and I'm short a pair of hands. You can load, you can dig, you can shovel dung?"

"Yes."

"You'll work for water and food and a place by the fire. No tokens. You don't like it, the desert's that way." He jerked a thumb at the horizon, then squinted down at me. "What's that?"

"A fox," said Zayn.

"It's *my* fox," I said, before I could stop myself. "And it has a name, thank you."

There was a long pause.

"Huh," said Master Dagan at last. "Talking fox. Saw a two-headed goat in the salt towns once." He turned away, already bellowing at someone about a loose rope. "Keep it out of the food stores. And *circle the wagons* at dusk, the lot of you, or so help me I'll feed you to it myself."

*Circle the wagons.* He said it at every halt, like a prayer. You'll see why I remember.

---

Zayn worked.

He was good at it. He loaded bales and dug out wheels and shovelled dung, and didn't complain. He kept his scarf wound high around his throat and his shirt tied close across his chest, so nobody would see the faint glow of the feather mark after dark.

He was happy. I don't think he knew it. But I knew what his face looked like when nobody was throwing anything at it, and I tried very hard to memorise it, the way you'd press a flower in a book you knew was going to burn.

On the second night, a boy came and sat beside him at the fire.

His name was Pell. He was the youngest lizard rider in the caravan, sixteen or so, with a gap in his front teeth. His lizard was a big female called Dustmother, and he talked about her the way some people talk about their grandmothers.

He'd brought dates. Plain ones, not honeyed, but he held out the cloth without being asked, and after a moment Zayn took one.

"You're from the edge villages," Pell said. "I can tell. You walk like the sand's going to bite you."

"It does, sometimes."

Pell laughed. It was an easy laugh, loud and unworried.

They talked late. Or Pell talked, and Zayn listened, mostly to the rumours along the road. There was a lot of grey out east this season, Pell said. Whole villages gone quiet. A well-keeper's family found sitting round a cold hearth with their bowls in their laps, smiling at nothing, saying *pass the salt, pass the salt, pass the salt*. And up in Veimore, the great red city, the old king had thrown his own eldest son out into the desert for dark magic. Hiding a Hollow-touched boy in the palace, people said, and trying to cure him.

"Cure him!" Pell shook his head. "You can't cure the grey. Everybody knows that. You give them to the dunes, quick, before they start smiling at you."

Zayn said nothing. His hand had gone to his chest.

I lay by the fire with my eyes half closed and said nothing either. My tail stayed very still.

---

On the third day, at noon, the falcons wouldn't fly.

Dagan's falconer threw the lead bird into the air, and it went up three wingbeats and came straight back to its perch and sat there with its feathers flattened, shivering, its hooded head turned east. He tried another. The same.

The camels knelt, one by one, without being told, and lowered their long necks and laid their heads flat on the sand and closed their eyes, the way camels do for the butcher.

"Kiri," Zayn said quietly.

"I know," I said.

The wind had stopped.

Out here the wind never stops completely. Now there was nothing. The air hung heavy and hot and dead. The Fire was high and white above us, and the sky was clear blue in every direction.

Except to the east.

To the east, low on the horizon, there was a line of black.

At first it looked like a sandstorm, and the caravan men relaxed a little. Dagan was already bellowing: circle the wagons.

But it wasn't moving like a storm.

Storms run *with* the wind. This was coming against it, out of the dead air, rising higher as it came, and it wasn't brown or yellow like sand. It was black, like the inside of a closed eyelid. And it was silent. The silence came ahead of it like a smell.

And it was getting dark.

Noon, the Fire at its height, and the light was going out of the world. The colours went first: the red of the wagons, the orange of Dagan's beard. Everything went the colour of old bone. Then the shadows went. As if something enormous had put its mouth to the sky and was drinking the light out of it like milk from a bowl.

"When the sky goes dark at noon," said Pell beside us, very softly. All the laughter had gone out of him. "Pray to the Fire or run."

"Run," I said. "Run now. All of you. Leave the wagons, cut the camels loose, *run*—"

Nobody listened. Nobody ever does.

---

It hit us like the end of the world.

The wind came first, a wall of it, so hard it knocked Zayn flat. Sand came screaming sideways.

Then the darkness came down over the caravan like a lid on a pot.

And inside it, something moved.

I've seen most of the creatures of the Hollow. I've seen nothing like the Veilbeast. Nobody who's seen it all at once has kept their name.

It was the storm, and it was something *inside* the storm, and you couldn't tell where one stopped and the other began. Its body was the black wind itself, coiling and folding on itself like a great gut, and in the folds of it were shapes. Limbs, perhaps. The suggestion of limbs, huge and slow and jointed in too many places, feeling across the sand the way a blind man feels for a dropped coin. One passed over us. It was *dense*, the way grief is dense, and where it brushed the top of an overturned wagon the wood went grey and soft and sagged like wet bread.

High up there were lights in the dark. Red ones. Dozens of them, scattered across the black, blinking slowly, not all together. Some were large as a well-mouth. Some were small and set in pairs, at about the height of a man's head, and there were a great many of them.

Eyes. All the eyes of everyone it had ever swallowed, still open in there, still looking out.

And beneath them, in the very heart of the storm, a hole.

Not a mouth. A mouth has edges. This was a great round silent place where even the darkness was darker, where the roaring wind went in and didn't come out. Sand flowed toward it in long hissing streams, like water to a drain. My ears popped. Something warm ran out of my nose. And the hole made a sound, at last, the only sound I ever heard it make.

It *breathed in*.

---

The front of the caravan went first, Dagan's own camel kneeling with its eyes closed. The dark closed over them, and when it lifted there was smooth sand, raked as neat as a temple floor.

Then the camels behind, roped nose to tail. The dark took the end of the rope and pulled, and they went in one after another like beads off a string, not a sound out of any of them.

The people screamed. And then the screams changed.

They didn't stop the way screams stop when someone dies. They *frayed*. A man would be shrieking for his brother, and halfway through the brother's name the scream would forget what it was for. It would trail off into a puzzled little note, then a hum, then nothing.

That's what the Veilbeast does. It doesn't eat you. Eating would be kind. It swallows you, and inside it is the Hollow, all of it at once, like being drowned in a well of everyone's forgetting. Whatever comes out the other side, if anything does, walks on your legs and doesn't know your face.

Zayn was on his feet. I don't know how. His scarf had torn loose, and the feather mark was blazing through his shirt, so bright it lit a circle of sand around him in the dark.

And the Veilbeast's red eyes, high up in the storm, turned toward it.

All of them. At once. The big ones and the small ones in their pairs.

"*Down*," I screamed at him. "Get *down*, it can see you, it can *see* you—"

The light in his hands came grey.

It came grey and thin and terrified, a dead candle in a dead wind. I watched the fear go all the way down into him, like a stone dropped down a well that never hits the bottom.

And then the voices started.

Soft and close, the way the valley had spoken, but these weren't echoes. These were the people it had swallowed, and it was *wearing* them.

"*Circle the wagons*," said Master Dagan's voice, very calmly, from the black. "*Circle the wagons. Circle the wagons. Circle the wagons.*"

"*Where's my*—" said a woman's voice. "*Where's my*— *Where's my*—"

And a child's. A little girl from the water-sellers. I never knew her name. "*Mama, it's dark. Mama, it's dark. Mama, it's dark.*"

Last words. That's all that's left of you, inside it, playing over and over in the black like a bell that won't stop swinging.

Then, through the sand, I saw Pell.

Dustmother was down. She lay on her side kicking, and Pell was pinned beneath her, one leg trapped under her weight. He was screaming. Not Zayn's name. He didn't know Zayn's name. *Sandborn.* It was the only one he had.

And the edge of the dark was coming toward him, slow and deliberate, sliding over the sand like a tide that knows exactly where you've buried your feet.

Zayn ran.

He ran straight into the wind, toward the boy who'd shared dates with him, and somewhere in those few steps the light changed, grey to gold, like a coal catching. He threw himself down over Pell and the struggling lizard with his arms spread wide, and the light came out of him in a dome. A shell of gold, closing over the three of them like cupped hands over a moth.

The darkness came down on top of it.

---

I was outside the light. I want you to know that. I wasn't brave. I'd gone flat in the lee of an overturned wagon, with my silver eye screaming at me to let the other half out and fight, and I stayed small.

I saw a small gold glow in the middle of a world gone black. The Veilbeast settled over it like a hen on an egg. The gold bent and went thin as a soap bubble, and the eyes crowded in close above it, hungry.

What Zayn saw was the underside of it.

He told me, much later, that from inside the dome the Veilbeast wasn't black at all. It was *grey*, close up. Grey and soft and folded, like the inside of a lung. And pressed against the outside of the gold, flattened like faces against a tent wall, were faces. Hundreds. Master Dagan, his orange beard gone the colour of ash. The falconer. The little girl from the water-sellers, her small hands pressed flat on the gold right in front of his face, saying *Mama, it's dark* with no sound, over and over, smiling, and not knowing what a smile was for.

They were trying to get in. Not to hurt him. They were cold, he said. They wanted the warm.

He held the dome. He held it with the gold cracking open across his skin like the glaze on a dropped pot. He could feel each face where it pressed. He said it was like being leaned on by every lonely person who'd ever lived.

And then, slowly, like a tide going out, the darkness lifted.

The black wall of the storm went rolling away west across the dunes, against the wind, its red eyes winking out into the distance one pair at a time. It was still noon. It had all taken less time than it takes to eat a meal.

I got up.

The caravan of Master Dagan was gone.

Sixty people. Forty camels. The road was swept clean. No bodies. No blood. A single wagon wheel on its side, still turning slowly, creaking, *creak, creak, creak*, in the dead air.

Far off across the dunes, small grey figures were walking away, slow and aimless, the way Husks walk. Their arms didn't swing. One of them had a beard dyed orange with henna. As he went, faint on what little wind there was, I heard him calling, cheerful and flat: "*Circle the wagons. Circle the wagons.*" He didn't look back.

In the middle of that great swept emptiness, a small golden glow went out.

---

Zayn was lying in a drift of sand with his arms still spread. The golden cracks were everywhere, even across his eyelids. He was shaking so hard his teeth clattered. There were marks on the sand all round the place where the dome had been. Small hand-shaped hollows, hundreds of them, pressed into the sand in a ring, where they had leaned.

Of Pell, there was only a hand sticking up out of the sand.

The storm had buried him, him and the lizard both. Zayn dug. He had nothing left, and he dug anyway, with his bare cracked hands, until he found a shoulder, and a face, and dragged the boy out by the collar.

Dustmother was dead beneath him. Her open eyes were grey all the way through.

Pell was alive. Mostly.

His leg was broken. And the left side of him, arm and shoulder and face, had gone grey where the edge of the dark had licked him before the gold closed over. His left eye was white as a boiled egg. His mouth smiled on that side only, slack and sweet, and he was mumbling names, half finished, trailing off. "*Dust*— *Dusty*— *Mo*—"

"Don't," I said. "Look at you. You've nothing left. You do this now and it'll bring every Hound for a day's ride. And it'll bring *that* back."

He laid his hands on Pell's face anyway. Of course he did.

---

It came gold this time without being asked. Warmth, flowing out of him and into the boy, and the grey began to lift, peeling back from Pell's cheek the way frost peels from a stone.

He saw Pell's life. A small boy tossing scraps to baby lizards, refusing to cry about his first bite. Then forward: Pell older, grey at the temples, telling children a story by a fire, his hands describing something huge. The children weren't delighted. They were frightened.

And then the other vision.

Longer now. Much longer. It didn't flash. It *took* him.

The full Ash above him, so vast and white it seemed to be falling. The Crown Dune under his bare feet. He knew its name now, though nobody had ever told him. He felt his arms rise. And then the fire started in his chest, and this time it didn't stop at a flash, and this time he *felt* it.

Not light. Fire. Real fire. He felt the skin of his chest tighten and split. He felt the heat go into his ribs, and climb his throat, and fill his mouth so that he couldn't scream, and come out through his eyes. He smelled his own hair burning, and under it, something worse, something like meat.

Below him, someone was shouting a name. Over and over. A man's voice, cracked down the middle.

Then it was gone, and he was on his knees on the caravan road, retching, with his hands still on Pell's face and the smell of burnt hair still real in his nose. It was his own hair. The fringe had crisped where the light came out of his eyes.

---

Pell opened his eyes. Both of them brown.

The grey was gone from his face and arm. His leg was straight. He took a breath, a huge shuddering one, and looked up.

At Zayn, cracked all over with glowing gold, light still leaking from the corners of his eyes, his hair smoking. At the swept, empty desert where an hour before there had been everyone he knew.

I watched him put it together. I watched him do the sum, the way frightened people always do, quick and wrong.

"It looked at you," Pell whispered. "I saw it. All those eyes. It came, and it looked straight at *you*."

Zayn didn't answer.

"It came for you." Pell was scrambling backward through the sand, away from him, his newly healed leg kicking. "Didn't it? You brought it. You're what it was looking for. Everyone's gone, Dagan's gone, they're all *gone*, and you—"

His face twisted.

"*Monster.*"

Zayn flinched. Like a slap.

"I'm not a monster," he said.

My tail glowed. It glowed bright and steady and true. But I watched it closely, all the while Pell was talking, and here's the thing I've never been able to forgive.

When Pell said *it came for you*, my tail didn't dim.

Not even a flicker.

Pell got to his feet, swaying on the leg Zayn had mended. Then he turned and ran, stumbling, out across the empty sand, and he didn't look back. Not once. I hope he's the old man by the fire. I hope he tells it wrong.

Zayn knelt where he'd left him and watched him go.

---

The ground began to move under us.

The sand began to sink and turn like water going down a drain. Where the caravan had been, a hollow was opening, and from the bottom of it, very faintly, came the creak of a wagon wheel, still turning, somewhere underneath.

Far off to the west, at the edge of the sky, the black storm had stopped moving.

Its red eyes had turned back toward us. All of them.

And very faintly, on no wind at all, a little girl's voice: "*Mama, it's dark.*"

"*Up*," I said. "Zayn. Up. *Now*."

He tried. He'd given everything to the dome and the rest to Pell, and there was nothing left. He got halfway up and the sand slid out from under him, pouring now, pulling him toward the centre of the hollow, toward the creaking.

I grabbed his collar in my teeth and pulled. I felt the silver half of me straining, wanting to come out, wanting to *grow*. *Let me,* it whispered. *I could hold the whole desert.* I almost let it.

The sand was at his waist. At his chest.

Then came the hoofbeats.

---

The pale camel came over the edge of the hollow at a dead run. A wrapped hand closed on Zayn's arm. A heave, a grunt, and Zayn came up out of the sand like a cork from a bottle and was thrown across the saddle face down.

"The *fox*," Zayn gasped. "Kiri—"

The rider didn't hesitate. He hooked me up by the scruff with the butt of the long spear and dropped me on top of the boy, and the camel was already turning, already running, out of the hollow and away from the storm.

Behind us, the Veilbeast was coming back.

I saw it over the rider's shoulder, rising black against the sky, faster now, eager. And on the ridge between us and it, a grey robe, flapping. A smooth white mask with no face.

Brother Seven was standing in our path.

He drew one grey blade as the camel thundered toward him and swung it at the boy slung across the saddle. A clean, fast, perfect stroke. It went past Zayn's throat a finger's width away and cut nothing but air.

Brother Seven doesn't miss by accident. He stood on the ridge and watched us go, the blade hanging at his side, and the storm came down over the place where he stood and he didn't move. He stood with his white mask tipped up toward all those eyes, as if they were old friends.

Just before the dark took him, I heard it, very faint, under the wind. Four notes, low and slow, climbing.

Then I lost sight of him in the sand.

---

The rider didn't stop until the Fire was going down.

By then the storm was a smudge on the horizon behind us. The rider slid Zayn off onto firm ground and set me down beside him. He crouched over the boy without touching him and looked at the golden cracks on the boy's face, and the singed fringe, for a long, long time. His wrapped hands were shaking. I filed that away with all the other things I wasn't saying.

Then he stood and pointed with the long spear. North and west.

"Wait," Zayn croaked. "Who are you? Why do you keep—"

The rider mounted, and in a few moments was gone into the dusk the way he'd gone the first time, without a word.

"That's twice," Zayn whispered.

"Yes," I said.

"Kiri, who *is* he?"

I didn't answer that. You know by now that I didn't.

---

We walked the way the spear had pointed. We had nothing else.

Almost nothing. Where the rider had crouched, he'd left a water skin, half full, without a word, the way Auntie Sabba used to leave her bundles.

Zayn made it last. By the next afternoon the skin hung flat against his hip. The golden lines on his skin faded slowly to a dull shimmer. Near dusk he ate one of Auntie Sabba's squashed honey dates. Just one. He held it in his mouth a long time, like a child holding a hand.

He didn't say anything about Pell, or my tail when Pell said *it came for you*. He'd seen it. I know he had.

Once he stopped and sniffed the back of his own wrist. He could still smell burnt hair on himself. I could too. I'm not sure he ever knew, after that, which fires were real.

The Ash came up late that night. It was thin, thinner than I'd seen it since we left Harrow's End, a pale scratch of white low over the dunes. It hardly gave any light at all.

And somewhere ahead of us, so faint only a fox could feel it, something was humming.

I felt it first in my teeth. Then in the pads of my feet. A deep, low, steady note rising out of the dark to the north and west, exactly where the spear had pointed, like a mother humming a child to sleep. Or a well, waiting.

I knew that sound, and what it did to people who drank from it. And I knew the rider had not pointed us at it by accident.

I didn't tell Zayn. He was walking with his head down and his hand pressed to the feather mark, and he hadn't heard it yet. In a little while he would.

After eleven bearers, you'd think I'd know what waits beyond every dune. I don't. That night I didn't know the desert had saved one more thing for him by the singing water. Not a monster, or a vision, or a stranger in grey.

Something worse, in the end. Something he could love.

If I had known, I'd have made him walk faster. Or slower. I've never decided which.

---

*End of Chapter Seven*
