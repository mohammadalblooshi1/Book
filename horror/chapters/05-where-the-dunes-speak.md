# Chapter Five
## Where the Dunes Speak

> *The sand writes; the wise read; the rest are buried. The sand writes on them too.*
> — a proverb of the old caravan scribes

---

The desert doesn't care about you. That's the first thing you learn out there, and the last thing most people forget.

It isn't cruel. Cruelty takes attention. The desert simply goes on being what it is, and if you happen to die in it, it covers you up with the same patience it covers everything else. Then, a few hundred years later, it uncovers you again, just to see. I've seen faces come up after the night's shifting, dry and perfectly kept, mouths open, as if they'd been calling for help the whole time.

Zayn knew the desert. It was the one advantage he had.

He knew to walk in the cool hours and lie up through the white glare of noon in the shadow of a dune's steep side, scarf over his face. He knew that under a tuft of thornweed there was sometimes damp sand, and if you dug and waited, a little brown water would seep into the hole. And he knew to lick the dew from the undersides of flat stones at dawn.

"That's revolting," I said, the first morning I saw him do it.

He wiped his mouth. "You eat scorpions."

"Scorpions are a *meal*. That is a *rock*."

"It's water."

I had no answer to that.

---

We walked for six days.

The straight path lasted only the first morning. After that, every dawn the way ahead looked different. Zayn would stand on the highest ridge he could find and turn slowly in a circle until something told him which way to go. A tug in the feather mark. Some mornings he stood there a long time, and I knew he was wondering whether the thing pulling him was the Weaver or something else, and whether there was a difference.

He asked me questions. Not many. He'd never had anyone to ask before.

"How old are you?"

"Older than you."

My tail glowed. He frowned at it.

"How much older?"

"Much."

It glowed again. He gave up on that one.

"Do you have a name?"

Nobody had asked me that in a very long time. The Boy of Salt had asked, once, and said it over and over on the way up the dune, as if it would help. "Kiri," I said.

He tried it under his breath. *Kiri.* He didn't say it to the wind. He'd learned that much. When the wind came round from the north that evening, he flinched at every gust, waiting for it to say his own name back. It didn't. The waiting was worse.

"Why did you watch me? All those years. In the village."

"Because somebody had to," I said.

That one glowed brightest of all, and he didn't ask anything else for the rest of the day.

He touched the bell a lot, and the feather mark, and at night the folded cloth where the broken feather lay, beating its slow wrong beat.

He didn't sleep much. When he did, he woke with his hands slapping at his chest, putting out a fire. Once he sat up and said, perfectly clearly, "It's so high up," and didn't remember it in the morning. I did.

On the third night he finished Auntie Sabba's bread. He ate it very slowly. The four squashed dates he left where they were. He wasn't there yet. He knew harder days were coming.

He was right about that, at least.

---

The dunes spoke on the fifth night.

We'd stopped at the foot of a long curving ridge, and Zayn had scooped a hollow in the sand to sleep in. The Ash had risen late and lopsided. I lay with my nose on my paws and watched him not sleep.

Then the sand began to move.

Something small. Close. On the long smooth face of the dune in front of us, the sand was rippling, the way the skin of your arm ripples when something moves under it. Fine lines were drawing themselves across the slope. Curves and hooks and straight strokes, crossing and joining, and they weren't being drawn on the sand. They were being pushed up from beneath it, from inside the dune, as if by a single fingertip moving very slowly just under the surface. As they formed, they began to glow.

Gold.

Zayn sat up so fast he sprayed sand everywhere.

The marks went on writing themselves. A line, then a second line beneath it, glowing across the dark face of the dune like Embers fallen out of the sky. They had the shape of meaning, the way a voice in the next room has the shape of words even when you can't make them out.

I could hear it, too. A faint dry whisper as the strokes moved, like a quill on old paper, like many quills, as if a great many hands were writing at once under the sand, all of them patient, all of them in the dark.

Zayn got up and walked toward it. He looked for a long, long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "I can't read."

Reading was for rich people. Nobody had ever thought to teach a nameless scavenger his letters, and it had never mattered before.

It mattered now. I could see it go into him like the word *monster* did.

"Kiri," he said. "What does it say?"

I didn't answer.

He turned. "You can read it. Can't you."

"Yes."

My tail glowed. There was no point lying about that.

"Then tell me what it says."

"No."

In the light from the dune his face was very young and very angry. "*Why not?*"

"Because it isn't written for me," I said. "The Weaver writes the sands, and the bearer reads them. Never the fox. If I read it to you, it's just a fox telling you things."

"It's written for me," he said, "in a way I can't understand. So it's written for nobody."

He said it flatly. That was worse.

"The Weaver forgot me in the sand when I was a baby," he went on. "And now it writes me letters I can't read. That's very funny. That's the funniest thing I've ever heard."

He turned his back on the dune, walked to his hollow and lay down in it, facing away from the light.

The writing glowed on behind him for a long time. The whispering went on with it. Then, slowly, the sand smoothed itself over, and the gold faded, and the sound stopped. Except that just before it stopped, the strokes under the sand went still all at once, every one of them, the way a room goes quiet when someone it was talking about walks in.

---

I'll tell you something now that I didn't tell him.

I read it. Of course I did. I've been reading the sands since before there were cities in this desert.

The bottom line was short. A single word, written large.

I'm not going to tell you what it was. It wasn't mine to read, and it isn't mine to tell. But I'll tell you this: I'd seen that line before. Not that word. But that line, in that place on the dune, at the bottom, written large. Eleven times I had seen it, over eleven different children, and each time the word was different, and each time it meant the same thing.

I lay awake the rest of that night with my nose on my paws, watching the place where it had been, and I don't think I've ever hated the Weaver quite so much. It writes like someone who loves you. It writes like that every time.

If he'd been able to read it that night, I wonder whether anything would have been different.

No. I know better. Nothing would have been different. It never is. That's not despair, by the way. That's arithmetic.

---

The Sandwraith came the next afternoon.

We were crossing a wide flat basin between two lines of dunes, the kind of place where the sand lies smooth and pale as spilled flour. It was the hottest hour. There was no shade in that basin, and Zayn had decided to push across.

I felt it first. A tremor under my paws. Small. Then again, stronger.

Then a sound, coming up through the sand. *Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.*

A camel bell.

There were no camels in that basin. There was nothing in that basin but us.

"Don't move," I said.

He stopped.

The sand ahead of us bulged.

It rose in a long low ridge perhaps forty paces long, like a blanket over something moving. It sank. It rose again, closer, and the sand on top of it darkened, the pale gold going black as if something were soaking up through it. The bell rang inside it. *Tonk. Tonk.*

"What is that?" Zayn whispered.

"Run," I said. He ran.

The ground behind us *erupted*.

It came up out of the basin like a fountain of black sand, rearing, impossibly long, a serpent made of darkness and dust. No scales. No eyes. Its body was the black sand itself, endlessly pouring and re-forming, hissing as it moved, and where its head should have been there was a wide blunt shape that opened in the middle like a split seam.

And through the pouring black of its body, as it rose, I saw what it had eaten.

A camel's ribcage, turning slowly inside it, the bell still on its bridle. A man's hand, brown and dried, the fingers still curled around the strap of a water skin. A face, pressed against the inside of the sand as it poured past, the mouth open in exactly the shape of the faces the desert sometimes gives back. They went round and round inside it like things in a slow river, and none of them would ever come out.

It was taller than any house in Harrow's End. It was still coming up out of the ground.

Sandwraith. They swim beneath the dunes the way fish swim beneath water, and when they find something warm, they come up and swallow it, and keep it, and go back down.

Zayn had something warm inside him. Something that, to every creature of the Hollow, burned like a beacon.

It came down at us like a falling tower.

Zayn threw himself sideways. The wraith struck where he'd been and went straight into the ground, and the basin heaved and knocked him flat. He came up spitting, knife in hand.

"Keep *running*!" I shrieked.

It burst up in front of him, a black wall of hissing dust with the bell clanking somewhere inside, and he skidded, fell, scrambled back. It was herding him. The way a cat herds a mouse into a corner. It knew this basin. He didn't.

The feather mark was burning through his shirt. He held out his hand, and the light came.

Grey.

He was terrified. A thin sputtering light flickering in his palm like a candle in a draught. The wraith didn't even pause. It reared up over him, blotting out the Fire, the split in its head opening wide, and the air that came out of it was cold and wet and smelled of the bottom of an old well, and of the people in it.

And that was when I did something stupid.

---

I went for it.

I'm not a fighter. I've survived this long by being somewhere else when the teeth come out. But I didn't think. I ran straight up the loose sand at the base of its body and bit it.

It tasted of nothing. Dust and cold. And, very faintly, of the people inside.

The wraith twisted. A coil of black sand whipped round and caught me, pouring and squeezing at once. I couldn't breathe. I felt myself being pulled down, toward the darkness under the dunes where the thing lived, and something brushed against my side in the black, and it was a hand. It was dry and light and it closed, gently, on my fur, the way you'd hold on to someone who'd just arrived. Welcoming me in.

I heard Zayn shout my name.

Through a gap in the black sand, I saw him get to his feet.

And I saw the light change.

It's the same every time. One moment it was grey, the light of a boy afraid for himself. The next, it wasn't, because he wasn't afraid for himself any more. He was looking at a small rude fox being dragged into the sand, and he forgot his own fear the way you forget a splinter when the house is on fire.

The light went gold.

It roared up out of his chest and out of his hands, and he didn't throw it, exactly. He *reached* for me, and the light reached with him.

It hit the Sandwraith square in the body.

Where the gold touched the black sand, the sand stopped pouring. It turned glassy and dark and then, with a sound like a thousand tiny bells breaking, it shattered. The coil around me burst apart, and the dry hand let go of me last. I fell, landed hard, rolled. Above me, the light raced up the wraith's body the way fire races up a dry palm. The whole vast shape shrieked, high and thin, and in that shriek, I swear to you, there were voices. Many. Then it collapsed in a slithering rain of black glass and ash that pattered over the basin for a long time.

What it had been keeping fell with it. Bones. The bell, rolling to a stop at Zayn's feet, ringing once, then quiet. The dried hand, still holding its water skin.

When it was over, Zayn was on his knees.

Golden cracks ran up his forearms and down his neck. And for a moment his eyes went far away and white, and his mouth opened, and I knew he was seeing it again, the Ash, the height, the heat. It had come without a healing, this time. Just from using the Light. It was learning the way in.

He came back. He looked at me.

"Are you hurt?" he said.

I spat out a mouthful of sand. "*Am I hurt.* I've just been half eaten by a sand worm and cuddled by a dead man, and you want to know if I'm *hurt*."

"Are you?"

"No," I said.

My tail glowed. He let out a long breath and sat back on his heels.

"Good," he said.

And I had to look away from him at the black glass scattered across the sand, because there was something in my eye. Sand, probably. There was a great deal of sand.

---

He didn't hear the humming until it was very close. Neither did I. I'll never forgive myself for that.

Light and noise bring things. We should have moved. Instead we sat among the black glass, catching our breath, and the first I knew of it was a shadow falling across Zayn's shoulders, and a smell. Burned cloth. And under it something sharper and stranger, like hair singed in a candle, like ink boiled off a page. I've smelled it in a great many places. It's the smell of a name being burned.

He looked up.

A figure stood on the ridge above us, against the white glare of the Fire.

Grey robes, wrapped close, the hems stirring in the hot wind. A hood. And under the hood, where a face should have been, a mask. Smooth and white and perfectly blank. Two narrow slits for the eyes, and behind them, darkness. Where the edge of the mask met the skin of his neck, the skin was raw and red and puckered, as though the mask had been worn so long it had begun to grow there, and every time someone had tried to lift it the face had come with it.

In each hand the figure held a blade, long and thin and slightly curved, and grey. Not the grey of steel. The grey of the Hollow, dull and dead. They didn't shine in the Fire at all. Looking at them was like trying to remember a dream.

The Unnamed. That's what they call themselves, the men in white masks. They burn their own names in a fire and take numbers instead, and they worship forgetting the way other people worship the Weaver. They hunt the bearer the way the Hounds do. But the Hounds are animals. The Unnamed know exactly what they're doing, and the worst of them do it kindly.

This one, we'd learn later, they called Brother Seven.

He came down the slope.

---

He moved like water running downhill. No wasted step, no sound. Zayn scrambled up with his rusty knife out before the first blade came.

He blocked it. More luck than skill. Steel met grey with a dull sound, like a stone dropped on sand. The second blade was already coming low, at his ribs. He twisted. It missed his side by a breath and opened his sleeve from wrist to elbow.

It didn't touch his skin. It didn't need to.

Zayn staggered. For an instant his face went blank and puzzled, as if he'd walked into a room and forgotten why. I saw him reach for something in his mind and close his hand on nothing.

That's what the grey blades do. They don't cut flesh so much as memory. Even a whisper of one, passing close, takes something with it, and it doesn't leave a hole you can feel. It leaves smooth sand.

He swung at the masked figure, wild and desperate. Brother Seven stepped inside the swing, hooked his ankle, and put him on his back. One grey blade came down and rested against his throat.

Zayn froze.

The feather mark was blazing under his shirt, and there was light in his hands, but it was grey again, flickering, useless. He'd spent everything on the wraith. And grey blades, I should tell you, are the only thing in the world that can cut the Light.

I flew at the figure's legs. A knee caught me in the ribs and sent me tumbling. When I got up, nothing had changed. Zayn on his back. The white mask looking down. The blade against his throat, and a thin line of grey spreading on the skin beneath it, like frost on a window.

Brother Seven didn't move.

---

He didn't press down. He didn't draw back. He stood there with one blade at the boy's throat and the other loose at his side, looking at Zayn through the slits of that blank white mask. I couldn't see his eyes. I don't know what was in them. I'm not sure there was anything left in them to see.

It went on long enough for the hot wind to drop.

And then, under the mask, very softly, he began to hum.

You could barely hear it over the hiss of settling sand. A few notes, low and slow, climbing. A pause. The same notes again, a little higher, like something lifting off the ground. Like wings. It came out of a mask with no mouth, as if from somewhere very far back in him, a room he'd been locked out of.

Zayn's eyes went wide. I don't think he knew why. But something in his face changed, the way the grey woman's eyes had changed in Harrow's End. Something long buried turning over in its sleep.

I knew that song.

I'm not going to tell you who used to sing it, or to whom. Not yet. I sat in the sand with my fur standing up along my spine, and I said nothing. Not then, and not for a long, long time.

Add it to the list. It's getting long, I know. Everything I don't say is a stone in my belly.

The humming stopped.

Brother Seven took the blade away from Zayn's throat.

He stepped back. One step, then another. The blank white mask tilted slightly to one side, as if he were asking himself a question and had forgotten what questions were for.

His hand came up to the edge of the mask. Touched the raw skin there. Pulled, a little, the way you'd worry at a scab.

Then he let go, and turned, and walked away up the ridge, and over it, and was gone.

---

Zayn lay on his back in the sand a long time, staring at the sky.

"He let me go," he said finally.

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

"I don't know," I said.

My tail glowed. True, as far as it went. I knew a great many things about that masked figure. But why he lowered his blade that day, and would again and again, I truly didn't know.

Zayn turned his head and looked at me. He was learning to listen to the shape of what I said.

"Do you know who he is?"

I got up and shook the sand out of my fur, very thoroughly, for a long time.

"It's going to be dark soon," I said. "We should find somewhere to sleep that isn't the middle of a wraith's larder."

He looked at me a moment longer. I saw him decide not to push.

He got up. His sleeve flapped open where the grey blade had cut it. There was a pale mark on his throat where the other had rested, like a line of old frost. He picked up his knife, and started walking.

That night, by a fire of dry thornweed, he sat for a long time with his knees drawn up, frowning into the flames.

"Kiri," he said. "There's something missing."

"Your sense, mostly. Your tent."

"No. In my head." He pressed the heel of his hand against his forehead. "There's a place where something was. I keep going back to it. I don't know what it was. I don't know if it was important." He looked at me, and his eyes were frightened in a way the wraith hadn't made them. "How can I miss something if I don't know what it is?"

I knew what it was. It was the taste of Hesset's tea. Smoke and mint. He'd hated it and loved it every night for years, and now it was gone, and he would never know it had been there. They always start with small things.

"Go to sleep," I said.

He didn't. He lay on his side with his back to the fire, and after a while, very softly, almost without meaning to, he began to hum. A few notes, low and slow, climbing.

He got it wrong. He didn't know the rest.

I lay beside him and didn't correct him, and out in the dark, beyond the edge of the firelight, the sand shifted very slightly, as if something under it had turned over to listen.

---

*End of Chapter Five*
