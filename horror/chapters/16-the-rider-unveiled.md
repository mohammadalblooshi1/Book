# Chapter Sixteen
## The Rider Unveiled

> *Every rider in the dark is someone's father. Pray he is yours.*
> — from a lullaby of the mountain herders, sung only to children who will not sleep

---

Have you ever tried to run across the desert at night with eleven children who have forgotten how to cry?

Don't.

Crying children are loud, but loud is a mercy. Loud means they still want something. These ones ran in silence, in a grey cloud of shaved heads, and when one of them fell he didn't call out. He lay with his face pressed into the sand until somebody bigger picked him up, and then he ran again, and his eyes never changed. Every so often, all together, they hummed. Two notes. No words. The chant from the ship's hold. They didn't know they were doing it.

And Zayn ran in front of them with a broken sword in one hand and Tobin's hand in the other and a half-dead fox stuffed down the front of his shirt, and he didn't stop once.

I could feel his heart through his ribs. It was going like something trying to get out.

Behind us, the Hounds were getting closer.

---

You'll remember the Hollow Hounds. Shadow wolves with ember eyes, and no smell, which is the worst of it. A real wolf stinks. You know it's coming. A Hound smells of nothing at all, the way a cold room smells of nothing, and the first you know of it is the cold.

They track the bearer's light the way a wolf tracks blood. Zayn's light was leaking out of him through the golden cracks on his arms, and on that night, with the Ash pared down to a fingernail, he must have shone like a wound.

They were calling to each other. Six voices. Then nine. One of them wasn't howling. One of them, off to the left, was saying *Zayn* in a small wet voice, over and over, the way a child says a word it has only just learned. The wind had carried his name a long way since that night on the dune above Harrow's End. Things had been practising it.

We came over a ridge, and the far side fell away too steep to run. The children stopped in a huddle at the top. Then, all of them at once, they turned and looked back the way we had come. Calmly. Like people waiting for someone they'd been told would come.

That frightened me more than the Hounds did.

Below us, in the trough we'd just crossed, eyes were opening in the dark. Pairs of them, red as coals, spreading out in a ring.

"Behind me," said Zayn to the children. His voice was very calm. "Stay together. Don't look at their eyes."

He set Tobin's hand into the hand of the oldest boy, who looked at it as if he didn't know whose it was. Zayn lifted the broken sword.

"You can't," I said, from inside his shirt. "Not with half a blade and nothing left. There are too many."

"I know," he said.

My tail glowed. It was true. He knew.

He stood between the dark and the children anyway, and the light came up in him. Not grey. It came up gold, because he had stopped thinking about himself at all, and I have learned to be very afraid of that particular gold. It's the colour they go near the end.

The first Hound leapt.

---

He cut it out of the air.

Half a blade, the way Soren had taught him. The gold ran down his arm into the steel, and when the sword went through the Hound it came apart, and for a moment, before it became ash, I saw what was inside it. Faces. Small grey faces pressed against the inside of the shadow like children against a window. Then sparks, and nothing.

The second one knocked him flat, and I went flying out of his shirt into the sand. Its jaws were an inch from his face. Its breath frosted his eyelashes. He jammed the hilt between its teeth, light burst from his palm, and it shrieked in a voice that was almost a word and was gone.

Then there were four at once.

He fought. He was bleeding from his cheek, the golden cracks were spreading up his arms like a map of rivers, and he was getting slower. I lay in the sand trying to make my legs remember how to be legs, and I watched him get slower.

The fourth Hound came over his shoulder, straight at the children.

The children didn't move. One of the little ones smiled at it.

And a spear came out of the dark.

---

It took the Hound in mid-leap and pinned it, and the Hound burst into ash that fell on the children's upturned faces like grey snow.

Hoofbeats. A pale camel, almost white in the dark, like something that had been dead a while and hadn't been told. On its back, a tall figure in grey wrappings, his face veiled, leaning low from the saddle to wrench his spear free as he passed.

The Veiled Rider. Twice before he'd come out of the dark for Zayn, and never a word, never a face. This was the third time.

He rode through the Hounds like a scythe through dry grass. I'd forgotten how good he was. That's a lie. I hadn't forgotten. I'd hoped to. The spear went left and right, the pale camel spun on the spot, and Hounds burst into ash wherever he went. Zayn fought beside him with gold flaring off the broken sword. The lizard riders came over the far ridge, took one look, and wheeled away, their masks turning to watch us as they went, all at the same angle.

Then it was quiet. Not one of the children was crying. Zayn was on his knees, gasping, grey flakes settling in his hair.

The Rider slid down from his camel. He stood looking at the boy. Then, slowly, as though his hands belonged to someone else, he pulled the veil away.

---

He was a man of perhaps forty with the face of someone left out in the weather for a hundred years. Lean, burned dark, beard cropped short and shot with grey, cheeks fallen in. He had the look of a man who has slept alone in cold places for so long that he's started to forget what other people's voices sound like.

And his eyes were light. Too light for a desert man. Grey-gold, like sand at dawn.

Zayn's eyes.

I watched the understanding arrive in Zayn's face one piece at a time, like the Fire coming up over a dune, and burn everything it touched.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

The man's mouth moved. Nothing came out. He tried again.

"I'm your father," he said.

The little bronze bell on Zayn's belt hung perfectly still. And beside Zayn in the sand, my tail lit up so bright that the man flinched from it as if it were a blade.

---

Zayn didn't move for a long time.

"Three times," he said at last.

"More than that." The man's voice was rough, like a door that hasn't been opened in years and doesn't want to be. "There were nights you never woke."

"You were *there*." Zayn's voice cracked down the middle. "All this time, watching, and you never—" His chest was heaving. "Why did you leave me? In the sand? Why did you *leave* me there?"

The man went down on his knees in front of him.

"To protect you," he said. "They were hunting us both."

The bell stayed silent.

And that was when Zayn broke.

I'd seen him cry before. Not like this. This came from a place under the floor of him, like the catacombs under a city. Fifteen years of it. Every stone. Every *Nameless*. Every night in the patched tent wondering who had thrown him away, and whether they had been right to.

He hit the man once in the chest with his fist. There wasn't any strength in it.

Then he fell forward, and his father caught him.

They knelt in the ash, the boy sobbing into the grey wrappings and the man holding him so tightly I thought something would crack, crying without a sound into his son's pale hair. Up on the ridge the eleven children stood in a row and watched with no expression at all, and hummed their two notes, and I put myself between them and that embrace without quite knowing why.

---

His name was Daruun.

I knew it already. I'd walked beside him once, when he was young and had a feather on his chest and was so afraid of the Crown Dune that he couldn't say its name aloud. He woke screaming most nights that year. He told me he could smell his own hair burning in his sleep. He was the eleventh. The one the murals call *the Runner*. In the City of Glass the glass keeps him as a boy of sixteen, running. It always keeps them as children. Have you noticed that yet? You will.

When he finally looked up from his son, his eyes found me.

"Fox," he said.

"Runner," I said.

Neither of us said anything else. Anything else would have taken all night, and some of it would have been shouting. I'd known who the Rider was from the first spear in the dark, the night of the stones. And I'd said nothing to the boy.

I'm noticing a pattern. I expect you are too. Hold on to it. It gets worse.

---

The mountain caves were a night's walk north, where the desert ended against a wall of red rock like the edge of a grave.

The dunes can't reach the mountains. That's why he went there. The sand rolls up to the cliffs every night and falls back, and it can't write on stone. I went down to the foot of the cliff once, while the others slept, and watched the sand come up against the rock in the dark. It scratched at it. Long, patient strokes. Like something trying to write with its fingernails.

The caves were high up, reached by a path that switched back and forth across the cliff. Inside: a small fire, hides, spears along the walls, and dozens of leather sacks hanging from pegs, each one full of the grey ash of Hollow beasts he'd spent fifteen years hunting. When the fire popped, the sacks shifted on their pegs, all together, as if the ash inside had turned over in its sleep.

The children lay down on the hides in a heap and closed their eyes at the same moment. Zayn counted them twice. Then he sat staring at his own left forearm.

There was writing on it. Scratched into the skin, faint and pink, in the clumsy letters Soren had taught him: *DON'T FORGET HIS EYES.*

"Did you do that?" I said.

"I don't know," said Zayn. "I don't remember doing it." He looked at it a long time. "Whose eyes?"

I didn't answer. I think the grey blades in the ship had already taken the colour of them, and some part of him had felt it going and tried to hold on with a knife, and then forgotten that too.

Daruun sat down by the sleeping children and, very softly, without seeming to notice, began to sing. A low, warm voice. Fire and rising wings.

The children's humming stopped. Every one of them, at once.

Zayn went completely still. It was the voice from the Singing Wells, the man in the water singing over a small boy's hand. He'd been hearing it, somewhere under the floor of himself, his whole life.

---

Daruun told him everything that night. I'll keep it short. Some of it isn't mine to give away.

He had carried the Light before Zayn. He'd been afraid. Not of pain, though there is pain, and he described it, and Zayn listened with his hand pressed to his chest. He was afraid of being gone.

"And something else," he said, looking into the fire. "When you get close to the Crown Dune, you start to hear it. The thing that wants you to burn. It isn't the Hollow, son. It's on the other side. It's hungry too, and it's been hungry a long time." He rubbed his face. "I was sixteen. I didn't want to be eaten. So I ran."

My tail did nothing at all. It didn't glow and it didn't dim. It hung in the firelight like a dead thing, and Daruun looked at it, and then at me, and neither of us said a word.

Because he ran, the Hollow grew. Years later he came down from the mountains and had a family, and was happy for a while. Zayn asked about his mother.

"Another night," said Daruun, and his face closed like a door with something leaning on the other side. "I promise. Not tonight."

The bell didn't ring. He meant it. There wasn't time.

On the night Zayn was born, the Unnamed came out of a storm. They wanted the Light, and they knew it had passed into the newborn. Daruun fought. He got the baby out. But his first son, a boy of five, was dragged into the storm by grey hands, and the last thing Daruun heard was the boy's screaming stop. Not fade. Stop. As if someone had closed a lid.

He searched for weeks. He believed the boy was dead. And he knew that as long as the baby was with him, they would find them both. So he carried his son across the desert to a poor village called Harrow's End, laid him in the sand in the middle of a storm, wrapped in grey cloth, and walked away without looking back, because if he'd looked back he would never have stopped.

"The storm hid my tracks," he said. "That was the desert, protecting you. I had to believe that." He watched the fire. "Every night since, I've wondered whether it was protecting you, or keeping you. Saving you for later. The way you'd save a lamb."

Zayn was quiet for a long time.

"Your son," he said. "The one they took. What was his name?"

Daruun closed his eyes.

"Tamir," he said.

And Zayn told him.

---

The guard in the hold. The phoenix song, hummed in the dark. The light running up the grey blade. A five-year-old boy in a tent, holding a newborn wrapped in grey. The mask cracking down the middle, one pale eye staring out of the crack.

"He's alive," Zayn said. "He's one of them. Brother Seven. He's been hunting me since the start." He swallowed. "And he keeps letting me go. He doesn't know why."

Daruun didn't move. I watched the words go into him and keep going, the way a stone dropped down a well keeps going after you've stopped hearing it.

Then he stood, walked to the mouth of the cave, and went down on his knees on the cold ledge above the desert.

I've heard a great many terrible sounds. The sound Daruun made on that ledge was worse than any of them, because it went on, and then it turned into something that wasn't a sound at all, just his whole body heaving in silence. Fifteen years. His son alive the whole time, his name burned, his face taken, taught to steal other children. And his father hiding in a cave with sacks of ash, telling himself stories.

Zayn didn't go to him. Forgiveness doesn't work like that. Not quickly.

He turned to me.

"Did you know?"

I could have said nothing. I'd had a great deal of practice.

"Yes," I said.

My tail glowed. Something went across his face that I am still, in the long nights, trying to forget.

"You knew he was my *brother*."

"Yes."

He turned away and didn't speak again that night.

I told him the truth about that one. There was another truth I was still sitting on, a heavier one, with blood under it. One out of two. You decide whether that counts.

---

In the morning, we gave the children away.

In a valley at the foot of the cliffs, beside a spring, the Tamarisk clan of nomad herders were camped, as they were every year when the Ash went thin. Their headman was broad and grey-bearded, with a voice like gravel. He looked at the eleven shaved heads for a long time.

"Unnamed?" he said.

"Not anymore," said Zayn.

The children were standing in a row, the way they'd been taught, humming. The headman's wife came out of the nearest tent, saw them, and went back in.

"We'll keep them," he said at last. "Until someone comes for them, or the grey lifts." He lowered his voice. "We'll keep a lamp lit where they sleep. And we'll tie their ankles at night, the first while. The ones who come out of the ship, sometimes they walk."

Tobin held on to Zayn's waist a long time.

"Will you find Bram?" he said into Zayn's shirt.

Zayn couldn't lie. He didn't know.

"If I see him, I'll tell him where you are. I'll tell him you were brave."

That was true. Tobin let go.

The smallest child, a boy of five, tugged Zayn's sleeve. "I don't know my name," he whispered. "They burned it. I watched. It smelled like hair."

Zayn knelt in front of him.

"Then you'll find it," he said. "Or you'll make a new one. I did."

The boy looked at him with no expression at all. Then, very slowly, as if remembering how, he nodded.

My tail glowed all the way back up the cliff.

---

That evening, Daruun gave him two things.

The first was a pendant on a leather cord: a knot of old bronze, a loop that turned back through itself and never ended. The infinity knot.

"Every bearer has worn one," said Daruun. "I took it off the night I ran. It's yours."

Zayn put it on. It lay below the feather mark, and it was warm, the way a stone is warm when someone has been holding it. Nobody had been holding it.

The second was a small, battered compass in a dented brass case.

"It doesn't point north," said Daruun. "It points toward whatever you're running from. For fifteen years it pointed at the Crown Dune. I stopped opening it. I could feel it pointing through the case, like a finger in my back."

Zayn opened it. The needle spun, wobbled, and settled. Not on the desert. Not on the far place where the highest sand in the world waited under the full Ash.

It pointed at the broken sword, lying on the hides beside the fire.

---

He'd carried that sword every step since the full Ash and never once really looked at it, because looking at it meant looking at a body in the morning sand, grey and cracked with gold.

He turned it over now. Half a blade, notched and dull. A plain leather grip. A round bronze pommel.

The pommel was loose. Gritty, as if it had been unscrewed and screwed back in a hurry, in the dark, by someone with shaking hands. He twisted it, and twisted again, and it came away, and the hilt was hollow.

Inside was a roll of paper, tied with thread.

A broken seal of grey wax, pressed with a little curling mark we both knew, because we had watched an old man press that same seal every evening in Thornwell by the lamp, with the falcon on its perch not blinking.

Orrin's seal.

Zayn read it himself. Slowly. Out loud, the way Soren had taught him, his finger under each word.

"*To the Brothers,*" he read. "*On the Night of Embers, when every lantern in Veimore is lit, the king will die. Be ready on the palace stairs. When he falls, put out the lanterns. Then the city. The eldest son is gone. The youngest will come to me. Speak no names.*"

His finger stopped at the bottom.

"*The Voice.*"

---

The fire cracked. On their pegs, the sacks of ash stirred. Down at the foot of the cliff, the sand went on scratching.

"He knew," Zayn said at last. "Soren. He was carrying this. Why didn't he tell me?"

"Perhaps he meant to," said Daruun. "Perhaps he ran out of time."

My tail stayed exactly as it was. I kept my mouth shut and looked at the fire. Let me keep it a little longer. It's the only thing I have that's heavier than I am.

"Soren taught me to read," Zayn said.

It came out of him like something breaking open. As if every evening at the Wells, every backwards S and falling-over R, every time Soren laughed and made him try again, had been leading to this cave and this paper.

As if it had all been for something.

I wanted to believe that with him. It frightened me too. Because when everything in a life starts lining up to lead somewhere, little one, you should ask who drew the line.

Later I saw him unwrap Hesset's broken golden feather. The break had nearly closed. It was mending as he walked toward the end, a little more every day, like a wound closing over a splinter. I looked at it and hated it.

He slid the letter back into the hilt and screwed the pommel tight. Then he stood.

"I'm going to Veimore," he said.

Daruun was on his feet. "Every gate in that city is waiting for the Prince-Killer. You'll walk straight into Orrin's hands."

"Probably," said Zayn.

The bell didn't ring.

"The king has to see this. Soren's little brother is in that palace. And Soren died carrying it." He picked up the sword. "I promised him I'd never give up."

Daruun looked at him a long moment. Then he put a hand on his son's shoulder.

Zayn let it stay. He didn't lean into it. He didn't call him father. But he didn't step away.

It's not nothing. In the desert, you learn to count the things that are not nothing. You count them the way the people of Veimore count their lantern oil: by the drop, knowing exactly how little is left.

---

*End of Chapter Sixteen*
