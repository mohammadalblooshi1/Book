# Chapter Thirteen
## Nameless Again

> *The loneliest grave is the one no one visits. The second loneliest is the one that visits you.*
> — a saying of the desert burial-keepers

---

For four days, he didn't say a word.

You'd think that would be a relief to me. I'd been begging for silence since the day I met him. Now I had all of it, and I'd have traded every scorpion between here and the edge of the world to hear him tell me to shut up.

He walked. That was all. He walked while the sand was still cold, and at noon when the Fire hammered down so hard the air shook like a struck bell, and at night under an Ash already beginning to thin, as if something had taken a bite out of it. He didn't choose a direction. He let the dunes choose. When a slope rose in front of him, he climbed it. When a valley opened, he went down. If the Weaver was writing the sand that week, it was writing for a boy who had stopped reading.

Soren's sword was tied across his back, wrapped in his scarf. It knocked against his spine with every step, and every time it did he flinched, and every time he flinched he kept walking. It had a rhythm to it after a while. Like a knock on a door. Like someone behind him, tapping his shoulder, wanting to be let back in.

He hadn't given Soren to the dunes. He couldn't make his hands do it. He'd sat until the wind began the work for him, and then he'd walked away and left the sand to finish, with the word the travellers had shouted chasing him the way stones had once chased him out of Harrow's End.

*Prince-Killer.*

He'd been called a lot of things. Nameless. Sandborn. Monster. Now he had a new one, and the worst part, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, was that he couldn't be sure it was wrong.

I followed a dune behind. I'd have walked closer, but I wasn't walking well.

---

You remember the storm. The pillar, and what I became to lift it, and how his light caught me full in my Hollow half and went down into it looking for the bottom.

There was a patch on my left side, the silver side, where the fur had come away. What was underneath wasn't fox. It was dark and faintly smoking, like a coal dropped in water that hadn't decided whether to go out. Sometimes, at night, when it was very quiet, I could hear it whispering. Not to me. To itself. The same three words, over and over, in a voice I knew.

It hadn't healed. I'd stopped expecting it to.

The night of the full Ash had made it worse. I'll say that much and no more.

So I limped. And he walked. And neither of us talked about it.

---

He still did the things that kept him alive. Fifteen years of scraping by don't leave you just because your heart has.

On the second day he found water. He licked his finger and held it to the wind, walked straight down to a hollow where camel-thorn grew, and dug until the sand went dark and damp, and drank.

He'd taught Soren how to do that. I watched him remember it. He sat back on his heels with wet sand on his fingers and his face went completely still, the way a well goes still after something has dropped to the bottom of it and you're waiting to hear it land.

You never do hear it land, in a deep enough well.

He got up and kept walking.

At night he hardly slept. He sat at the top of whatever dune he'd stopped on, and with one finger he wrote in the sand.

The same word, every night. Five letters, slow and careful, the way you write a thing you've only just learned, tongue between his teeth. Soren had made him practise it fifty times by the fire, laughing at how he wrote the S backwards. *It's my name, Zayn. Have some respect.* He didn't write it backwards anymore.

S. O. R. E. N.

And every morning the dunes had moved in the night, and the word was gone.

That's the trouble with being given to the desert. There's no stone. There's no place. The sand takes you and keeps moving, and the people who loved you have to carry the grave around inside them, because there's nowhere else to put it. After a while the grave starts to feel like it's carrying you.

---

On the third morning, the word was still there.

Not where he'd written it. He'd written it on the crest, where he sat. This one was at the bottom of the slope, fifty paces down, in a smooth bowl of sand where the wind hadn't reached. Five letters. The S the right way round. Pressed in deep with one finger, the way he pressed.

His handwriting. I'd know it anywhere. So would he.

He stood over it a long time.

"Did you write this?" he said. Not to me, really. To the air.

I hadn't. I can't write; I have paws. He knew that. He'd had his back to that slope all night and I'd been beside him, and I'd have sworn he never moved.

But there were his own footprints, going down to it from where we'd slept, and none coming back.

He didn't say anything. He rubbed it out with his foot, carefully, the whole word, until the sand was smooth. Then he looked at his finger for a while, turning it over, as if it belonged to somebody else.

That night he didn't write anything at all.

---

On the third night he tried to lie.

He was sitting with his knees up and the sword across his lap, and he said, so quietly I nearly missed it, "It wasn't my fault."

His face twisted. His hand flew to his chest. The feather mark flared under his shirt, hot and sharp, and he doubled over and pressed his palm against it until the light went out and there was a smell in the air of scorched cloth.

He couldn't even lie to himself. Not for comfort. Not once.

Most people get to tell themselves small kind lies in the dark. *There was nothing I could have done. He didn't suffer. It was quick.* Lies like that are how people get up in the morning. Zayn never got any. Every night he went down into the dark with nothing but the truth, and the truth was this: they had argued under the full Ash, and he had walked away, and he had watched the fire go out and called it nothing.

"The sand remembers," he whispered afterwards. The old words.

The mark didn't burn. It wasn't a lie.

But I don't think he meant it the way he used to. I think he'd started to hear it the other way round. Not *the sand will remember you*, like a blessing. *The sand remembers*, like a threat. The sand remembers what you did.

I limped up the slope and sat down a little way off.

"It wasn't your fault," I said.

My tail lit up, bright and steady as a lamp. I wanted him to see it more than I've wanted most things.

He didn't turn his head.

"Don't," he said.

It was the first word he'd spoken in three days. Hoarse, as if his voice had rusted shut.

"It's true," I said. "Look at my tail. Look at it."

"It glowed at the City of Glass too," he said. "Right up until it didn't."

I had nothing for that. I'd told him one lie in fifteen years, one small, merciful lie, and it had cost me every true thing I'd ever say to him afterwards. Remember that, if you ever think a lie is cheap. It's the most expensive thing there is. It buys you nothing and it costs you everything after.

He was quiet a while. Then, without looking at me: "Where were you?"

The wind moved over the dune.

"That night," he said. "After I walked away. You weren't with him. You weren't with me. Where were you?"

I opened my mouth.

I'm not going to tell you what I nearly said. I'm not going to tell you where I was, either. Not yet. There's a night in this story I haven't told you about, and I'm not ready, and it's my story, so I get to choose. That's the only power a storyteller has. The power to make you wait in the dark.

I closed my mouth.

He nodded slowly, as though I'd answered. Then he lay down with his back to me and the broken sword in his arms, and I sat beside him until the Embers went pale, and the burned place in my side whispered its three words to itself, and neither of us said anything at all.

---

There was a twist of waxed paper at the bottom of his bag. Three dates rolled in honey, the last of the four, gone sticky and dark, carried all the way from Harrow's End.

On the fourth evening he took them out and held them in his palm for a long time.

Then he did something I didn't understand until later. He put one to his lips, just touched it there, and closed his eyes, and I watched his face go hunting for something. Searching, the way a tongue searches for a missing tooth.

He was trying to remember what honey tasted like. The grey blade had taken it in the storm, and it had come back, mostly. *Mostly*. He was checking.

I don't know what he found. He wrapped them up again and put them back without eating any. Eating them would have meant saying that this was as bad as it got, and some small stubborn part of him still wasn't ready to promise that.

Or else he was afraid to taste them and find nothing there at all.

I'm not sure, even now, which. I'm not sure it was hope, either way.

---

The fourth night was the one I'd been afraid of.

He stopped at the bottom of a long slope instead of the top. In fifteen years he had always slept high, where he could see what was coming. Now he walked down into the cold trough between two dunes, where the wind poured sand down the slip-face in hissing curtains, like water, like something being poured over a body.

And he lay down.

On his back, sword on his chest, arms folded over it, looking up at the Embers. He let the sand come.

It came softly. A trickle over his feet. A drift against his side. The dunes were moving the way they moved every night, and he had lain down in the path of one, and he was very still, and he was watching the Embers with an expression I'd seen before on other faces, a long time ago. Relief. That's the word. It's the most frightening expression a living face can make.

"Zayn," I said.

He didn't answer.

"Zayn. Get up."

The sand was over his ankles now. His knees. It was spilling into the folds of his shirt, into the hollow of his throat.

The dead are given to the dunes. That's what desert people do. He hadn't stayed to see the sand close over Soren. So he was giving them the next best thing.

"*Get up.*" I got my teeth in his sleeve and pulled with everything I had, which that night was less than a kitten has. My burned side split and smoked and I yelped, and his arm didn't move.

I could have changed. You're thinking it. I was thinking it. I could have let the dark half out and dug him free in three strokes.

He'd seen that half once, and been so afraid of it that his light had nearly burned it out of the world. I didn't know what it would do to a boy in his state to wake with that thing's breath on his face and all its voices in his ears. I thought it might be the thing that finished him.

So I stood there in the sliding sand, shaking, a small burned fox shouting at a boy who wouldn't listen, while the desert buried him an inch at a time.

And the light came.

---

It wasn't his light. That's what I knew first.

His light starts at the mark and pours outward. This came from the slope beside him, from nowhere at all. It was soft and gold, and it didn't burn. It glowed the way a lamp glows in a window when someone is waiting up for you.

And it was cold. That's what nobody puts in the songs. The sand around it frosted. I watched the frost spread out from it in fine white feathers, and my breath came out of me in a cloud.

I didn't see anyone in it. I want to be honest about that. I only saw the light on Zayn's face, and his eyes open wide, and his lips move.

He told me later what he saw. I'll tell it the way he did.

Soren was sitting on the slope beside him. Cross-legged, elbows on his knees, the way he sat by the fire when he was about to say something annoying. The scar along his jaw. His hair coming loose. Light came through the edges of him, like a cloth hung in front of the Fire.

He was grey. Zayn told me that, and then he wouldn't tell me any more about it for a long time. Grey, like the hollow that morning. And the golden cracks were still in him, running across his face and down his throat, and they were glowing again. Brighter than they'd been. As if the thing that had been trying to get out of them had nearly made it.

The sand didn't shift under him. Not a grain. He had no weight at all.

His hand was bare. No ring. Zayn noticed that. He couldn't stop noticing it.

"You look terrible," said Soren. "That's weird."

His mouth moved a moment before the words came. Just a moment. Like someone speaking to you from the other side of a wide valley.

Zayn made a sound. It wasn't a word.

"You're lying in a hole," said Soren. "Letting a dune sit on you. Do you know how stupid that looks from where I am? Very. It looks very stupid."

"I left you," Zayn whispered.

Soren stopped smiling.

"Yeah," he said. "You did."

There was no anger in it. That was the worst part, Zayn told me. He said it the way you say *the sand is hot*. A plain fact laid down between them, like a stone on a grave.

"I left you," Zayn said again, "and you died."

"I know. I was there." Soren leaned forward. He laid his hand on Zayn's shoulder, and it was *warm*. Zayn swore to me it was warm, warm as a living hand, warm as the Fire on a cold morning, though the frost was spreading out from him across the sand the whole time. "Get up anyway."

"Who did it?" Zayn said. "Soren. Who—"

Soren's face went still. He opened his mouth, and for a moment nothing came out of it but the smell of cold ash, and his eyes went somewhere past Zayn's shoulder, into the dark, and stayed there. As if someone were standing there. As if he'd seen them before.

Then he looked back.

"I can't," he said, and for the first time he sounded young, and frightened, and seventeen. "I don't think I'm allowed. I think there's a lot I'm not allowed." His grip tightened. "Get up, Zayn."

"I can't."

"Yes, you can. You're just choosing not to. It's different." His eyes were very bright, and very pale, and they didn't blink. Not once, the whole time. "Never give up, Zayn. You promised."

Zayn shut his eyes.

He had. Under the full Ash, on the last night, Soren had said *Promise me you'll never give up*, and he had said *I promise*. And he could not lie. Not even lying in the sand with a dune on his chest.

He had promised. So it was true. So he had to make it true.

When he opened his eyes, the slope was dark and empty, and there was only a fox standing knee-deep in the sand beside him, with a tail blazing so bright it hurt to look at.

I hadn't seen anything. I'll swear to that.

But my tail had been burning gold the whole time, and it doesn't do that for dreams.

And the frost was still there on the slope, in the shape of someone sitting cross-legged. It stayed there until the Fire came up and took it.

---

He got up.

He dug himself out with his hands, the way he'd once dug himself out of collapsed rooms in the Old Ruins, and climbed the slope on hands and knees, dragging the sword.

Then he looked at me. Actually looked, for the first time in four days.

"Did you see him?" he said.

"No," I said.

My tail stayed bright. He watched it.

"But you believe me."

"Yes," I said. It stayed bright.

He looked at it a long moment. "He was warm," he said. "He was warm, Kiri. And everything around him was freezing." He swallowed. "Is that how it's supposed to be?"

I've walked with eleven bearers. I've seen a great many things come back to the people who loved them. Some of them were what they looked like.

"I don't know," I said.

That was true too. I wish it hadn't been.

He looked away, out over the moving dunes. But he didn't turn his back on me. That night, for the first time since the full Ash, he slept with me curled against his side. He slept with one hand on the sword and the other on my back, and twice in the night I felt him wake and look at the slope where the frost had been.

It's not nothing. In the desert, you learn to count the things that are not nothing. You learn to count them very carefully, because there aren't many.

---

This next part is where I went wrong.

In the morning he walked on, and I couldn't keep up.

The dune had cost me more than I'd thought. By the time the Fire was two hands above the horizon I'd folded up in the shade of a rock with my legs not listening. He was three dunes ahead. Then four. I told myself I'd catch up when the heat broke.

Which is why I was a long way off, lying on a ridge, when the lanterns came.

You don't see lanterns in daylight. That's what made me lift my head. A little string of them, swaying over the eastern dunes: camels, six or seven, with lanterns hung from their saddles still burning in the full morning Fire, pale little flames you could hardly see, the way they burn on the walls of Veimore day and night because someone is afraid of what happens if they ever go out. Rich camels. Red tassels. Water skins so fat they sloshed.

They came straight to him. Not near him. *To* him, as if they'd known exactly which dune a tired boy would be standing on.

Fox ears are very good. Fox legs, that morning, were not. I dragged myself to the crest of the ridge and lay flat and listened.

The lead camel knelt. A man climbed down.

He was old, his hair silver and combed long over a robe the deep blue of the sky just after the Fire sets. His face was soft and kind and lined, someone's grandfather. His voice was low and gentle, the kind that makes you lean in.

And his eyes were grey.

Faded grey, flat and pale, like cloth left in the Fire for a hundred years. Like a Husk's eyes, before the white sets in.

The fur along my spine stood straight up, and the burned place in my side stopped whispering. All at once. Just went silent, the way insects go silent when something walks past in the dark.

I couldn't have told you why. That's not true. I could have. I'm not going to. Let's leave it there.

Behind the old man, another figure climbed down: the biggest man I had ever seen. Shaved head. Arms like the trunks of date palms. A face that didn't look as though it had ever made an expression it meant.

"Take the boy's bag," the old man said softly. "Can't you see he can barely stand?"

The giant lifted the bag gently from Zayn's shoulder and carried it back to the baggage camel and tied it there, among the water skins.

Remember that. I didn't. I was watching faces. I should have been watching the bag.

---

"You are the boy who glows," the old man said.

Zayn didn't answer. His hand had gone to the hilt of the broken sword on his back.

"My name is Orrin," said the old man. "I am the vizier of Veimore. I serve King Absalon." A pause. "You'll have heard my name. From him."

Zayn's knuckles went white.

He had. Soren had told him the whole story by the fire: the stable boy he'd hidden, the broken sword, and the soft old voice of the vizier calling it treason, calling it dark magic, until his father believed it.

"Yes," said Orrin. "I named him traitor." He bowed his silver head. "I was wrong. I've spent a turn of the Ash crossing this desert to tell him so. And I came too late."

He reached into his robe and held something out on his open palm, and I saw Zayn's whole body go still.

A ring. Heavy gold, worn smooth, the crest of Veimore cut into its face.

Soren's ring. The one he never took off.

"He gave me this," Orrin said gently.

On the ridge, my tail flickered.

I *think* it dimmed. I'm nearly sure. But the Fire was bright across the sand, and a tail is a hard thing to read in daylight, and I was in pain, and afraid. All of that is true. None of it is an excuse. I've had a very long time to try to make it one.

"When?" said Zayn. His voice cracked on it.

"On the night of the full Ash," said Orrin. "I found him at last. By his little fire. We spoke. He forgave me, I think, or began to. And he gave me his ring and said, *If anything happens to me, find the boy who glows. Look after him.*" The old man's voice shook, very slightly, in exactly the right place. "I didn't understand what he meant. Not until morning."

There was no bell. Hesset's bell was tied to a camel twenty paces away, in a patched bag, among the water skins, and it didn't make a sound. I've thought about that silence a great deal. Whether it rang inside the bag and the water skins swallowed it. Whether a bell can be smothered the way a voice can.

Zayn reached out, slowly, the way you reach for something that might vanish. Orrin let him take the ring. It was warm from the old man's robe. Zayn closed his fingers over it, and his face crumpled.

He'd last felt that gold on a living hand.

"Did you kill him?" Orrin asked quietly. Almost kindly. "They're saying you did."

Zayn's eyes were streaming. He could have said *no*, and it would have been true.

But the boy could not lie, and in his heart, the truth was something else.

"I left him," he said.

Orrin nodded slowly. "I know," he said. "So did I, once, a long time ago. Someone I loved." His grey eyes were wet. "Everyone leaves someone, child. It's what we do afterwards that matters."

He held out his hand. After a moment, Zayn gave the ring back. Orrin tucked it inside his robe, carefully, close to his heart.

"It should go to his father," he said. "And his brother. I'll see that it does. But you need food, and shade, and sleep. There's an oasis town two days east. I have a house there. Come with me." A pause, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck. "And when you're stronger, I'll tell you about your father."

Zayn's head came up.

"I knew him," said Orrin. "Long ago. He carried what you carry. I can tell you who he was. If you want to know."

---

By the time I got there, Zayn was already climbing onto a camel.

I came down the slope in a sort of controlled fall, my side smoking. Orrin's soft grey eyes found me, and he smiled.

"Ah," he said. "The fox. Of course."

There was no surprise in it at all. He looked at the burned place on my side, the dark showing through, and he looked at it the way you'd look at a door you already had the key to.

I stood at the camel's feet with my legs shaking and my fur on end and looked up at the boy. He was holding the reins in one hand. His other hand was pressed flat to his chest, over the mark.

I could have said a great many things. I've had a long time to make a list.

I said, "I don't like him."

Four words. My tail glowed. They were perfectly true.

Zayn looked down at me. He looked tired beyond anything a boy of fifteen should be. "You don't like anyone," he said.

That was true too.

"And you told me *yes* once," he said, more quietly. "Remember?"

He turned the camel's head east and followed the old man's lanterns over the dune.

I stood there in the sand a long moment. Then I followed. What else was I going to do? I'd been following him for fifteen years.

The giant walked at the back of the line. He didn't look round once. But at the top of the dune he stopped, and he waited, very patiently, until I'd passed him, so that he was behind me all the way.

I didn't know then what it would cost, those four small, cowardly, true words, and all the words I didn't say after them. I know now. I'll tell you. Not yet.

The burned place in my side didn't whisper again all that day. Not once.

It was listening.

And above us the Ash was thinning, night by night, like a face turning slowly away, and I had started counting again.

*End of Chapter Thirteen*
