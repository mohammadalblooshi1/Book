# Chapter Four
## The Bell and the Feather

> *The bell rings for the liar, and the liar is often you. It does not ring for silence. Nothing does.*
> — words stamped around the rim of an old healer's bell

---

His tent was gone.

Not burned. The people of Harrow's End were too frightened that night even for fire. Fire makes light, and light, they had just learned, brought things out of the dark.

So they had torn it down. Somebody had pulled up the stakes and slashed the patched walls in nine colours and trampled the whole thing flat into the sand, the way you'd stamp on a scorpion you weren't brave enough to pick up. His blanket and water skin were gone. His jar of tokens had been smashed, and the tokens taken. Fear doesn't stop people from being practical.

And on the flattened cloth, someone had drawn a circle in ash, with a line through it. The sign the grandmothers scratch over the doors of houses where someone has died of the coughing sickness. *Keep out. Whatever was here is catching.*

Zayn stood looking at it for a while in the dark.

"Well," I said. "At least you won't have to pack. And look, they've labelled it for you. Very thoughtful."

He didn't laugh. He knelt in the wreckage and began to dig through it with his hands, quietly, methodically, the way he dug through the ruins. A scavenger to the last.

He found the twist of waxed paper first. Four dates rolled in honey, the ones he'd been saving. Somebody had stepped on them. They were squashed flat and full of sand. He brushed them off carefully and put them inside his shirt with Auntie Sabba's bread.

Then he found the cloth.

It was buried under the collapsed tent pole: a square of grey fabric, softer than anything else he owned, woven with small strange marks no weaver in Harrow's End had ever been able to name. The desert had wrapped him in it fifteen years ago. He'd slept with it under his head every night of his life.

He held it against his face. Then he went still.

"It smells of smoke," he said.

It always had. He'd simply never noticed, the way you never notice the smell of your own house. Burned cloth, very old, very faint. I didn't tell him what kind of smoke. I didn't tell him that I'd smelled it on the night the desert left him, rolling across the dunes from the place where his family had been, along with another smell, the one that clings to the robes of men who burn their own names.

He folded the cloth small and tucked it away with the rest.

"That's everything," he said.

A knife, a cloth, some bread, four ruined dates. Fifteen years, and that was everything.

---

Hesset was waiting for us.

Of course he was. The lamp was still burning when we reached his tent, the bells turning slowly on their threads. There was tea already poured, two cups. Hesset sat cross-legged on his worn rug, his milky eyes fixed on nothing, his white beard falling over his folded hands.

"Sit," he said, before Zayn had even ducked through the doorway. "You're bleeding on my herbs."

Zayn didn't sit.

He stood in the doorway with the lamplight on his face. The golden cracks along his jaw had faded to a faint shimmer. His shirt was torn at the back and stiff with blood. He was shaking. Not from cold.

He was looking at the lamp. At the little flame in it. He couldn't seem to stop looking at it, and he couldn't seem to bear it, and every time it fluttered, his hand jerked toward his chest.

"You knew," he said.

Hesset turned his face toward the sound. For a long moment he said nothing.

Then he said, "Sit down, child. Let me see to your back. And then you can shout at me, and I promise to listen to every word."

The bells were silent. Every word of it was true.

---

He sat, in the end. I think he was too tired not to.

Hesset's hands were old and dry and very gentle. He peeled the torn shirt away and washed the wound with water from a clay bowl, then packed it with a paste that smelled of bitterleaf and smoke. Zayn hissed through his teeth. He didn't cry out.

"Close to the old one," Hesset murmured, his fingers moving over the pale scar under Zayn's left shoulder blade. "The sand does like to write in the same place twice."

He turned his blind face toward the corner where I sat with my tail curled over my paws.

"And you," he said. "You've finally decided to talk, I hear."

"News travels fast," I said.

"Garrem screams very loudly." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's been a long time, fox."

"Not long enough."

Zayn twisted round to stare at us. "You *know* each other?"

Hesset and I said nothing at the same moment, which was its own kind of answer.

I'll tell you this much, since you're listening and he wasn't: yes. We knew each other. We had known each other for a very long time, longer than the boy could have guessed. The last time Hesset and I had stood in the same place, he'd had eyes, and he'd been younger than Zayn, and he'd been running. He ran so fast that when he finally turned around, the last thing he ever saw was the place he was running from, white and enormous under a white Ash. Then the dark came down over him and never lifted.

That's Hesset's story, and he never told it to the boy. So I won't tell the rest of it to you. Not yet.

"Drink your tea," said Hesset, and pressed the cup into Zayn's hands.

Zayn drank it. Smoke and mint, bitter as always. He hated it as always. It was the first thing all night that felt like home.

Remember that. I asked you to, before.

---

"What am I?" he said at last.

The tea was finished. The lamp had burned lower. Outside, the wind was whispering along the walls of the tent, testing the seams.

"You know the story," said Hesset.

"The Phoenix-Born." Zayn's voice was flat. "The Weaver puts a piece of its light inside a child. The child carries it to the highest dune in the world and sets it free and drives the darkness away."

"Yes."

"That's a story. It's just a story you tell."

Hesset said nothing.

"I'm not one of them," said Zayn. "I'm not—"

He stopped. His face twisted. His hand went to the feather mark and pressed hard, and I saw him bite down on the inside of his cheek until it bled.

It was burning.

He'd been about to say *I'm not one of the Phoenix-Born.* He'd wanted it to be true so badly he'd nearly said it. And the mark had caught the words before they reached his lips and set them alight, and for a moment the smell of scorched skin filled the little tent, sharp and unmistakable, and the bells all trembled on their threads without ringing.

He sat with his eyes squeezed shut, breathing through his nose, waiting for the pain to fade. When he opened them, there was a thin brown mark on his shirt, over his heart, where the fabric had singed.

"Ah," said Hesset softly. "Yes. That's the trouble with you, isn't it? You can't even lie to yourself."

"I saw it," Zayn said. "When I healed Tobin. I saw myself on a dune. Burning." He swallowed. "Is that the story too?"

Hesset's hands went very still in his lap.

"What did you feel?" he asked. Not *what did you see*. *What did you feel.*

"Everything," said Zayn.

The old man didn't answer. He turned his blind face away toward the wall, and for a moment I thought he looked like someone listening to a sound very far off, a sound he had spent three hundred years trying not to hear.

---

Hesset rose. It took him a while. His knees cracked like dry sticks. He felt his way past the hanging bitterleaf to an old wooden chest in the far corner. Zayn had never once seen it open.

Hesset opened it now. I caught a glimpse inside before his body blocked it. Other bundles. Small ones, wrapped in old cloth, laid in a row like loaves. A child's sandal, burned black along one side. I looked away. Some things I've had a lifetime to stop looking at, and I'm still not good at it.

Hesset brought out a bundle wrapped in oiled leather.

"When the desert left you on the edge of this village," he said, "you were wrapped in that grey cloth you carry. And tucked inside it, against your chest, was this. Oszkar's father would have sold it. So I took it before anyone saw, and I've kept it for fifteen years. Open it."

Zayn opened it.

It was a feather.

Gold, or something like gold, as long as his hand and so fine you could see the lamplight through its edges. Every barb was perfect. But it was broken, snapped clean across the middle, the two halves clinging together by a few bent barbs. Along one side the barbs were twisted and crushed, as if a hand had closed on it and tried to wring it in half. At the break, the gold had gone dark. Not tarnish. Grey, the dead flat grey of the lane outside Bram's house.

And it was warm.

Zayn held it in both palms and felt the warmth soak into his skin. It was the same warmth as the mark on his chest. The same shape. The same fine lines. And as he held it, very faintly, it beat. Once. Twice. Out of time with his heart, as if it were keeping time with something else.

"It's broken," he said finally.

"Yes," said Hesset.

"Can it be fixed?"

"Some things mend themselves," the old man said, "when they're ready."

True. The bells didn't stir. My tail stayed bright.

I'll tell you now, because it doesn't spoil anything: it did mend. Slowly. One barb at a time. Every time the boy learned something true about himself, something hard and painful, the feather grew a little more whole. I watched it the whole way. I've watched eleven of them, and they all mend, and they're always whole on the same night, under the same Ash.

A feather is mended so it can fly. That's all I'll say. Every time it healed, I hated it a little more.

---

There was a second gift.

Hesset reached up without looking and unhooked the smallest bell from its thread, no bigger than the tip of Zayn's thumb, bronze worn smooth with age, strung on braided leather.

"This one," Hesset said, "is for you."

He tied it around Zayn's neck himself, old fingers fumbling at the knot.

"You cannot lie. You know that by now. But the rest of the world can, and the rest of the world will. This bell will ring when someone lies to you. Not loudly. Not always in time. But it will ring."

Zayn touched it. It lay against his chest just above the feather mark, cold against the heat of his skin.

"How do I know it works?"

Hesset lifted his cup and smiled. "My tea," he said, "is the finest in the whole desert."

The bell rang.

A small, clear sound, like a single drop of water falling into a silver cup. And at the same moment my tail dimmed, the glow going out of it like a breath blown across a lamp, then coming slowly back.

Zayn looked at the bell. Then at my tail. Then at me.

"Your tail," he said slowly. "When you lie, it…"

"Yes, yes. Glows for the truth, goes dark for a lie. Anybody's lie, not just mine. Very embarrassing. Imagine trying to play cards." I curled the offending thing tighter. "Now you've got two ways of knowing when somebody's lying to you. Most people don't get one."

"And when *you* lie?"

"I don't," I said.

My tail glowed.

He was still staring at it. I could see him going back over everything I'd said that night, word by word, trying to remember whether it had glowed.

It had. All of it.

Later, that would matter more than either of us knew. A fox who never lies only has to do it once.

---

"Would you die for them?" Hesset asked.

He said it quietly. He was turning his empty cup slowly between his palms. He might have been asking whether Zayn wanted more tea.

Zayn looked up. "What?"

"The people of this village. Oszkar, who called you Sandborn. Garrem, who cheated you every week of your life. The children who threw stones when you were six, and the men who threw them tonight. Bram." A pause. "Would you die for them?"

The tent was very quiet. Even the wind outside had stopped, as though it, too, were waiting for the answer.

Zayn opened his mouth.

I'll always see it. He opened his mouth to say *yes*, because it was what a hero would say, what the Phoenix-Born said in every story Hesset had ever told him. The feather burned before the word was out. He flinched, and the singed mark on his shirt darkened.

He closed his mouth, and opened it again, to say *no*. Because they had stoned him. Because they had called him monster and drawn the death-sign on his tent. Because he owed them nothing at all.

That burned too.

And between the two burnings, for just an instant, the lamp flame filled his eyes, and I saw him flinch from it as if it had reached for him. The vision came uninvited. Only a flicker. The white Ash. The heat in his mouth. He made a small sound, like a child woken from a nightmare who doesn't yet know he's awake.

He sat with his hand pressed hard against his chest, eyes wet, mouth half open. Neither word would come.

"I don't know," he whispered finally.

My tail glowed. The bell was silent.

Hesset nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd expected, and dreaded.

"Good," he said. "That's honest. Hold on to it. It won't be your answer forever."

"Why are you asking me that?"

Hesset didn't answer. He turned the empty cup in his hands, round and round, round and round, the way the dunes turn, the way the Ash turns. The way everything out here comes back to where it started.

---

It was almost dawn when Zayn asked the question that mattered.

"Where do I go?"

"The City of Glass," said Hesset.

Zayn blinked. "That's a story too."

"Most things are, until you walk into them. It lies deep in the desert, farther than anyone from Harrow's End has ever gone. It's the place where the first bearer said no to the Light, and the sand around him melted into glass." His blind eyes moved, as though following something a long way off. "The walls there remember. Everything you need to know about what you are is written on them. The faces of the ones who came before you. Older than me. Older than the fox, almost."

"Steady on," I said.

"How do I find it?"

"You don't. You walk, and the dunes find it for you. They'll open a way if you're meant to reach it."

Zayn turned the broken feather over in his hands. "Will you come with me?"

Something passed across the old man's face. Quick, gone almost before I saw it. But I've known Hesset a long time, and I knew what it was. Shame. And under the shame, older and worse, fear.

"I don't leave the edge of the desert," he said. "I can't. The desert and I have an agreement. I made it a long time ago, and I'm still paying for it."

True. The bell didn't stir.

Zayn was quiet. Then he asked it. The same question he'd asked two nights ago, when his tent had still been standing.

"What happens to them? The Phoenix-Born. When they get to the dune and set the light free. What happens to them, after?"

I held my breath.

And Hesset, who had sat among his truth bells for more years than the boy could have guessed and never told a lie that mattered, looked at him with his blind eyes and said:

"Go to the City of Glass. The answers there are older than mine, and truer. You'll understand it better when you see it written."

The bell didn't ring.

Of course it didn't. Every word was true. My tail stayed gold. There was nothing in it for either of us to catch.

But I looked at Hesset across that tent, and Hesset, who could not see me, turned his face toward me anyway. And we both knew.

He had not lied to the boy. He had done something worse. He'd stood beside the truth with a cloth over it and let the boy walk past. He'd sent a child who can't read to a city made of writing, and he knew exactly what was written there.

That night, in that tent, an old man I had once loved like a son pushed a fifteen-year-old boy into the gap between *true* and *the whole truth*, and wished him well.

I didn't say anything either. Remember that, when you're judging him. I was sitting right there, and I could have said it, and I didn't. I never have. Eleven times, I never have.

---

"May the sand remember you," said Hesset at the tent flap.

The Embers were fading. The eastern sky was turning the deep, bruised blue that comes just before the Fire. Beyond the clay houses of Harrow's End the grey lay low and flat along the edge of the world. It hadn't moved in the night. It hadn't needed to. Along its edge, quite still, stood a line of small grey shapes. From that distance you might have taken them for posts. They were all facing the tent.

"Will it follow me?" Zayn asked. "If I go. Will it leave them alone?"

"I don't know," said Hesset.

The bell was silent. He truly didn't.

Zayn nodded. Then he did something I'd never seen him do in fifteen years. He stepped forward and put his arms around the old man, quickly and clumsily, like someone who's never been taught how. Hesset went very still. Then his dry hands came up and rested on the boy's back, either side of the new wound, and held there a moment too long. The way you hold someone at a graveside.

"Go," Hesset said roughly. "Before I make you drink more tea."

We went.

---

We walked north into the deep desert as the dunes were finishing their night's work.

If you have never seen the Destiny Desert move, I'm sorry for you, and also not. It's the most beautiful thing in the world, and one of the most frightening. In that last grey hour before dawn the dunes were settling into their new places. Whole hills slid slowly sideways with a sound like a vast animal breathing in its sleep. Ridges folded. Hollows opened. Paths that had been there yesterday were gone, and new ones lay smooth and fresh between the dunes, untouched by any foot.

One of those new paths opened right in front of us.

It ran straight out from the edge of Harrow's End, between two great golden slopes, smooth as a swept floor. It hadn't been there an hour ago. Zayn stopped at the start of it and stared.

"It's for me," he said.

"Apparently," I said.

It was very straight. Desert paths are never straight. This one went north like a line drawn by a ruler, like a road laid down to a single place by someone who did not intend you to wander. Like a throat.

He looked back once.

Harrow's End sat behind us in the blue light, small and dark and huddled around its well. He could make out the flattened patch where his tent had been. At the far edge, a thread of smoke rose from Hesset's tent straight up into the still air.

And along the eastern side of the village, grey.

He touched the feather mark. He touched the bell. He touched the folded grey cloth inside his shirt, where the broken feather now lay wrapped against Auntie Sabba's bread, beating faintly, out of time.

"The sand remembers," he whispered.

Then he turned his back on the only home he'd ever had and walked out into the desert, and the dunes closed softly behind us. I listened for it, and I heard it: a long, slow, satisfied sound, like a mouth closing.

---

About an hour later, as the Fire came up red over the eastern dunes, I found a scorpion.

It was a fat one, the colour of old copper, scuttling out from under a stone to warm itself in the first light. I caught it neatly behind the stinger and sat down in the middle of the path to eat it.

I took my time. I chewed with my mouth open. The shell made a wonderful noise, a wet, splintering crunch that echoed off the dunes, and I made sure to crunch every single leg individually.

Zayn stopped and turned around.

"Do you have to?" he said.

"I do, actually," I said, with my mouth full. "It's very nutritious. You need building up. You'll want your strength for the end."

"That's disgusting."

"*You're* disgusting. You smell like bitterleaf and old blood. At least my breakfast was alive this morning."

He stared at me. And for a moment, just a moment, something happened at the very corner of his mouth. It wasn't a smile, not yet. He wouldn't truly laugh for a long time, not until he met someone who could make him. But it was the place a smile would go, one day, if things went well.

Things never go well. But they go, and sometimes there are smiles along the way, and I've learned to take those where I find them, like water under a stone.

He turned and kept walking. I finished my scorpion, and licked my whiskers, and trotted after him.

My tail was glowing. I'll let you work out why. And I'll tell you that I didn't say the other thing, the thing I wanted to say, that this time would be different. I knew what my tail would do.

---

*End of Chapter Four*
