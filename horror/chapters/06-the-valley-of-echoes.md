# Chapter Six
## The Valley of Echoes

> *Shout your name in the valley, and it will shout back your fear. Whisper it, and it will learn it.*
> — a warning scratched on a cairn at the valley's mouth, in a hand no one living can read

---

The dunes ended on the ninth day, and the ground fell open like a wound.

One moment we were walking a long golden ridge. The next, the sand simply stopped, the way skin stops at the lip of a cut, and below us was rock. Red rock, the colour of old blood that has been left to dry in a bowl, split down the middle by a canyon so deep the Fire couldn't reach the bottom of it.

It ran north as far as I could see. The walls were sheer and banded carved by some long-dead river into ledges and hollows and curved shapes like the insides of ears. A valley made of ears.

At its mouth, half buried in drifted sand, stood a cairn of piled stones with something scratched into the topmost one. Around the base of the cairn lay a ring of small things. A sandal. A wooden spoon. A child's clay whistle with the end bitten off. Offerings, or things dropped by people running. The desert doesn't label its exhibits.

Zayn looked at the scratches for a while. Then he looked away. He'd stopped asking me to read things for him since the night the dunes wrote at him and he'd stood there like a cow in front of a sermon.

"We have to go through," he said. "Don't we."

It wasn't a question. The feather mark was tugging north, straight down into that red throat of stone. And on every other side, the dunes had closed in during the night, steep and soft and very patient, the way a crowd closes in at a hanging.

"The desert does like to make its point," I said. "Usually with someone's head."

---

"Listen to me," I told him, at the top of the path that wound down into the canyon. "Listen properly, for once in your short life. This is the Valley of Echoes."

"Another story?"

"Every story is a story until it eats you. Listen. Whatever you do down there, don't talk. Don't shout. Don't call out. If you trip, swear inside your head. If you see something *else*, keep that to yourself even harder."

"Why?"

"Because the valley answers," I said. "Not with your voice. With what's underneath it. The things you're most afraid of. The things you'd never say out loud." I paused. "And it remembers what it hears. If you say your name down there, little scavenger, the valley keeps it. It'll be calling you by it long after you've gone. People have heard their own names coming up out of this canyon fifty years after the owners died."

He looked at me. My tail was glowing in the shade of the canyon's lip, steady and bright.

"Have you been here before?" he asked.

"Yes."

"What did it say to you?"

I started down the path without answering. After a moment, I heard him follow.

---

It was cold at the bottom.

Not cool. *Cold*, the damp, creeping cold of a cellar, which is wrong in a desert the way a smile is wrong on a corpse. The air smelled of stone and standing water and, underneath that, something sweetish and old that I decided not to name for him. Somewhere ahead, something dripped, *tik, tik, tik*, and the sound came back from everywhere at once, like a great many small fingers tapping on the stone.

We walked in silence.

The walls rose higher as we went. The sky narrowed to a ribbon, like the light at the top of a well you've fallen down. Our footsteps echoed, every one, sent back to us faintly from the stone. They came back a little *late*. And there were a few more of them than we'd made.

Zayn noticed. He stopped once, and the footsteps went on for three more paces without him, and then they stopped too, as if whoever was making them had also paused to listen.

He looked at me. I looked at the path. We kept walking.

In the hollows of the walls, as the morning wore on, I began to see the marks. Handprints, some of them. Long, five-fingered streaks in the soft red stone, as if someone had walked along this ledge with their hand on the wall, slowly sinking. And here and there, scratched into the rock, the same short word over and over, in letters neither of us could read. I didn't need to read it. I'd seen people write it before. It's what people write when they're trying to keep hold of their name.

For most of the morning, the silence held.

Then, around midday, the path narrowed to a ledge along the canyon wall, with a long drop to a dry streambed on one side. There were small bones down there. And a loose stone turned under Zayn's foot.

He caught himself. He didn't fall. But he gasped, the small sharp intake of breath anyone would make, and with it, before he could stop himself, came a sound.

"*Ah*—"

It went up the canyon wall like a bird let out of a box.

We both froze.

The echo came back. It should have come back as *ah*. That's what echoes do. They return what you give them.

This one didn't.

"*Thrown away*," said the valley.

---

It wasn't loud. That was almost the worst of it. It was soft, like someone whispering an inch behind his ear. And it was in his own voice. Zayn's voice, but younger. The voice of a very small child who has been crying for a long time and has stopped expecting anyone to come.

"*Thrown away*," it said again, from farther up the canyon, and again, fainter, from somewhere below us, down among the little bones. "*Thrown away. Thrown away.*"

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

I could see him trying not to breathe. He was pressing so hard on the feather mark that his knuckles had gone white as the bones below.

"*Mistake*," said the valley.

This voice was different. Older. Deeper. A man's voice, and I didn't recognise it. But it said the word the way you'd say it about a pot that had come out of the kiln cracked. Without anger. A thing not worth the water it would cost to keep.

"*Mistake. Mistake. Should have left him in the sand. Should have left him for the dogs.*"

"Keep walking," I breathed. As quietly as a fox can breathe, which is very quietly indeed. "Don't answer it. *Walk.*"

He walked. One foot in front of the other, with the voices coming from everywhere. Something moved in a hollow in the rock ahead of us. A shape the size of a crouching man, pressed back into the dark of the hollow, with its face turned to the wall. As we passed, it didn't move. But I heard it whisper, under the other whispers, in a voice that was nobody's at all: "*…don't answer it, walk, don't answer it, walk…*"

My words. Already. The valley had taken them and was wearing them.

"*No name*," said the valley. "*No name. Nobody's. Nothing.*"

Then came a voice I *did* recognise. Everyone in Harrow's End would have recognised it. Sharp-tongued and cracking, a voice Zayn had heard at the well every day of his life.

"*Monster.*"

Zayn stopped walking.

---

"*Monster*," said Bram's voice, high up on the canyon wall. And then other voices joined it, dozens of them, from every ledge and ear-shaped hollow and crack in the stone. The whole village of Harrow's End, shouting together the way they had shouted with the stones in their hands. Oszkar. Garrem. Hadric at his well. Little children who had learned the word before their letters.

"*Monster. Monster. Monster. Monster.*"

And under it, very faintly, the thud of stones. Over and over. Landing on something soft.

He turned to face it.

I knew what was going to happen. I'd known since the top of the path. Some things you can warn a person against all you like. They'll do them anyway, because some people would rather die than let the thing stand unanswered. In my experience, they usually get the chance.

"*I'm not!*" Zayn shouted.

It tore out of him, loud and raw. The valley caught it in its red mouth and turned it inside out like a glove and threw it back.

"*NOT A HERO*," the valley roared. "*NOT A HERO. NOT A HERO. NOBODY. NOBODY. FORGOTTEN.*"

It was his own voice again. Not a child's now. His own, exactly as it was, and that was the cruellest thing of all. Because none of the others had been true. Not *thrown away*, not *mistake*, not *monster*. Those were other people's words. But this one was *his*. It was the fear he'd kept in the deepest cellar in him, under the dreams of being a hero, under everything.

That he'd do it all, every terrible thing, and in the end it wouldn't matter. Nobody would remember him at all.

"*FORGOTTEN*," howled the valley. "*FORGOTTEN. FORGOTTEN. FORGOTTEN.*"

And then it did something I had never heard it do, in all the times I'd passed through. It said his name.

Not *Sandborn*. Not *the Nameless*. The name he'd given himself at seven, sitting on the lip of a well. The name he had never once said to the wind.

"*Zayn*," the valley whispered, almost tenderly, from every wall at once. "*Zayn. Zayn. Zayyyn.*" Tasting it, the way a dog learns a scent. "*Who was Zayn? Nobody. Nobody remembers Zayn.*"

He went down on his knees on the ledge, both hands over his ears. It didn't help. It was coming from inside his hands.

---

I bit him.

Not hard. On the wrist, just enough to sting. He yelped, and the yelp went up the canyon wall too, and the valley sent back something wordless and wet, like someone crying with their face pressed into the dirt.

"Up," I said. I said it very low, almost no voice at all. "Get up and walk, or I'll bite you somewhere that'll make it hard to sit for a week, and then you'll have something *real* to cry about."

He got up. He walked.

And I'll tell you something I'm not proud of. When I'd said *up*, I'd said it a fraction louder than I meant to.

The valley heard me.

"*Again*," it whispered.

It was my voice. Old and thin and very, very tired.

"*Again. Again. Again.*" And then, so faint I've sometimes told myself I imagined it: "*Too late. Too late. You're always too late.*"

Zayn turned his head and looked at me.

I didn't look back at him. I kept my eyes on the path and my mouth shut, and I walked, and after a while he walked too.

Behind us, as we went, I heard the valley practising. Trying out the two names it had learned that day, side by side, the way a child practises new words. *Zayn. Kiri. Zayn. Kiri.* Over and over. Learning them, for later.

He never asked me about *again*. I've always been grateful for that. I'm not sure what I'd have told him.

---

The valley let us go a little before sunset.

The walls opened out slowly, the way a fist unclenches when its owner has finally died. The canyon spilled us out onto a wide stony plain where a few thorn trees grew crooked and black against the sky, all bent leaning away from the valley's mouth. There was a pool under an overhang of rock. Real water, brown and cold and tasting of iron, like a split lip. Zayn drank until he was sick, then drank again, more carefully.

Neither of us said anything for a long time.

The Fire went down. The Embers came out. The Ash rose late again, a little thinner than it had been the night before, a pale curl of white low over the canyon's mouth like a fingernail paring.

Zayn sat with his back against a rock and the grey cloth from Harrow's End wrapped round his shoulders. Very quietly, he said his own name under his breath. Once. Testing. As if checking it was still there.

From the mouth of the valley, faint across the stony plain, something said it back.

He didn't say it again.

"Is it true?" he said at last, barely above a whisper.

"Is what true?"

"What it said. That I'll be—"

He stopped. He couldn't say the word. Not because it would burn, but because he was afraid it wouldn't.

"The valley doesn't tell the truth," I said. "It tells you what you're afraid of. That isn't the same thing."

My tail glowed. He looked at it for a long time, as if he were deciding whether to believe it. My tail glows for things that are *true*. It has never once glowed for things that are *enough*.

Then he lay down on the stony ground, pulled the cloth up over his ears, and slept.

---

He dreamed.

He was standing on a plain, and the plain was grey.

Not the grey of the Hollow at the edge of Harrow's End, not exactly. Softer. A flat, endless grey that stretched to every horizon. No sky. No sound. Not even his heartbeat. He put his hand to his chest to feel for it, and found nothing there to feel, and the hand went on sinking a little way into himself, as if he were made of ash, before he snatched it back.

And there were sheep.

They moved slowly across the plain around him, dozens of them, grazing on nothing. They were made of grey mist. When they turned their heads he could see through them. They had no eyes, but their mouths kept chewing, and when one came close he heard that it was chewing words. Tiny, mumbled, half-swallowed words. *Mother. Water. Home. My name is—* and then the grinding of teeth, and the word was gone.

Among them, leaning on a long crook, stood a boy.

He was small. Smaller than Zayn. Twelve or thirteen, thin as a stick, in a patched brown robe that was the only colour in that whole grey world. His feet were bare. His face was turned away.

"They always come back," the boy said. "Have you noticed? No matter how far they wander. You just stand still, and wait, and they come back to you. Everything comes back to the Hollow in the end."

His voice was soft and ordinary. A little hoarse, as if he hadn't used it in a very long time.

Zayn tried to speak, and found he could.

"Who are you?"

The boy thought about it. He frowned, the way you'd frown trying to remember where you'd put something down.

"I don't remember," he said at last. He didn't sound upset. "I used to. I'm almost sure I used to." He shrugged his thin shoulders. "It doesn't hurt, after a while. Not having one. It's like taking off a sandal that's been rubbing."

He turned his head.

His face was a boy's face, thin and brown and dusty, with a narrow chin and a crooked front tooth. All except his eyes. Where his eyes should have been, there were two small round holes, very neat, as if someone had pressed them out with a thumb. Through them Zayn could see the grey. The endless, empty grey, going on forever inside his head.

"I heard you in the valley," the boy said gently. "*Forgotten.* You're right to be afraid of it. You should be. I'm sorry. I wish someone had been honest with me, when I was where you are."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" The boy smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the terrible thing. The kindest smile anyone had given Zayn in his whole life, except perhaps Auntie Sabba's at the edge of the village. "They've told you the stories. The Phoenix-Born. They rise like the fire-bird, they save everyone, they're remembered forever." He shook his head slowly. "No one remembers the bearers."

The grey sheep had stopped chewing. They had all turned their eyeless faces toward Zayn.

"They light lanterns for them once a year, out in the towns," the boy said. "On the Night of Embers. A whole sky full of little lights. And not one person holding a lantern could tell you a single bearer's name. Not their faces. Not whether they screamed at the end." He tilted his head. "They did, you know. Some of them. You'll want to know that, later, when it's your turn. You won't be the first to scream."

"That isn't true."

The words came out of Zayn, and as they did, the feather mark gave a single sharp throb. Not quite a burn. Not quite. A warning.

The boy saw it. His hollow eyes went to the mark, and something in the grey behind them stirred, like a fish turning in deep water.

"Isn't it?" he said softly. "You can't even say it without it hurting."

He came closer. He walked without leaving footprints, and stopped an arm's length away and looked up at Zayn with those two holes full of nothing. Up close, he smelled of wool and smoke and something burnt. Hair, maybe.

"Ask the fox," he whispered. "Ask the fox how many. Ask him what he isn't telling you."

And he reached out one small, dusty hand toward Zayn's chest, toward the feather, and where his fingers came near, the light under Zayn's skin began to go grey. Not dim. *Grey*. And the boy's fingers, where the light touched them, grew a little more solid. A little more real. As if he were drinking.

"There," said the boy, with his eyes closed. "Oh, there. I remember warm."

---

Zayn woke with a shout.

He was sitting bolt upright on the stony ground with the grey cloth tangled round his legs, and his hands were glowing. Not gold. Grey. A dim, cold, sickly light, like the light on that endless plain. It flickered between his fingers and then went out.

The Embers were fading. Dawn was close.

And there, in the dust of the stony ground beside where his head had lain, were marks. Small, careful marks, like hoofprints. Sheep's hoofprints, in a ring all round him, as if something had stood over him in the night and grazed.

I was standing over him with every hair on my body on end. I'd already looked at the prints. I'd already decided not to look at them again.

"What did you see?" I said.

He told me. I could see that he didn't want to, but he couldn't help it. The grey plain. The sheep chewing words. The boy with eyes like thumbprints. *No one remembers the bearers.* *Ask the fox how many.*

I listened to all of it without a word.

When he'd finished, I was quiet for a moment. Then I said:

"Don't listen to him."

My voice came out strange. Low and rough, as if something had got caught in my throat and was still alive in there.

"Kiri—"

"Don't listen to him," I said again. "Whatever he says to you. However kind he sounds. However sad. *Especially* when he sounds kind. Don't you *dare* listen to him." The fur along my spine stood straight up. My silver eye burned like a coal. "I knew him. He's a liar."

My tail blazed. Brighter than he'd ever seen it, brighter than the lamp in Hesset's tent, so bright it threw my shadow out long and black across the stones and over the ring of little hoofprints.

Zayn stared at it. Then at me.

"You *knew* him?" he said. "Who is he? He said he didn't remember his own name. He said to ask you *how many*. How many what?"

"That's all," I said.

"But—"

"That's *all*."

I turned my back on him and walked away across the stony plain, up onto a tall flat rock at the edge of the canyon, and I sat there with my tail wrapped round my feet.

He didn't follow. He'd learned that much about me, too.

---

I sat on that rock until the Fire came up.

I looked at the sky the whole time. The last few Embers were going out one by one, like eyes closing. I don't know whether the Weaver lives up there. It's where I look when I need to be angry at something that never answers.

*You let him in,* I thought at it. *You let him walk right into the boy's sleep. Nine days out of Harrow's End, and you've already let him in. Did you open the door yourself? Do you two still talk?*

The sky said nothing. It never does. That's how you know it's listening.

I'll tell you what I didn't tell Zayn, since you're listening too.

His name was Malek.

He was a shepherd. A small, poor, ordinary shepherd boy, a long time ago, before the City of Glass was glass. He had a crooked front tooth and a patched brown robe that had been his father's, and he was frightened of the dark, and he talked to his sheep because nobody else would listen.

Nobody except me.

He was the first. The very first child who ever carried the Light. And I was his friend. His only friend. I loved him the way you love the first of anything, before you've learned what loving things is going to cost you.

He called me a liar once, too. At the end. Just before. He stood on a hill with the light pouring out of his chest and his eyes still brown and still his, and he said, *You knew. You knew and you walked me here anyway.*

He wasn't wrong. That's the part the valley found.

I've told you the valley says what you're afraid of. On that ledge, when it whispered *again* in my own voice, it had found my fear and said it perfectly.

That I would walk beside one more child to that dune. That I would watch it happen again. And again. That I'd be the one who brought them, every time, the patient fox at their heel, like a dog bringing the lamb.

Behind me, I heard Zayn get up. I heard him come and stand at the foot of my rock, quietly, not saying anything. I noticed he was careful not to step in the hoofprints.

After a while I climbed down.

"North," I said. My voice was almost normal. "There's a caravan road out past those thorn trees, unless the dunes have eaten it. You need water, and food, and to be somewhere with other people for a while, so you'll stop having conversations with things in your head."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"Kiri," he said. "Was he really a liar?"

"Yes," I said.

My tail glowed. Every liar I've ever known has told the truth sometimes. That's how they get you to keep listening.

He nodded slowly, and we walked out across the stony plain toward the thorn trees, with the Fire rising red on our right and the Valley of Echoes silent at our backs.

It was silent. I listened very carefully. But I could have sworn, just as we passed out of earshot, that somewhere deep in the red stone, in a child's voice, sweet and patient and in no hurry at all, something was calling his name.

*Zayn. Zayn. Come back, Zayn. We'll wait.*

---

*End of Chapter Six*
