# Chapter Three
## Monster

> *Beware the child who glows; the desert has plans for him, and the desert is always hungry.*
> — a saying of the desert grandmothers, told to children who stay out after dark

---

Bram ran first.

I'll give him that. Whatever else he did, before and after, Bram was the first one into the grey that night.

"*Tobin!*"

The grey swallowed the name. Sound doesn't carry in the Hollow. You shout, and the shout leaves your mouth and simply stops, like a stone dropped into thick mud. Bram shouted his brother's name three times, and each time it died a hand's width from his lips.

And each time, somewhere deeper in, something said it back to him. Softly. Getting it a little more right each time.

Zayn was on his feet before he knew it. His legs were shaking. His palms still burned where the golden cracks ran across them. He looked at the flat dead place where the lane stopped being a lane, and took one step toward it.

"No," I said.

He took another.

"I said *no*. You're half dead on your feet, and that is the Hollow. Not a story. The thing itself. You walk in there tonight and something else might walk out wearing you."

He looked down at me. "He's seven," he said.

And then he went in after Bram, and I went in after him, because apparently that's who I am.

---

The grey feels like nothing.

It's the feeling of a room after everyone you love has left it, spread over the whole world. The colour goes first. Then the sound. Then, if you stay long enough, the reasons you came. Every time I come out of it, I'm a little less sure which of my memories are mine.

Zayn walked with his hand flat against his chest, and the feather under his palm was the only warm thing for a hundred paces in any direction. Every few steps he whispered something, too low for the grey to steal.

*The sand remembers. The sand remembers.*

There were Husks in there. Standing between the grey houses like fence posts, faces turned up toward a sky with no Embers in it. As we passed, their heads came round, slowly, one after another, following the warmth of the mark. They smiled their wrong smiles and murmured their last sentences into the dead air. One of them was a boy no older than Tobin. He was saying, *"I'm not tired, I'm not tired, I'm not tired."*

Zayn didn't look at him. I was proud of that, and sick about it.

We found Bram on his knees at the end of the lane.

Tobin was sitting in front of him in the sand, cross-legged, the way small children sit when they've been told to wait and haven't been told for how long. Four Husks stood around him in a neat square, one at each corner, facing inward. Keeping him. The way you'd keep a lamb in a pen.

He wasn't crying any more. That was what frightened me most. His face had gone the colour of old ash, and his eyes were open and calm and terribly white.

Bram had him by the shoulders, shaking him gently, desperately.

"Tobin. Tobin, it's me. Look at me. *Look* at me."

Tobin looked at him. Politely, the way you'd look at a stranger who had stopped you in a market.

"Who are you?" he said.

And around him, the four Husks said it too, in four different voices, at exactly the same moment. *"Who are you?"*

Bram made a sound I've heard many times and still have no word for. It's the sound of something tearing that isn't cloth.

---

They got him out of the grey between them. Bram carried him and Zayn walked behind, his glowing palms held up like lanterns, and the grey drew back from them a little. The four Husks didn't follow. They stayed exactly where they were, standing around the empty square of sand, facing inward at nothing. I looked back once. They were still guarding it.

Hadric the well-keeper was waiting at the edge with a lamp. He took one look at his younger son's face and sat down in the sand as if his legs had been cut from under him. Behind him people were gathering: Oszkar, Garrem with a cudgel, neighbours clutching shawls.

Bram laid his brother on the hard ground outside the house.

Then he turned and looked at Zayn.

It wasn't hatred. It was the look of someone at the bottom of a deep pit, seeing a rope and hating that it was this particular rope.

"You did something," Bram said hoarsely. "Before. You did something with the light and that woman's eyes went *brown*. I saw it."

Zayn said nothing.

"Do it again."

"I don't know how."

It was true. My tail glowed with it, soft gold in the dark. Bram didn't know what that meant yet, but he saw it, and something in his face crumpled.

"Please," said Bram.

I don't think he'd ever said that word to Zayn before. It came out of him rusty and broken, like a gate that hadn't been opened in years.

"Please. He's my brother. *Please.*"

Zayn knelt.

---

I want to tell you he laid his hands on that child like the heroes in Hesset's stories. He didn't. His hands were shaking so badly that when he set them on either side of Tobin's face, the boy's head rocked. He was thinking of the Husks, and the grey, and forty people watching him from behind their lamps. He was thinking about what they'd say. He was thinking about himself.

So the light came grey.

It leaked out of his palms thin and sickly, the colour of the thing he was trying to fight. It lay over Tobin's face like dirty water. And Tobin, under it, smiled. Slowly. Too wide. The Husk smile, on a seven-year-old's mouth.

Zayn snatched his hands back as if he'd been bitten.

"I can't," he whispered. "I can't, I'm making it worse, it won't—"

"Stop looking at them," I said, right by his knee, low. "Stop thinking about what they see. They're not the ones who need you."

"I don't know how to—"

"Look at *him*."

He looked.

And I watched it happen for the second time that night. I watched him stop being a frightened boy surrounded by people who hated him, and become a boy looking at a child who had forgotten his own name. A child who was, in that moment, exactly as alone as Zayn had been every day of his life.

The light went gold.

---

It came up out of him gently this time, softer and warmer than the blaze in the lane. It flowed from his chest down his arms and into Tobin's face like honey poured into water, and the grey began to lift.

And Zayn saw.

He saw a baby born in the hot season, red-faced and furious, while a boy of eight pressed his nose to the doorframe. He saw that same boy carrying the baby on his shoulders to the top of the dune behind the well, pointing at the Embers and making up names for them. *That one's the Goat. That one's the Fat Man. That one's you, see, the little one, right next to me.* He saw Bram sneaking him extra bread, and sitting alone behind the house one night after their father had shouted, crying silently into his knees where nobody could see.

Nobody but the small boy watching from the window.

Then the pictures turned and ran forward. Tobin taller, with his brother's shoulders, laughing across a fire. Tobin's hands, older, holding a lantern. Blurred, like a painting left in the rain. The future always is. But warm. Full of colour.

And then, behind all of it, the other vision.

It didn't wait its turn this time. It came up through Tobin's future like a hand coming up through water and took hold of him.

The Ash, enormous above him, so close it filled half the sky, pale and pitted like a skull. Sand beneath his own bare feet, higher than any dune he'd ever climbed, falling away on every side into the dark. His own arms, opening; he couldn't stop them opening. Light running out of the feather on his chest, cracking him from the inside, and this time he felt it. Not a beautiful heat. A real one. His skin tightening across his ribs like a drum. His lips splitting. The wet in his eyes boiling, and the world going white and staying white. The smell of honey, and hot metal, and hair, and now, under it, the smell of meat.

He tried to scream and found that the fire had already taken that.

Somewhere below, someone was shouting.

He couldn't hear what. He thought, wildly, through it, that it might be his name. Then he thought: *which one?*

---

Tobin gasped.

A huge, ragged breath, the breath of someone held underwater who has just broken the surface. The colour flooded back into his face, brown and pink and alive. His eyes cleared, white to grey to the ordinary brown of a well-keeper's son.

He blinked up at the sky.

Then he saw Zayn.

I understand why he screamed. Imagine you're seven, and you've just come back from the place where there is nothing, not even yourself. The first thing you see is a face bent over yours, and the face is burning. Golden cracks running up its neck and across its cheeks like a pot about to shatter. Light pouring out of its eyes like tears. Its mouth open in a scream with no sound in it. And a fox beside it, staring at you with one amber eye and one silver.

Tobin screamed for both of them. He twisted out from under Zayn's hands and scrambled backward across the sand until he hit his brother's legs, and he clung to them and kept screaming.

Zayn's light snapped out like a blown lamp.

He knelt there with his hands still raised, slapping at his own arms, his own face, putting out a fire that had already gone. Tears were running down his cheeks. Some were his and some belonged to the vision, and he couldn't have told you which.

And across Tobin's cheeks, where Zayn's fingers had been, lay two thin golden lines, faintly shining.

Bram saw them.

He looked down at his brother, clinging and sobbing, with gold scored across his face like a burn. He looked at Zayn, cracked and glowing and crying light. He looked at the talking fox.

He was fifteen. His brother had forgotten him and been given back, and the price was written in gold across that small face, and Bram understood none of it. He'd been taught his whole life to fear the strange thing at the edge of the village. That's what fear does, in the end. It reaches for the oldest word it knows.

"*Monster!*"

It cracked out of him like a whip.

"Get away from him! You *monster*, get away from my brother, get *away*—"

---

It went into Zayn the way it always did. Like a hook.

He flinched. Just a flinch, as if he'd been slapped. Then he said, quietly, the way he'd said it at the well, the way he'd said it his whole life:

"I'm not a monster."

My tail burned bright. Nobody was looking at it.

They were looking at the light still flickering in the cracks of his skin. And somewhere out in the dark, at the edge of hearing, something howled. A long thin sound that rose and broke like a child crying itself out, and was answered by another.

"He's calling them," said Garrem. His cudgel trembled in his fist. "You hear that? He's *calling* them."

"The grey came when he did," said a woman behind him. "Two nights ago. I saw him. Up on the dune. He was saying something to the wind."

A murmur ran through the crowd: the ugly sound of frightened people discovering they all agreed.

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

Because she was right. He had whispered his name to the wind, and the wind had whispered it back, and the grey had come. Every denial that came to his tongue, the feather burned before it reached his lips.

So he said nothing. And to the people of Harrow's End, his silence sounded exactly like guilt.

"Oszkar," said Garrem.

The headman looked at Zayn a long moment. I'll say this for Oszkar: he didn't enjoy it. He had the hopeless face of a man who has decided to feed one thing to the dark so the dark will leave the rest alone. Villages have been making that bargain forever. It has never once worked.

"Get out, Sandborn," he said. "Get out of Harrow's End, and don't come back."

The first stone came from somewhere at the back of the crowd.

---

It hit Zayn's shoulder. Not hard. A test.

He didn't move. He was still on his knees, looking at Tobin, who had stopped screaming and was staring at him over his brother's leg with huge wet eyes.

The second stone hit his ribs, and the third his cheek, and after that I lost count.

"*Run*," I snapped. "Up, now, you stubborn sack of bones, *run*! I'm not burying you here. The soil's terrible."

He got up. He didn't run. He walked, backward at first, then turned and walked away through the crowd with stones coming at him from every side. The crowd parted, then closed behind him, the way a fist closes. A rock struck his back just below the left shoulder blade. I heard the sound it made. It hit within a finger's width of the old scar, the one he'd carried since he was six.

He staggered. He kept walking.

He never raised his hands. Not once. I want you to know that.

I bit a man's ankle. To the bone. I'm not ashamed of that.

---

At the edge of the village, where the last houses gave way to hard ground, there was a small clay house with an oven behind it.

The door opened as Zayn passed.

Auntie Sabba came out. She was very small, with a face folded like a dried fig. She didn't look at the crowd.

She walked straight up to Zayn and pressed something into his hands.

Flatbread, still warm from the stones, wrapped in a square of faded blue cloth knotted at the top.

He knew that cloth. He stared down at it, then up at her, and I watched him understand.

"Eat, child," said Auntie Sabba. Her voice was dry and thin and fierce. "The sand remembers kindness too."

Then she went back inside and shut the door. I imagine she sat at her table and shook for a long time. I imagine she checked the names above her door, too, and counted them twice.

Zayn stood there holding the bread.

It was the first time in his life anyone in Harrow's End had touched him gently. He stood at the edge of the desert, bleeding from a dozen places, holding that bread against his chest as if it were made of gold.

"Come on," I said, as softly as I've ever said anything. "We can't stay."

He walked out into the dunes. Behind us the lamps of Harrow's End went out one by one, as if the village had closed its eyes. Or as if something were walking from house to house, pinching the wicks.

---

The Hounds found us before midnight.

I heard them first. A low sound under the wind, like breath drawn through teeth. Then a howl, close, from behind the dune to our left. Then another, ahead. Then one directly behind us, so near I felt it in the fur of my tail.

"Fox," Zayn said.

"Yes. I know. Keep walking. Don't run. Running makes them happy, and you don't want to see them happy."

They came over the dune like smoke poured from a jug.

Hollow Hounds. Six of them, shaped like wolves, if a wolf were made of the darkness between the Embers. Long and low, their bodies fraying into black mist at the legs and tail, too many ribs showing through the dark of their sides. Their eyes were coals, the only colour in them. They spread out in a slow circle around us, and the sand didn't dent under their feet, and the air inside the circle went cold enough that Zayn's breath came out white.

They track the Light. Every time a bearer heals, it rings out across the desert like a bell, and the Hounds lift their heads and follow. Zayn had healed a child three hours ago. He might as well have lit a bonfire on a hill and sung.

"What do they want?" he whispered.

"You," I said. My tail glowed. I wished it hadn't.

He pulled his rusty knife. His other hand went to his chest, and he tried, I watched him try, to call the light.

It came grey. A sputter in the cracks of his palms, and then nothing. He was too frightened, too full of stones and the word *monster*. He'd just watched himself burn. Nobody reaches eagerly for the thing that's going to kill them.

The Hounds saw it falter, and their coal-eyes brightened.

The first one sprang.

Zayn slashed at it. The knife passed through its shoulder like a hand through smoke, and the Hound hit him full in the chest and knocked him flat. Its jaws opened above his face. There was no mouth inside. No teeth, no tongue, no throat. Only more dark, going down and down, much farther than the Hound was long, and a cold breathing up out of it like the air from the bottom of a dry well where something has been lying a long time.

And at the bottom of that dark, very small and far away, I heard a voice. A child's voice. *"Who am I?"*

I went for its throat. I'm small. I'm not stupid. My teeth closed on something cold and slippery, and the Hound shook me off with a twist of its head and sent me tumbling down the slope.

Rolling through the sand, I felt something stir in the silver half of me. Something huge and hungry uncoiling, stretching, tasting the cold in the air the way you'd taste a familiar meal. It liked the Hounds. It recognised them. I didn't let it out. Not that night. That's a story for later, and you won't like it, and I like it even less.

As it turned out, I didn't need to.

Because that was when the spear came out of the dark.

---

One moment the Hound was crouched over Zayn's chest with its jaws spread. The next, there was a long shaft of pale wood standing straight up out of its back, and the Hound was coming apart.

It didn't bleed. Shadow things don't. It crumbled into a drift of black ash that fell over Zayn's face and chest like soot, and it was cold, and it smelled faintly of a burned-out lamp.

Then the hoofbeats.

They came from the north, a heavy rolling drumming I felt through my paws before I heard it. A camel came over the crest of the dune at a full run. It was pale, almost white in the Ember-light, like something carved from bone. On its back rode a figure wrapped from head to foot in grey cloth. There was no face to see. Only wrappings, and a veil, and a dark slit where the eyes should be.

The rider leaned from the saddle, pulled the spear from the sand in one smooth movement, and drove it through the second Hound without slowing. The third leapt at the camel's flank. The rider took it out of the air.

The last three ran, their coal-eyes winking out one by one in the dark.

---

The pale camel halted at the top of the dune perhaps twenty paces away.

The rider looked down at us.

Zayn was sitting up in the sand, black ash all over his chest, knife still clutched in his fist. In the dark, the golden cracks along his jaw were faintly glowing.

The rider didn't move or speak. But I could feel the weight of that look from where I lay, and it wasn't a hunter's look. It went on a long time. Longer than it should have. The spear-hand, I noticed, was not quite steady.

"Who are you?" Zayn called.

The wind answered him. Nothing else did.

Then the rider turned the camel and rode away north into the dark, the long spear held upright like a banner. Soon there was nothing left but three drifts of cold black ash and a set of tracks the dunes were already filling.

"Who was that?" said Zayn.

I licked the ash off my paw and said nothing.

Who the rider was is a story for its proper time. If I tell it now you'll skip ahead, and then where will we be? Be patient. He was. Fifteen years, he was patient. Patience is just fear that has learned to sit still.

---

Zayn sat in the sand a long time after that, holding Auntie Sabba's bread in his lap.

The golden cracks on his face and arms were fading slowly, the way colour goes out of a coal. His back was bleeding where the stone had hit. Every so often he turned his hands over, checking they were still skin.

He looked back toward Harrow's End. At its eastern edge the grey lay over the houses like a sheet over a dead man. Only one light still burned in that whole black huddle of clay: a single, stubborn lamp at the far edge of the village, glowing through the walls of a tent hung with herbs and bells.

Hesset was awake.

Zayn looked at that lamp for a long time.

"I saw it," he said at last. He wasn't talking to me. He was saying it to the dark. "When I healed him. I saw myself. On a dune. I was burning, and I couldn't stop it, and I couldn't scream." His voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it. "All those stories. The Phoenix-Born. He *knew*."

I didn't answer that either. But my tail, lying in the sand beside him, glowed a steady, quiet gold.

Zayn stood up. He tucked the bread inside his shirt, against the feather mark, where it was warm. He picked up his knife.

Then he started walking back toward the light. Toward it. They always do. That's the whole trouble with them.

---

*End of Chapter Three*
