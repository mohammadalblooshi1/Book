# Chapter One
## The Boy the Sand Forgot

> *When the grey comes creeping and the names are lost,*
> *A child of the feather will carry the cost.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born, as sung over cradles in the desert villages

---

He is burning.

The Ash hangs full and white above the Crown Dune, so close and so swollen that it looks like something that has fed. The highest sand in the world lies under it, and on that sand a boy stands with his arms open, as if he is about to catch something falling out of the sky.

The fire doesn't start at his hands. It starts in his chest, under the mark, the small golden feather that has glowed in the dark his whole life. It splits like a seed splits, from the inside, because the thing inside it has grown too big to stay. Light comes through the cracks in his skin. It runs down his arms and up his throat. It comes out of his eyes, and his eyes go with it.

You can smell it. Nobody ever tells you that part in the songs. The light smells of honey and hot metal, and under that, very faintly, of hair.

And the boy doesn't scream.

That's the part I always remember. I used to think it was courage. Lately I've wondered whether the fire takes the scream first, the way a thief cuts the bell off a goat before he leads it away.

The light keeps growing until it isn't a boy any more. It's a shape, two great wings of gold unfolding across the dark, wide enough to cover the dunes from one edge of the sky to the other. Then it falls. Gold rains down over the desert, and everywhere it lands, the grey draws back.

Draws back. I want to be careful with my words, because you'll hold me to them later.

Somewhere below, someone is shouting a name. The wind is listening to every letter.

---

But that is the end.

Let me begin where the boy had no name.

---

You'll want to know who I am, I suppose. Everyone always does, and they never like the answer.

I am a fox. Small, sand-coloured, with ears tipped black as if they've been dipped in ink, or in something worse. My tail glows like a coal when someone tells the truth, which makes me the worst possible company at a funeral. I have one amber eye and one silver eye. We'll get to the silver one later. Don't look at it too long. Things look back.

I'm older than every city in this desert. I'm older than the city that turned to glass. I have walked beside eleven children who carried the Light, and I have watched every one of them climb the same dune under the same full Ash. Children. Notice that word, because the songs don't. *The Phoenix-Born*, *the chosen*: very good words for not saying *child*. The Boy of Salt was nine. He asked me, at the top, whether it would be like putting his hand in the cooking fire, and I told him no, and my tail went out like a pinched wick.

Some of them were brave. Some of them ran. I'm still angry at the ones who ran. I'm angrier at the sky for asking. I'll tell you about all of them eventually, if you're good and stop wriggling.

This is the story of the twelfth.

He was the one I loved most. Don't tell the others.

---

The village was called Harrow's End, which tells you most of what you need to know about it.

It sat on the last hard ground before the Destiny Desert, forty clay houses crouched around one deep well like old men guarding a single coin. To the north and east, the dunes rolled away gold and endless, and they moved every night. They *moved*, whole mountains of them, sliding in the dark with a sound like something very large breathing through its nose, rewriting the paths while everyone slept. Some mornings there were long dragging furrows in the sand that ended at the doorsteps and went no farther. Nobody talked about it.

The people of Harrow's End feared the sand the way you'd fear a large animal that hadn't decided yet whether to eat you.

They feared the boy a little more.

He was fifteen that year, as far as anyone could guess. The desert had dropped him on the edge of the village after a sandstorm, a baby wrapped in grey cloth that no weaver in Harrow's End could name. No mother, no tracks. Only a baby, lying in a perfect circle of smooth sand in the middle of the storm's wreckage, crying.

And a fox, sitting a short way off, watching.

I'll admit nothing about that.

The village kept him the way you'd keep a stray dog that wandered in during a storm. They fed him when they remembered and kicked him when they didn't. They called him *Sandborn* when they were feeling kind and *the Nameless* when they weren't, which was most days. Nobody ever gave him a real name. You don't name the lamb that's been set aside.

So he gave himself one. He was seven years old, sitting alone on the edge of the well, and he decided he would be called *Zayn*. He didn't know where the word came from. It simply arrived in his mouth one evening, already shaped, as if someone had left it there for him. He told no one. A name was the only thing in the world that belonged to him, and he had already learned that the people of Harrow's End took things.

They weren't the only ones. But he hadn't learned that yet.

---

On the morning this story begins, Zayn was underground.

There were ruins north of the village, half-swallowed by the dunes: the bones of some town so old that even the stones had forgotten its name. Every few weeks the sand opened a new room. Once, on one wall, very faint, he'd found a list of names scratched into the plaster, every one of them scratched out again afterwards, letter by letter, by someone with a great deal of time.

The village called the ruins cursed, which was convenient, because it meant the only person willing to dig through them was the boy they already thought was cursed. Zayn was a scavenger. It was the only work anyone would give him.

He crouched now in a chamber no taller than a goat, his scavenged knife between his teeth, prying at a strip of copper that ran along the base of a wall. Light fell in thin gold blades through the cracks above. Every time he moved, sand hissed down from the ceiling in a long slow exhale, and then stopped, and then, a moment later, hissed again. As if the room were breathing with him. As if it were learning how.

"You know," I said from the doorway, "most people your age are asleep at this hour. Or eating. Or doing anything that isn't crawling around inside a dead building that very much wants to keep them."

He didn't answer. He never did when I talked. Back then he thought I was a strange fox that hung around his tent, and that the words he heard were his loneliness playing tricks on him. I let him think it. Better a boy who thinks he's going mad than a boy who knows what I am.

The copper came loose with a crack. Zayn grinned around the knife, the brief, crooked grin of a boy who doesn't get many reasons to smile and doesn't waste them.

Then the wall groaned.

It was long and low and unhappy, a great weight changing its mind. Dust poured from the ceiling. Zayn froze. His hand went to his chest, to the spot just under his collarbone where the mark sat beneath his shirt. He always did that when he was afraid.

The mark was a feather. A birthmark the colour of honey, with fine lines in it like the barbs of a real one. On dark nights it glowed. Faintly, so faintly you could pretend you hadn't seen it. Zayn had spent fifteen years pretending he hadn't seen it.

Under his palm it was warm. Warmer than skin should be.

The wall held. The groaning stopped.

And in the silence afterwards, from somewhere deeper in the ruin, from a part of it no one had dug out in a thousand years, something knocked. Twice. Politely. The way a guest knocks.

Zayn didn't move for a long time.

Then he tucked the copper into his bag and crawled out into the Fire, and he did not look back, and I loved him a little more for having the sense not to.

---

The Fire is what we call the sun out here. The moon is the Ash, and the stars are the Embers: all the little bits of light left over after the sky burns down each night. Desert people are dramatic. You also notice everything in it is named after something burned.

By the time Zayn walked back into Harrow's End, the Fire was high and white and merciless, and his bag held enough copper to buy water for three days.

He got water for one.

"Copper's cheap this month," said old Garrem at the trading post, sliding a single clay water token across the counter. Each token was worth one cup from the village well. "Too many scavengers."

There was exactly one scavenger in Harrow's End, and both of them knew it.

Zayn didn't argue. He looked at the token for a long moment, then picked it up and turned to go.

He made it halfway across the square before someone said, "Look. The Nameless is back from his grave."

Bram.

There's one in every village. Bram was Zayn's age, broad-shouldered and quick-tongued, the son of Hadric the well-keeper, which made him almost a prince in a place where water was money. He sat on the edge of the well with three other boys around him like flies around a date.

"Find any ghosts down there, Nameless?" Bram called, hopping down from the well. "Find your mother? Maybe that's where they found you. In a hole, with the rest of the rubbish."

Zayn kept walking.

There was a scar on Zayn's back, a pale, puckered line just below his left shoulder blade. He'd been six. A group of children had decided that the strange boy with the too-light eyes was a demon, and demons could be driven out with stones. Bram had been five years old that day and standing at the front. Neither of them ever spoke of it. Neither of them ever forgot.

"Hey." A rock hit the sand by Zayn's foot. "I'm *talking* to you, monster."

That stopped him.

It always did. He could take *Nameless* and *rubbish* and *grave-rat*. But *monster* went into him like a hook. Maybe because, on the dark nights, when the feather glowed under his shirt and he lay awake feeling it pulse in time with something that was not his heart, a small and frightened part of him wondered whether it was true.

He turned around.

Bram was grinning. Waiting. Hoping.

And Zayn said, very quietly, "I'm not a monster."

He said it because it was true. That was the thing about Zayn, and it's the first clue in this story, so pay attention: **he could not lie.** Whenever a lie got as far as his tongue, the feather on his chest would burn as if a coal had been pressed into it, deep enough that he could smell it, sharp enough to bring water to his eyes. Once, at eight, he'd tried to push a lie through the pain and woken in the sand with a blister the shape of a feather.

So he told the truth, always, even when the truth got him hit. Mostly *because* the truth got him hit.

"Not a monster," Bram repeated, in a high, mocking voice. The boys laughed again.

Zayn turned and walked away with his hand pressed flat against his chest, and nobody followed.

From the shadow of a doorway, I watched Bram's grin fade once Zayn was gone. He looked down at his hands for a moment, as though he wasn't sure whose they were.

My tail flickered. People are rarely only one thing. Remember that. You'll need it, later, when you want to hate him.

---

Zayn's tent sat a little way outside the village, on a patch of hard ground between the last house and the first dune. It was patched in nine colours, and the wind came through the seams at night and whistled, and on some nights the whistling sounded almost like words. It was his.

There was a bundle waiting outside it.

It appeared every few days, with no footprints leading to it. A square of faded blue cloth, knotted at the top. Inside was flatbread, still faintly warm from the baking stones. And sometimes, on the good days, a twist of waxed paper holding four sticky dates rolled in honey.

Today was a good day.

Zayn sat down in the doorway of his tent and held the dates in his palm for a long time without eating them. Dates and honey were the finest thing he knew of in the world. He always saved them, for the hardest days, as if he could store sweetness the way you store water against a drought.

He didn't know who left the bundles. He had decided, when he was small, that it must be the Weaver, the one who "writes the sands", and the Weaver had made a mistake with him, felt bad about it, and left bread to apologise.

He was wrong about that. The Weaver doesn't apologise. I've waited a very long time to hear it try.

It was Auntie Sabba.

She was the old widow who baked bread for half the village in a clay oven behind her house. She never once spoke to Zayn in public or defended him. She was old and alone and afraid, and in a place like Harrow's End, kindness out loud could cost you everything. So she was kind quietly, at dawn, and left before anyone could see.

The world is held together by people like Auntie Sabba: too frightened to be brave, good anyway in small, secret ways. Zayn never learned her name in all his years in that village. But the sand remembers. That's what he used to tell himself on the worst nights, lying alone in his patched tent and listening to the wind try out its words.

*The sand remembers.*

He meant it as a comfort. He didn't think very hard about the other side of it, which is that a thing that remembers everything also forgets nothing. Not a kindness. Not a cruelty. Not a name.

---

When the Fire went down and the first Embers came out, Zayn went to see Old Hesset.

Hesset's tent sat at the far edge of the village, even farther out than Zayn's. It was hung inside and out with dried herbs and with dozens of tiny bronze bells on threads. The bells were silent most of the time. They rang only when someone in the tent told a lie.

He was the oldest man anyone in Harrow's End had ever seen. His skin was brown and creased like a dry riverbed, his beard was white as salt, and his eyes were milky and pale. He was blind, and had been for longer than anyone could remember. The village came to him for medicine and left as fast as they could.

Only Zayn came for the stories.

"You're late," Hesset said, before Zayn had even pushed aside the tent flap. "And you smell like copper and disappointment. Garrem cheated you again."

"One token," said Zayn.

"For three days' worth of copper." Hesset clicked his tongue. "Sit. There's tea."

There was always tea. It tasted of smoke and mint, and Zayn drank it every night and hated it every night, and would have died before he admitted that. Hold on to that taste for me. You'll remember it longer than he does.

"Which one tonight?" Hesset asked.

"The Phoenix-Born," said Zayn. He asked for that one every time.

Hesset smiled. Fond and sad at once, the way a man smiles at a letter from someone he once let drown.

"Again?" he said. "You know it better than I do."

"Tell it anyway."

So he did.

He told of the time before time, when the Weaver first wrote the sands, and of how a darkness came, born from fear, that made people forget who they were. Whenever the darkness grew too strong, the Weaver would place a piece of its own light inside one child. That child would carry the light across the desert to the highest dune in the world. There, under the full Ash, the child would set the light free and drive the darkness away.

"Whenever the darkness grows too strong," Zayn repeated. "How often is that?"

Hesset's hands paused on his cup. "Often enough," he said.

The bells didn't stir. True. Horribly, precisely true.

"The Phoenix-Born," Hesset went on softly. "That's what the old songs call them. They rise like the fire-bird from the ashes. They save everyone."

Zayn's eyes were shining. They always shone at this part.

You have to understand what that story meant to a boy who was nameless, thrown away and called a monster. It said that sometimes the forgotten ones are chosen. He had never told anyone his secret dream. But I knew it the way you know the shape of your own paw.

He wanted to be a hero.

Not rich. Not even loved, though he wanted that too. He wanted to *matter*. He wanted the people who threw stones to look up one day and understand that they had been wrong about him.

I have heard eleven children want that. The sky has never once failed to give it to them. That's the cruelty of it. It always keeps its word.

"What happens to them?" Zayn asked. "After? When the darkness is gone?"

He had never asked that before.

Hesset went very still. On their threads, the bronze bells waited. I watched one of them tremble, very slightly, the way a dog trembles when it hears a footstep it knows.

"They are remembered," Hesset said at last.

No bell rang. It was true, after all. Just not the whole truth.

*The whole truth* and *true* are two very different animals, and the space between them is where most of the pain in the world lives. Children fall into that space. Hesset knew exactly what happened to the Phoenix-Born, better than anyone alive. He chose not to say. I've been angry at him about that for a very long time. But that's later.

"It's late," Hesset said, and the moment passed. "Go home. And Zayn—"

The boy paused at the tent flap.

"Remember the rule," said the old man. "Never say your name to the wind."

The elders said the darkness had ears, and the wind carried everything to it eventually, and a name spoken carelessly into the open air could be found by things that collect names. In the old villages, mothers gave their babies a false name for the first year, so that if anything came calling in the night it would ask for the wrong child.

It never asked for the wrong child. But it was a nice thought.

Zayn nodded. He stepped out into the night.

And because he was fifteen, and lonely, and because the only thing in the world that was truly his was a name nobody else would ever say, he walked to the top of the nearest dune, faced the open desert, and whispered it.

"Zayn."

The wind took it, gently. Too gently. It carried it away north and east over the moving sand, toward the dark.

He stood there a long while afterward, his hand pressed to his chest, feeling oddly lighter, as though he'd sent a letter to someone who might one day write back.

Then the wind turned.

It came back over the dune from the north, cold, much colder than a desert night has any right to be, and it brushed past his ear, and in it, very faint, very far away, something said:

*"Zayn."*

In his own voice. Exactly his own voice. Only empty, the way a cup is empty, the way a room is empty after a death. Said back to him the way you'd repeat a word in a language you don't speak, to see how it feels in the mouth.

Then he saw it.

Far out on the horizon, where the gold of the dunes met the black of the sky, there was a line of grey. It was low and flat, like mist lying on water, except there was no water in the Destiny Desert. It hadn't been there in fifteen years of nights. It wasn't moving fast. It didn't need to.

It had no edge. Where the dunes ran into it they went on being dunes for a little while, paler and flatter, as if forgetting how.

Above it, the Embers were going out.

Not dimming. Going out. One by one, low on the sky, the way lamps go out in a house where someone is walking from room to room, pinching the wicks. And he understood, with the cold, sure understanding you have in nightmares, that the thing on the horizon was not coming toward the village. It was coming toward the place where his name had been spoken.

The feather on his chest began, very faintly, to burn.

And for one instant, no longer than a blink, he wasn't on the dune at all. He was somewhere higher, much higher, with a white Ash swollen above him, and there was heat in his mouth, and the smell of honey and hot metal and hair. Then it was gone, and he was on an ordinary dune with his heart going like a trapped bird.

He thought he'd imagined it. He was very good, back then, at that.

He did not see me sitting at the foot of the dune behind him. My tail glowed steady and bright, because everything about that moment was true, and I have never hated my own tail quite so much.

"Well," I said to no one, to the wind, to the Weaver if it cared to listen. "Here we go again."

Twelve. I counted it out on the sand with one claw, the way I always do, and then I smoothed the marks away before he could see them.

The grey was coming.

And this time, it had learned his name. It was practising.

---

*End of Chapter One*
