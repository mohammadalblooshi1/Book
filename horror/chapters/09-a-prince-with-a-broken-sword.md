# Chapter Nine
## A Prince with a Broken Sword

> *A broken blade still cuts, if the hand is true. So does a broken promise.*
> — words cut above the door of an old swordsmith's forge, the rest of the line chiselled away

---

Night one. I counted it at dawn, which is cheating, but I'm the one telling this story and I'll count how I like. You'll find that the one telling the story always does.

Zayn woke to the sound of someone fighting nobody.

He came up out of his cloak with his knife already in his hand, the way he'd woken every morning of his life, and found Soren in the open sand between the wells, moving through the grey light before the Fire. He was barefoot. His broken sword flashed and turned and cut at the air in long, looping patterns. He stepped and spun and stepped again, breath slow and even, as if the whole thing were a dance he'd been taught before he could walk.

Which, as it turned out, it was.

Zayn sat and watched him for a long time. He'd learned to fight from bullies and falling ceilings. He'd never seen fighting that was *beautiful*. He hadn't known it could be. It looked, he told me later, like someone arguing with the dark and winning.

"You're staring," Soren said, without stopping.

"Sorry."

"Don't be. I'm magnificent." He finished the pattern with the broken point an inch from a palm trunk, held it there a moment, then lowered it. "Taught by the finest swordmaster in the city, and then by every guard who wanted to knock a prince on his backside and call it a lesson. I've never lost a fight."

From somewhere inside Zayn's pack, muffled by the cloth around it, came a small, clear *ting*. He'd kept the bell wrapped up since the caravan, where nobody could see it glint and ask.

Soren stopped. He looked at the pack. He looked at Zayn.

"It's a truth bell," said Zayn. "It rings when someone lies to me."

I glanced down at my tail. It had gone as dim as an old wick.

"Ah," said Soren. He sheathed his sword with great dignity. "Twice. I've lost twice. Once to a palace guard when I was nine, and once to a camel."

No bell. My tail glowed. Zayn's mouth twitched at one corner.

"A *camel*?"

"It was a very large camel, and I don't want to talk about it."

---

Something was wrong with that sword, and Zayn noticed it while Soren was cleaning sand out of the guard.

The blade was snapped, of course. But the hilt was strange too. It was wrapped in old dark leather, and the pommel at the end was a round cap of bronze that didn't quite match the rest, as though it had been made for a different sword. Or a different hand.

"Why don't you fix it?" he asked.

Soren didn't look up. "Some things you don't fix."

My tail stayed bright. It was true; it just wasn't an answer.

"The old smiths of my city made their hilts hollow," Soren said after a moment, as if he'd decided to give Zayn a different answer instead. He twisted the bronze pommel, and it turned and came off in his hand. Inside the grip was a narrow, empty space about the length of a finger. "You're supposed to keep a prayer in here, written on a scrap of paper. So the Weaver rides with you into battle. My grandfather kept a lock of my grandmother's hair in his. They buried him with it. In Veimore we bury everything with everybody. We're very thorough."

"What's in yours?"

Soren tipped the hilt toward him. Empty. Dark in there, like a little well.

"I was never much good at praying," he said, and screwed the pommel back on.

Zayn nodded slowly. "Me neither."

It was a small thing. Two boys admitting a small thing to each other beside a well. But I've learned to watch small things. They're usually where the big ones are hiding. Like scorpions under a flat stone, or a letter in a hilt.

I didn't know that last part yet. I'd like to say I did. It would make me sound wiser, and I'd have done something about it.

---

He told his story that night, by the fire, with the wells humming around them like a crowd that had come to listen.

Zayn didn't ask. That's important. Zayn had never in his life asked anyone to tell him who they were, because asking meant they'd ask him back. But somewhere in the long hot day, between Soren showing him how to hold a blade properly and Zayn showing Soren which palm fronds had grubs in them, something had loosened between them. When the Embers came out, Soren just started talking.

"I'm from Veimore," he said.

Even Zayn, who'd never been farther than the Old Ruins until a few weeks ago, knew that name. Everyone in the desert knows it. Mothers in Harrow's End used it to frighten children: *Behave, or the red city will come for your name.*

"The City of the Dead and Darkness," Soren said, and smiled crookedly at the fire. "That's what the free towns call it. They're not wrong. The walls are red. Not sunset red. Blood red, and very high. There are lanterns on every wall, thousands of them, burning day and night, and they must never all go out. Not ever. There are men whose whole lives are just walking the walls with oil jars, filling lanterns. They don't sleep much. You can always tell a lamp-man by his eyes." He poked the fire. "And under the city there are catacombs, deeper than wells. On quiet nights, if you lie with your ear on the palace floor, you can hear them. Not voices. Just a sort of knocking. Very slow. The priests say it's the stone settling."

"Is it?"

"I was a prince. Nobody told me the truth about anything." He shrugged. "And on the walls of every street, someone's painted the names of the dead. Every name. Back to the beginning. You walk to the market and you're walking past your great-great-grandmother. The painters go round touching them up every year, because the names fade." He paused. "They fade faster than they used to. That's what the old painters say. Some of them fade in a single night. You go to bed and your grandfather's on the wall, and you wake up and there's just a sort of pale patch where he was. Nobody knows why."

"It sounds…" Zayn searched for a word.

"Grim. Yes. It's lovely, though, in its own way. A city that refuses to forget anybody." He was quiet. "Mostly."

He turned the heavy gold ring on his finger.

"My father is King Absalon," he said. "I'm his eldest son. Or I was. It's complicated."

My tail glowed like a lamp. Zayn looked at it, and then at the young man who'd spent last night scraping Hound-ash into a jar with a wooden spoon, and his mouth fell slightly open.

"You're a *prince*."

"Was. Probably still am, technically. Nobody's quite sure. I think it depends who you ask, and whether they're holding a knife at the time." Soren shrugged. "Close your mouth. You'll get sand in it."

---

There was a boy called Nim.

He worked in the palace stables, Soren said, looking after the riding lizards and the king's white camels. He was maybe twelve. He had ears that stuck out like jug handles, and he laughed like a donkey with hiccups, and he knew the name of every animal in the stables and told them good morning each one, every day.

"I used to hide out there," Soren said. "When lessons got too much. When my father got too much. Nim never told anyone. He'd just hand me a brush and point at a lizard."

One night Nim was sent outside the walls on an errand, past the last lantern. Nobody would ever say who sent him. He came back grey.

"Not all the way," Soren said. "Not a Husk. But half of him was gone. He didn't know the lizards' names any more. He didn't laugh. He'd just stand there, looking at his hands, turning them over as if they belonged to someone else." He was staring into the fire now, and his voice had gone flat and careful, like someone walking on a roof that might give. "And he'd smile, sometimes. That was the worst. Not his smile. A different one. Like someone had shown him what a smile was once and he'd copied it wrong. And he'd say *good morning*. Just that. *Good morning, good morning*, at midnight, to the wall."

Zayn's hand had gone to his chest. Behind my eyes, I heard a man with an orange beard saying *circle the wagons* across an empty desert.

"The law says the emptied are to be taken outside the walls and given to the dunes. Before they spread it. So I hid him."

"Where?"

"An old storeroom over the stables. I brought him food. I sat with him every night. I talked to him about the lizards. I read him the names off the walls, all the old names, thinking maybe if he heard enough names he'd find his own again." Soren swallowed. "Once, after about a month, he almost smiled. His own smile. I swear he did. *Almost*."

The fire cracked. Nobody said anything.

"The Vizier found out," Soren said. "Orrin. My father's advisor. He's—" He stopped. "He's very soft-spoken. He's never once raised his voice in my hearing. He has grey eyes, pale grey, like someone left them out in the Fire too long. When I was little I used to think he was blind. He isn't. He sees everything. He just doesn't seem to *keep* any of it." He picked at the bandage on his shoulder. "And he stood in front of my father's throne and said, very gently, that the crown prince of Veimore had been hiding a creature of the Hollow inside the palace walls. Practising dark magic on it. *Treason*, he called it. He said it so kindly, Zayn. Like he was sorry he had to."

Grey eyes. Soft voice. I'd been in the desert a long time. I knew a few men with eyes like that. None of them had ever been sorry for anything.

"Was it true?" Zayn asked.

"I was hiding him," said Soren. "The rest was a lie."

My tail glowed for both halves of that.

"They sent guards that night. Six of them. I stood in the storeroom door." He touched the hilt of the broken sword lying beside him. "One of them had an iron shield, and I hit it wrong. I was trying to get past it to the one who'd grabbed Nim's arm. The blade went in half. I kept fighting with what was left." He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I'm very good with what's left. I've had practice."

"And Nim?"

The wells hummed.

"Orrin had him taken away," Soren said. "That's all anyone would tell me. *Taken away.* I asked everyone. I asked the guards and the gatekeepers and my father. Nobody knows where. Nobody even remembers him properly. That's the thing that frightens me, Zayn. I go back over it, and I ask the stable hands, and they say *what boy?* Like he was never there." He looked down at his hands. "I've been looking for most of a year. I sell ash, and I ask in every free town, and nobody's ever seen a boy with jug-handle ears. Sometimes I wake up and I can't remember his face either. Just the ears. And the *good morning*."

"And your father—"

"Wouldn't look at me." Soren's voice didn't change. That was the worst part. "Orrin did all the talking. My father just sat there with his hand on his face. And then I was outside the gates with a broken sword and a bag of flatbread, and that was that."

He picked up a stick and drew in the sand with it. A small figure. A stick-boy.

"My brother Idan ran after me," he said. "He's ten. He got past two guards and all the way to the outer gate in his bare feet. He was crying so hard he couldn't talk. He just grabbed my robe and wouldn't let go." His mouth twisted. "They had to carry him back. Orrin carried him. Very gently. Idan stopped crying the moment Orrin picked him up. Just stopped, all at once, like a lamp going out." He rubbed the stick-boy away with his palm. "I can still hear him. I can still hear him *stop*."

Zayn looked at the smeared place in the sand for a long time.

Then, very quietly, because a story was owed for a story and Zayn always paid what he owed, he told Soren about Harrow's End.

He told him about the stones. The scar under his shoulder blade. Bram, and the lane full of Husks, and the light, and Bram's little brother, and the faces afterwards. He told him about the valley, and his own voice saying *forgotten*. He told him about the caravan, and the faces pressed on the gold, and the little girl. He told him about the word. Every time somebody had used it.

When he finished, Soren didn't say anything for a while.

"They'll call you a monster, Zayn," he said at last. "They called me one too." He looked up from the fire, and the crooked smile came back, a little shaky at the edges. "Let's be monsters together, then."

My tail lit up the whole grove.

And somewhere out past the palms, beyond the light of the fire, I heard something move in the sand. Not a Hound. Something slower. Something that had been listening to two boys say their secrets aloud in the dark, and had liked it.

I went and sat between them and the dark for the rest of the night. Neither of them asked why.

---

Night four. That was the night of the scorpions.

I want to say first that it was not my fault. I want that understood.

I had caught a fine fat one under a flat stone at the edge of the grove, a golden desert scorpion about the length of Soren's hand. I brought it back to the fire to eat it, as is my right. I crunched it slowly and with great enthusiasm, because I've found over the centuries that nothing ruins a bearer's appetite faster, and a bearer with no appetite is a bearer who isn't thinking about anything worse. Zayn turned away and made a small sick noise, which was what I'd been going for.

Soren, however, leaned in.

"What does it taste like?"

"Like victory," I said. "And a little like old boots. With a finish of regret."

"Can I try one?"

Zayn turned back round. "*No.*"

"Why not? He eats them every day. He looks fine."

"I am older than your city, and half of me is—" I stopped. "I have a very strong stomach."

Soren had already got up. He came back a short while later with his own scorpion pinned under the flat of his broken sword, looking deeply pleased with himself. He pulled the sting off with two fingers, the way he'd watched me do it, and held the thing up to the firelight. Its legs were still going.

"Soren," said Zayn.

"Don't be a coward, Zayn. Princes eat all sorts of things. I once ate a whole roasted lizard eye at a feast because the ambassador from the salt towns was watching. It looked back at me the entire time."

He put the scorpion in his mouth, and crunched.

His face did several interesting things in a row. Then he swallowed, nodded slowly, and said, "Huh. Actually, that's not so—"

He was sick for a full day.

---

I've watched a great many people be ill in my time. Plague. Fever. The bitter cough off the salt flats that takes whole towns in a season, and leaves the last one alive to bury the rest, and then him too. Soren being sick from a scorpion was not the worst of it. But it was by far the loudest.

He lay in the shade of the palms with his arms flung out like a man dropped from a great height. He groaned. He turned interesting shades of green. Every so often he rolled over and was sick into the sand. Then he rolled back and announced, to the sky in general, that he was dying.

"Tell Idan," he croaked, "that I died bravely. In battle. Against a beast of terrible size."

"It was the length of your hand," I said.

"Tell him it was *this big*." He tried to spread his arms and gave up halfway. "Zayn. Zayn. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"I'm leaving you my sword. It's broken. Look after it. Don't let the fox near it. He'll eat it."

I'll be honest with you. When he said that, something cold went down my back, the way it does when you hear a door open in an empty house. I've heard a great many people make their wills as a joke. The desert has a terrible sense of humour, and it never forgets a line.

And Zayn laughed.

It wasn't a big laugh. It came out of him in a short, surprised burst, like a bird startled out of a bush. Then another, bigger, and then he had his hand pressed over his mouth and his shoulders were shaking. He tried to stop, and couldn't, and that only made it worse.

Soren lifted his head off the sand. His face was green and his hair was full of grit.

"Is that—" he said. "Kiri. Is he *laughing*? I'm dying and he's *laughing*."

"It would appear so," I said.

I'll tell you something now, and you can do what you like with it.

I had not heard Zayn laugh in eight years. Not since he was seven, and gave himself a name, and sat on the edge of the well in Harrow's End saying it over and over under his breath until it made him giggle. After that, the village took the laughing out of him the way they took everything else. One stone at a time.

And here was this green, groaning, ridiculous prince, flat on his back in the sand at the Singing Wells, and he had given it back without even trying.

I want you to hold on to that. Hold it the way he held that date in his mouth. Because I've told you how this ends, more or less, and I'm going to have to keep telling you, and there aren't many sounds like that one coming.

Zayn offered to help. He held out his hands, with the faint gold already stirring under the skin.

"Absolutely not," Soren moaned. "Every Hound for three days would come running, and I'd have to fight them lying down. And you'd go all strange in the eyes again. I'll be sick the honest way, thank you."

So Zayn sat with him. He brought him water, strained and boiled, and wet a cloth for his forehead. All through the long hot afternoon, every so often, he'd look over at Soren groaning on the sand and start laughing again.

I didn't eat any scorpions in front of either of them for three days after that. I'd like that noted. It was a sacrifice. I know about those.

---

The lessons started on the sixth night.

It was Soren's idea. He'd been watching Zayn try to make sense of some marks scratched into the stone lip of the oldest well, centuries ago, and finally he said, "You can't read, can you?"

Zayn's whole body went stiff. His hand went to his chest.

"No."

He said it to the fire. In Harrow's End, not being able to read was nothing. Hardly anyone could. Only the rich could read, the rich and the priests and the people in cities, people like Soren. And somewhere under his ribs Zayn had already started to dread the moment Soren would realise how far below him he was, and look at him differently, and go.

But Soren just smoothed a patch of sand flat with his palm and picked up a stick.

"Right," he said. "Come here."

He drew a shape. A sharp line, a slant, another line, like a lightning flash lying on its side.

"That's a *zed*," he said. "It makes a *zzz* sound. Like a fly. Or like the start of you." Beside it he drew three more shapes. "*Ah*. *Yuh*. *Nnn*."

He sat back.

"That's your name," he said.

Zayn stared at it.

Four shapes in the sand, wobbly and crooked, because Soren wrote like a prince who'd always had someone else to write for him. *ZAYN*. The only thing in the world that had ever been his, sitting there outside of him, where anybody could see it. Where anything could.

"Is it—" Zayn's voice cracked. "Is that like saying it? To the wind?"

"The wind can't read," said Soren.

"You'd be surprised," I said.

They both looked at me. I looked at the fire. My tail glowed, steady and calm. I'd seen the dunes write. I'd seen what they wrote back.

Zayn copied the four shapes, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. Then again. Then again. He was still doing it long after Soren had fallen asleep, by the fire and then by the Embers, until the patch of sand in front of him was covered edge to edge with his own name.

In the morning, the wind had smoothed it all away.

Nearly all.

I found it while the boys were still asleep. Out past the palms, a long way from the fire, at the lip of the oldest well, where the stone was soft enough to scratch. *ZAYN*. Scratched deep, over and over, in a ring all the way round the well-mouth, in his crooked new hand. The scratches were fresh. There was blood in some of them, from a split fingernail.

When he woke, I looked at his hands. The nail on his right forefinger was torn to the quick. He looked at it, puzzled, and sucked it, and didn't remember. I watched him not remember. Then I went and kicked sand over the well-lip until the letters were filled in, and I didn't tell him, and I didn't tell Soren, and I sat a long time afterwards with my nose pointed at the black water, wondering which of us the well had been talking to in the night.

He wrote it again, that evening, by the fire. Awake.

---

It went both ways. That's what I want you to understand about the two of them. Nobody was rescuing anybody. Every night Soren taught Zayn a few more letters, and every day Zayn taught Soren how not to die.

He showed him how to stretch a cloth over a hole in the sand before dawn so the dew would gather and drip into a cup. Which grey-green plants had water in their roots and which had poison. That if you dig on the outside bend of a dry stream bed, not the inside, you'll sometimes find damp. He taught him to smell a sandstorm coming a whole day off, a dry metal smell like an old coin. He taught him to listen for the change in the sand's hiss that meant the wind was turning, and, more quietly, the other change, the one where the hiss stops altogether, which means something under the sand is listening too.

"And when the dunes move at night," Zayn said, "you don't follow the sand. The sand lies. You follow the Embers. They never move." He pointed up. "That bright one. And those three in a line. Keep them on your left and you're walking east."

Soren looked up at the sky for a long time.

"Nobody ever taught me that," he said. "I had six tutors. Not one of them taught me anything that would keep me alive."

"Nobody ever taught me either," said Zayn. "I just didn't die."

They looked at each other over the fire. Then Soren started laughing, and after a moment, so did Zayn.

*Yet*, I thought. *You just didn't die yet.* I kept it behind my teeth, with all the rest.

---

On the tenth night, Soren reached into his pack and pulled out a small clay pot with a wax seal, and two handfuls of dates he'd shaken down from the palms.

"Honey," he said, cracking the wax. "From a beekeeper in the salt towns. Cost me two whole Hounds. Don't tell anyone, it ruins my reputation as a dangerous exile."

Zayn went very still.

He told Soren then about the cloth bundles outside his tent, and the dates rolled in honey, and how he always saved them. He'd keep them for days, sometimes weeks, for the hardest days. As if sweetness was something you could store up like water against a drought.

Soren listened. Then he dipped a date in the pot and held it out.

"Then let's have it on an easy day," he said. "For once. See what that's like."

Zayn took it.

They ate the whole pot. Every date. They licked their fingers and argued about which of the Embers was brightest, and I sat between them in the warm, and my tail glowed so steadily all night that I gave up trying to hide it. It was the best night. I've measured every night since against it. None of them have come close, and I've had a great many.

I counted that one. I counted all of them. The thin Ash had gone and left the sky dark for a few nights, and now it was coming back, a little fuller every evening, like a cup being filled. Like a pyre being built, one stick at a time.

When Zayn told Soren that Old Hesset had sent him to find the City of Glass, Soren didn't even pretend to think about it.

"I'll come," he said. "I know roughly where it is. And frankly, you need someone with you who can read the signs."

"I can read *some* letters now."

"You can read your own name, Kiri's name and the word for *camel*." Soren grinned. "I'm coming."

My tail stayed bright. He meant it entirely. He'd have walked into the Veilbeast for that boy, and I think, by then, he knew it.

I looked up at the Ash, filling. I didn't say anything. Out past the palms, in the dark, the thing that liked to listen shifted in the sand, and settled, and was patient.

*End of Chapter Nine*
