# Chapter Fourteen
## The Ringing of the Bell

> *The bell does not lie, but it can be too late. A bell rung too late is only a funeral.*
> — a saying of the healers at the desert's edge

---

The town was called Thornwell, and it was the kind of place you could love if nobody in it was trying to kill you.

It sat in a shallow bowl between three dunes, around a spring so old the stones of its well had been worn into the shape of hands. Nine date palms stood over it. Somebody long ago had decided nine was lucky, so the people of Thornwell never cut one down and never planted a tenth. There was a market of striped awnings, a dyer's yard hung with red and saffron cloth, and a hundred flat-roofed mud-brick houses. A free town. No king, no walls. Just water, and people who'd decided to stay near it.

They hung lanterns in their doorways now, day and night, the way Veimore does. Every house. The rumours had come in on the trade roads: the grey moving in the south, whole villages going quiet. Nobody in Thornwell said the word *Hollow* out loud. They just kept their lamps lit, and didn't look too long at strangers, and hurried their children indoors when the Fire went down.

Orrin's house stood on the edge of the market. Blue shutters, a courtyard with a fig tree, and a fountain that actually worked, which in the desert is about as close to showing off as you can get.

"Animals sleep in the yard," said the steward at the gate, looking down at me.

"I'm not an animal," I said. "I'm a guest with fur."

The steward went pale, the way people do the first time, and looked at Orrin.

Orrin smiled his soft smile. "The fox may go where he likes," he said. "Outside."

So I slept on the roof.

---

They gave Zayn the top room. A real bed with a real blanket, a basin of clean water, a window that looked out over the nine palms. He stood in the doorway looking at it the way you'd look at a meal you suspected was a trap.

Then he took off the broken sword and laid it on the bed, and his bag beside it. And for the first time since the full Ash, he slept through a whole night without the sword in his arms.

I watched him through the window from the roof's edge, my burned side pressed against the warm bricks.

I didn't sleep.

The servants in that house didn't speak. I noticed that on the first night. They moved through the rooms with lamps and basins and folded cloths, and they never said a word, not to Orrin, not to each other. When one passed below me in the courtyard I saw her lips moving, very slightly, the same small shape over and over, like someone saying a name to keep from losing it.

I told myself it was a prayer. I've told myself a lot of things.

---

You have to understand how good Orrin was at it.

Most liars are bad at it. They talk too much. They hurry. Orrin never hurried. Orrin had all the time in the world, and he wanted you to feel it, the way you feel the weight of a warm blanket.

On the first evening there were dates on the table. Dates rolled in honey, on a blue clay plate.

Zayn stopped in the doorway and stared.

"Soren told me you liked them," said Orrin gently. "Sit. Eat."

The bell was upstairs, in the bag on the bed. I was on the roof with my ear to a crack in the shutter, and my tail went dim as a dying coal. Nobody saw.

Zayn sat. He ate one date, slowly, with his eyes shut, and I watched him go searching for the sweetness the grey blade had taken in the storm. I watched him find it. His face broke open with relief. He ate another. He didn't touch the twist of waxed paper in his pocket, carried all the way from Harrow's End. He ate Orrin's dates instead, and his eyes filled, and Orrin pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in days.

That's how it's done. Not with chains. With honey, and a real bed, and an old man who knows exactly when to look away.

---

Orrin wrote letters in the evenings.

He sat at a low table by the lamp with paper and ink and a little stone seal he wore on a chain around his neck. When each letter was done he folded it, dripped grey wax onto the fold, and pressed the seal in with his thumb. A falcon waited on a perch in the corner. Every night it flew out of the window with a letter tied to its leg, and every night, just before it went, it turned its head and looked at Zayn for a long moment with one black eye.

On the second evening Zayn sat across from him and watched the pen move.

"*Water,*" he said quietly. "That word. Upside down. It says *water*."

The pen stopped.

Orrin looked up. For a moment, only a moment, something moved behind those faded grey eyes, quick and cold as a fish turning in a dark well.

"You can read," he said.

"A little. Soren taught me."

"Did he." Orrin set down the pen. "He was a patient teacher?"

Zayn almost smiled, the first I'd seen since the full Ash. "No. He was terrible. He laughed every time I wrote an R. He said they looked like camels falling over."

"Then you must be a very good student," said Orrin, and went back to his letter, and folded it, and sealed it.

I remember the seal. The grey wax, and the soft way he pressed it, and the little curling mark it left. I didn't know why I was remembering it. Some part of me must have known. The part of me that always knows things a moment too late.

---

On the third evening he talked about his son.

The lamp had burned low, and the way conversations wander after dark, they'd come round to families.

"I had a son once," said Orrin.

He said it to the lamp, not to Zayn.

"And a brother. A long time ago, before I came to the palace. There was a sickness that year in the town where I was born. It came through like a wind. My brother first. Then my boy." He turned his cup slowly in his long fingers. "He was six. He used to sit on my knee while I wrote, and try to catch the pen."

My tail glowed gold on the roof. Every word was true.

That's the thing about truly dangerous liars. They hardly ever lie. They tell you the truth, and the truth is sad and real, and it opens you like a key in a lock. Then, once the door is open, they walk in and close it softly behind them.

"I'm sorry," said Zayn.

"So am I." Orrin smiled, tired and gentle. "I spent a long time trying to forget them. It doesn't work, you know. Not the way you'd hope." He looked at the lamp a moment longer. "Not unless you're willing to forget everything else as well."

Zayn was quiet. Then he said, "Tell me about my father."

Orrin set down the cup.

"He carried what you carry. The Light. Before you. The murals have a name for him. You've seen them, I think. In the glass city."

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

"*The Runner*," he whispered.

"The Runner. He was afraid of what the Light would ask of him. So he ran." A pause. "He's alive, Zayn."

Zayn's breath went out of him as if he'd been struck.

"Where?" His voice cracked. "Where is he?"

"When you're stronger," said Orrin, and reached across the table and laid a hand over Zayn's, very lightly. It was a dry hand, cool, and it stayed a moment too long. "I promise. When you're stronger, I'll take you to him."

It's a strange thing to sit on a roof and watch a boy's heart get caught on a hook. He leaned toward that old man the way a plant leans toward a window, not knowing the window looks out onto a wall.

He trusted him.

For the first time since Soren, Zayn trusted someone.

---

That night he climbed up to the roof.

He sat down beside me with his knees pulled up, looking out over the nine palms and the dark dunes beyond. The Ash was a half now, and shrinking.

"He's alive," Zayn said at last. "My father."

"I heard."

"He said he'd take me to him."

"I heard that too."

He turned and looked at me. "You don't believe him."

My tail stayed exactly as it was. I didn't say anything.

"Kiri."

"Wear the bell," I said.

He frowned. "What?"

"Hesset's bell. Take it out of your bag and wear it." I looked at the palms. "Humour an old fox."

"Why?"

I didn't answer.

"*Why?*" Sharper. "If you know something, *tell* me. If he's lying, tell me what about."

There it was. The door, wide open.

*Don't trust him.* Three words. And he would have asked *why not*, and I'd have had to tell him where I'd been on the night of the full Ash, and what I'd been, and everything after. I wasn't ready to say it out loud. Not to him. Not even to you, yet. And I had no proof, nothing but my own word, and my own word had gone dark in the City of Glass.

I'm not going to make excuses. I'll only tell you what I did.

I said nothing.

Zayn looked at me a long time. Then he made a small, tired sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Where were you?" he said. "That night."

I said nothing.

"That's what I thought," he said, and went back down the stairs.

Behind him, on the far roof across the courtyard, one of the silent servants was standing in the dark. I don't know how long she'd been there. She was looking at me. Her lips were still moving, still shaping that same small word. When she saw I'd seen her, she turned and went down, and I heard no footsteps on the stairs.

---

In the morning, he wore the bell.

Out of spite, I think, to prove me wrong, the way you'd taste a dish someone swore was poisoned just to show them. Or maybe for Hesset. He'd been angry at the old man since the murals told him what Hesset hadn't. Maybe, in that soft blue house, he'd started to forgive him a little. You can't always tell why people do things. Sometimes they can't either.

He tied it to his belt on a short thread, under the fold of his scarf. A small bronze bell no bigger than a thumb.

It didn't ring all morning. Nobody lied to him all morning. Orrin was in his room, writing.

I went down into the market to find something to eat.

---

That's where I saw Bram.

I almost didn't know him. The boy I'd last seen in Harrow's End had been broad and loud and well-fed, nearly a prince. This one was thin as a stick. Rags. Feet bound in strips of cloth gone black. Lips split from thirst. He stood at the old well with his hands cupped, begging for water, and nobody gave him any. They looked at his face and stepped round him.

There was a grey streak down one side of it. Not the old one, from the Husk in the lane. A new one, running from his temple into his hair, and the hair there had gone grey too, all the way to the roots, as if someone had laid a cold thumb on him and pressed. When he blinked, the eye on that side was a little slow to follow.

I went very still in the shadow of an awning.

He didn't see me. He was watching the house with the blue shutters the way a starving dog watches a butcher's door. When the market got busy around noon he slipped off down a narrow alley between the dyer's yard and the wall.

I followed him.

The alley turned, and turned again, and ended in a little dead square full of broken pots, where the dyer's red and saffron cloths hung over the wall like curtains in a room where someone has died.

Someone was waiting there.

Grey robe. Smooth white mask.

I flattened myself behind a cracked water jar and stopped breathing.

---

"Well?" said the mask. Its voice was flat and dry, like sand poured from a cup. It didn't seem to come from behind the mask. It seemed to come from all around the little square at once.

"He's here," said Bram. His voice shook. "You were right. The house with the blue shutters. The top room, over the palms. He sleeps by the window. He's got a broken sword. He keeps it on the bed."

"You're certain it's him."

"I'd know him anywhere." Bram swallowed. "I've known him all my life."

"Good. Tonight, then."

"And Tobin?" Bram took a step forward. "You *promised*. When you found us in the desert, you said you'd keep him safe from the grey. You said if I found the glowing boy, you'd give my brother back."

"When we have the bearer," said the mask, "the child goes free."

My tail went dark.

Completely dark, like a lamp with the oil run out. I wanted to scream at him, *Look at me!* But he wasn't looking at me. He was looking at that blank white oval as if it were the only door left in the world.

"Everyone's gone grey," he said. His voice cracked in half. "Do you understand? All of them. My father sits by the well all day with the bucket in his hands, and he says—" He stopped. He made himself go on. "He says, *Draw it up slow, boys, there's plenty.* That's all. Over and over. That's what he was saying to us the morning it came. He doesn't know my name. He says it to me like I'm a stranger. Oszkar walks round and round the square. Garrem stands in his doorway counting tokens that aren't there. *Everyone.*" He pressed his hands over his face. "I took Tobin and I ran, and we walked for days, and then you came, and—" He stopped again. "He's seven. He's only seven."

"Tonight," said the mask.

It didn't turn to go. It just stood there with its blank face pointed at Bram, perfectly still, until Bram backed out of the square himself. And when I looked back, after Bram had gone, the mask was still standing there, facing the empty place where he had been.

Then, very slowly, it turned its face toward the water jar I was hiding behind.

It didn't come over. It didn't need to. It only tilted its head, the way a Husk does, as if it had heard a sound it almost remembered.

I have never in my life left a place so quickly.

---

I've thought about that conversation a great deal.

At first it made no sense. Why would the Unnamed need a starving boy to tell them where Zayn slept? Their master was the one who'd *put* him there. Orrin had chosen the room. Orrin had chosen the bed.

I understood later. A man like Orrin never lets his own hands be seen. If anything went wrong, if the bearer lived long enough to wonder how the masks had found him, it would be Bram's face he remembered. The boy from his village. The boy who'd thrown the first stone. Not the kind old man with the dates, who'd tried so hard to keep him safe.

It was a clever plan. It would have worked.

Except for a small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

---

I ran.

Faster than I'd moved since the storm, my burned side tearing and smoking, across the market, under a camel, through a basket of figs.

The gate of the blue house was shut.

I found a place where the bricks were rough, and climbed. I'm not built for climbing. I'm built for running and digging and being rude. It took far too long. By the time I dropped into the courtyard, the Fire was going down red behind the palms, and the whole yard was the colour of the inside of an eyelid.

The giant was standing by the fountain.

He turned his head and looked at me. That was all. He stood between me and the door, and when I tried to go round him, he was there. When I tried the other way, he was there too. He never seemed to move. He was simply always where I was going.

I could have changed. I've told you that before. I'll tell you again before this story's done.

I was still standing there, shaking, deciding, when I heard the sound upstairs.

---

They were sitting at supper. That was the strange thing, Zayn told me later. Such an ordinary evening.

Bread and lentils and a little bowl of dates. Orrin had been telling him why nobody in Thornwell planted a tenth palm, and for one evening Zayn had almost forgotten to be sad.

Then footsteps on the stairs. Many of them, soft and quick and perfectly in step.

And under them, faint, a hum. No tune. No words.

The door opened.

Grey robes. White masks. Five, then eight, filling the doorway and spilling into the room, and the lamps all leaned away from them, every flame bending the same way, as if a cold wind had come in with them. In their hands were blades, long and thin, the colour of old ash, and they drank the lamplight instead of reflecting it.

Zayn was on his feet. The table went over. His hand went to his belt for the knife, and the knife wasn't there. It was upstairs in his bag, with the broken sword, on the bed by the window, exactly where Bram had said it would be.

And Orrin rose.

He rose smoothly from his cushion, the old man in blue, and stepped between Zayn and the masks. He put one arm out behind him, shielding the boy, the way a father shields a child.

"Stay behind me, child," he said. His voice was low and calm and full of love. "You're safe. No one will harm you while I live."

And the bell rang.

---

It wasn't loud. That's what I always remember. I heard it from the courtyard, through the shutters: one clear note, high and sweet as a drop of water falling into a well. A tiny sound.

It filled the whole room.

The masks stopped humming. All of them, at once.

Zayn went completely still.

He looked down at his belt. At the bell, swinging on its thread, still shivering with the note. Then he looked up at Orrin's back. The silver hair. The outstretched arm.

Orrin turned his head. The soft face didn't change at all. But the eyes did. They moved down to the bell and rested there, and something in them went flat and cold and very, very tired, like a man who has been wearing a heavy cloak for a long time and has finally been allowed to put it down.

"Ah," he said quietly. "Hesset."

Zayn's lips were white. "The ring," he whispered. "Did he give you the ring?"

"Of course he did," said Orrin.

The bell rang again. Louder. And it didn't stop shivering after, the way a bell does. It kept trembling on its thread, humming to itself, as if it had more to say and couldn't find the note.

Then it all came at once. Zayn told me he could feel the pieces falling into place one after another, each a small stone striking his back. The bag on the camel. The dates on the plate. *Soren told me you liked them.* The ring that had not been on Soren's hand. The ring that had been warm from Orrin's robe.

*He gave me this.*

"No," said Zayn. It came out of him like something tearing. "*No.*"

Orrin sighed. He lowered his arm.

"I should have searched you myself," he said, and stepped aside.

---

The masks came in.

Zayn fought. I want you to know that. No knife, no sword, no friend, and he fought anyway: fists, the overturned table, a lamp swung in a burning arc that sent two masks reeling back with their robes alight. They didn't scream as they burned. They just patted at the flames, calmly, and came on.

And the light came.

But it came *grey*.

Thin and dim and sputtering, the colour of fear and fury. It crawled out of his fists like smoke and did nothing at all. A mask stepped in close and laid a grey blade flat against his forearm, almost tenderly.

Zayn screamed.

It didn't cut. There was no blood. But where the blade touched him, the grey light went *into* it, drawn out like water into dry sand, and something left him. A memory. He'd tell me later he never knew which. Only that it had been warm, and now there was a cold place with smooth edges where it used to be, and that he sometimes caught himself reaching for it, in the dark, the way you reach for a step that isn't there.

That was when I came through the shutters.

I don't remember how I got past the giant. Then I was in the room in a spray of broken wood, going for the nearest mask with every tooth I had.

I got one ankle. That's all. It was cold. Cold as a cellar, and soft, like something kept too long out of the light. Then a hand like the side of a house closed round my middle, right over the burn, and the giant lifted me, and I made a sound I'm not proud of, and he threw me.

I hit the wall. The world went white, then grey, then very far away.

---

The last thing Zayn saw, before the sack came down over his head, was the doorway.

Someone was standing in it. Behind the masks, in the shadow at the top of the stairs. A thin boy in rags, feet wrapped in cloth, a grey streak down his face.

Bram.

They looked at each other across the wrecked room. The boy who'd thrown the first stone, and the boy it had hit.

"They've got Tobin," Bram said. His voice was barely there. "I'm sorry. Zayn, I'm sorry."

He had never once, in fifteen years, called him by his name. I don't think he'd even known it, until the masks told him.

The bell on Zayn's belt hung silent. Every word was true.

Then the sack came down, and it smelled of burned cloth and something sweeter underneath, and the grey blades came with it, and the room went dark.

---

I woke alone on the floor of a room that smelled of lamp oil and spilled lentils.

The masks were gone. The old man was gone. The broken sword was gone from the bed upstairs, and the bag with it. Out in the courtyard the fountain was still running, as if nothing had happened. The silent servants were moving through the rooms with their basins and cloths, cleaning up, setting the table back on its legs, and none of them looked at me. Not one.

On the floor near the table lay a scrap of snapped thread, and beside it, on its side, a small bronze bell.

I picked it up in my teeth. It was still faintly warm. And very faintly, so faintly I might have imagined it, it was still trembling.

I didn't know where they'd taken him. I didn't know whether there'd be anything left of him when I found him. I only knew that the bell had rung, and rung too late, and that I had been standing in a courtyard when it did, with the truth in my mouth, saying nothing.

Bram told me, many years later, that he never forgave himself for that night.

Neither did I.

*End of Chapter Fourteen*
