# Chapter Ten
## The City of Glass

> *Here the first one said no, and the sand wept glass. Here the rest said yes, and no one wept at all.*
> — scratched into a glass wall, in a hand no one living can read

---

Night fourteen. We saw the City of Glass at sunset, which is the only proper way to see it, and the worst.

It rose out of the dunes like something the desert had coughed up and couldn't swallow back. Towers and broken arches and whole streets of glass: green as a bottle, black as oil, amber as honey, and here and there a cloudy red, like a bruise seen through skin. When the Fire went down behind it, the whole city burned from the inside, every tower lit like a lantern. Like a great many small pyres, standing in rows.

Soren stopped walking.

"Weaver's teeth," he said softly.

Zayn didn't say anything at all. His hand was on his chest. The mark under his shirt was hot, throbbing in time with his heartbeat. He told me later that it felt like something on the other side of the mark was pressing a hand against it, from inside, the way you'd press your palm to a door to feel whether the room beyond was on fire.

I sat down in the sand and did not look at it.

---

It had taken us four days to walk there from the Singing Wells. Zayn led us by the Embers, and Soren read us the old waymarkers. They were good days. I want you to know that. Every evening they sat by a small fire while Soren drew letters in the sand and Zayn copied them.

He'd learned a good many by then. His own name, and Soren's, and mine. *Water*, *fire*, and *camel*, which Soren insisted was the most useful word in any language. And on the twelfth night, sitting under the Embers, he'd asked Soren to show him the sky-words.

"Fire," Soren had said, drawing. "Embers. And Ash. That one's easy. Three letters. *Ah. Sss. Huh.*"

Zayn had copied it ten times. He didn't know yet why it mattered. Neither did Soren.

I did. I could have stopped the lesson. I didn't. I'm a great one for not stopping things.

---

"Here the first one said no," I said, when they'd both stared at the city long enough. "That's what the old stories say. The first bearer came here, a long time ago. There was an ordinary city here then, with markets and dogs and people quarrelling about the price of oil. He stood on that hill there," I nodded toward a glassy rise at its heart, "and the Weaver asked him to give the Light away, and he said *no*. And the Light went the wrong way. It went *inward*. It burned so hot the sand around him melted. The whole city. Everyone in it." I watched a cloud of glass-dust blow across the dunes. "The sand wept glass."

My tail was glowing. Every word of it was true.

"Everyone?" said Soren.

"Look at the walls when we're inside," I said. "Not too closely."

"How do you know all that?" Zayn asked.

"Stories," I said.

My tail stayed bright, because I'd *been* the story. But Zayn didn't know that, and I didn't tell him.

I'd stood on that hill. I'd watched a small, poor, furious boy with a shepherd's crook close his hands around the Light instead of opening them. I'd called his name over and over, and he'd looked straight at me and hadn't known who I was. Then the whole city below had screamed, all at once, and stopped, all at once, the way a scream stops when there's suddenly no air.

I hadn't been back in a very long time.

"Kiri?" said Zayn.

"I'm fine," I said. "Glass makes my paws cold. Let's get inside before the Hounds smell us."

My tail flickered. That was only half true, and Zayn saw it, and for once he was kind enough not to say so.

---

You have to walk through the streets to get to the great hall. There's no other way.

I tried not to look, and so I'll tell you what Soren saw, because he looked. He couldn't help it.

The walls of the City of Glass don't have names on them. They have *people* in them.

Shadows, mostly. Dark smudges inside the glass, where the sand had set around them before they could fall. A man with his arm thrown over his face. A woman curled over something small. Two shapes in a doorway, holding each other. Hundreds, along every street, like flies in honey. The last light threw their shadows onto the street, long and reaching, so that we had to walk on them.

Soren stopped in front of one. A child, near the ground, small enough to have been four or five. Face pressed to the inside of the glass. Mouth open.

"Don't," said Zayn quietly, and took his arm, and pulled him on.

Soren went. But he kept looking back. I heard him whispering, under his breath, the way he'd told us he whispered to Nim. He was just saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry*, to each one as we passed, all the way down the street.

That was Soren. I've never forgotten it.

---

The murals were in the great hall at the centre of the city.

The roof was gone, and the walls were glass all the way up, smooth and dark, and the evening came down through the open top in long violet bars. And in the walls, *inside* the walls, deep in the glass like the shadows in the streets, there were pictures.

Nobody had painted them. Nobody had carved them. They were simply *there*, floating in the glass the way your reflection floats in water. The glass had remembered them. Memory, set hard.

There were eleven.

They ran in a long curve around the hall, each one taller than a man. Under each one, set into the glass in pale gold, ran a line of letters.

Soren had gone to find the old cistern. "Don't touch anything," he said as he left. His voice was too bright. "Especially you, fox."

So it was only Zayn and me in the great hall when he started to read.

---

He went slowly. Two weeks before, he hadn't known a single letter. Now he stood in front of each mural with his lips moving, sounding out the gold shapes one by one, the way a child sounds them out. *Thuh. Eh.* A long pause. His finger traced the letters on the glass without quite touching them. The glass breathed cold onto his hand, like a cellar door.

He couldn't read most of it. But he could pick out letters he knew, and every so often a whole word came clear for him like a coin turning up in the sand.

"*Smith*," he said, in front of the third mural. "The… Smith."

And then he stopped, and frowned, and looked at the picture properly for the first time.

It showed a smith's boy. Not a man. Broad in the shoulders from the bellows, but a boy. Thirteen, perhaps. He held a hammer in both hands, and the hammer was too big for him. His feet were bare.

Zayn moved to the next. *Twin*, he found, and a word he didn't know. Two figures, almost the same, both children, one reaching out a hand toward the other, who was turning away.

In the fifth he found *king*. A boy again. A crown on his head too large for it, slipping down over one ear.

In the seventh, *salt*. A boy with white crystals in his hair and on his eyelashes, like frost, smaller than any of them. Ten, maybe. He was smiling. The kind of smile children put on for grown-ups who are watching them be brave.

In the eighth, a falcon lifting from a wrist so slim the bird's talons wrapped all the way round it.

"Kiri," Zayn said. His voice had gone thin. "They're all—"

"Keep reading," I said.

He stopped in front of the first one for a long time.

It showed a small boy holding a crook. The boy was turning away, out of the picture, his face hidden. The glass all around him was black and cracked, as if something had struck it from inside. I didn't look at it. I looked at the floor.

"Is that—" Zayn said. "That's him. The boy with the sheep."

"Keep reading," I said.

He did.

The ninth mural showed a boy walking away from a dune with his hands pressed over his eyes. Not an old man. A boy. But something in the way his head tilted, as if listening for something no one else could hear. Zayn looked at it for a long while, and something moved across his face that he didn't put into words. I didn't help him. I'll tell you about that one later. I promise.

The eleventh was clouded, as if the glass had breathed on itself. You could just make out a figure running. No taller than Zayn. Nothing else.

And then he went back to the beginning and looked at them all again, together. That was when he saw it.

---

It wasn't the words that told him first. It was the pictures.

In the first, the boy with the crook was turning away. In the ninth, the boy with his hands over his eyes was walking away. In the eleventh, whoever it was was running. But in the other eight, the picture was the same.

A dune. A very high dune, so high that its top went up out of the picture into the dark. A full white Ash hanging above it, enormous, filling half the sky. And on the top of that dune, a child with its arms open, and fire rising up out of it like wings.

Eight times. Eight different children. A girl with long hair, the oldest of them, maybe fifteen. The smith's boy with his hammer dropped at his feet. The little king with his crown fallen off. The boy of salt, so small that the fire coming off him was taller than he was. Each one on that same dune, under that same Ash, with their arms open, and burning.

And the glass had remembered their faces. That was the thing he couldn't look away from. In the pictures where the children turned away, you couldn't see their faces. In the eight that burned, you could. The glass had kept exactly how each of them looked, in that moment, with the fire coming up through them.

Not one of them looked like a hero in a story.

Every one of them looked like a child who has just found out that it hurts.

Zayn had seen that picture before.

He'd seen it every time he used the Light. With Bram's little brother in his arms. On the caravan road, when he'd smelled his own hair burn. At the Singing Wells, with his palms blistering. Every time, a little longer, a little clearer, a little hotter.

He went back to the first of the eight, the girl with long hair. His finger moved along the gold letters underneath, past all the words he didn't know. Right to the very end, where there was one short word on its own.

Three letters.

*Ah. Sss. Huh.*

"*Ash*," Zayn whispered.

He went to the next mural, the smith's boy. The last word was *ash*.

The next. *Ash*.

The next. *Ash*. *Ash*. *Ash*. Eight times, eight murals, eight children, the same word at the end of each line. Soren had told him it was an easy one.

He stood in the middle of the great hall of the City of Glass with the night coming down on him through the broken roof, and he understood.

It wasn't a picture of something that *might* happen.

The Phoenix-Born didn't save everyone and go home. They didn't grow old. They were children, every one, and they climbed the Crown Dune under the full Ash and opened their arms and burned, every one of them who'd been brave enough, and the grey went away for a while, and they were *ash*.

*They are remembered*, Hesset had said, in his tent full of herbs and bells, when Zayn asked what happened after.

No bell had rung. It was true, after all. The glass remembered them. Perfectly. Forever. Their faces at the worst moment of their lives, set hard for anyone to look at.

---

He didn't cry. I'd half expected him to.

He just sat down on the glass floor, slowly, the way a very old man sits, and pulled his knees up to his chest. His hand was pressed flat over the feather mark. He was quiet for a long time. Through the glass floor beneath him, faintly, I could see more shadows, very deep down.

Then he said, without looking at me, "You knew."

"Yes."

My tail glowed. I couldn't have lied about that if I'd wanted to. I'm not even sure I wanted to.

"Hesset knew."

"Yes."

"They're all children," he said. "Kiri. They're all *children*. Why are they all children?"

I didn't answer. I looked up through the broken roof at the sky, where the first Embers were coming out, and I felt something rise in me that I'd been swallowing for a very long time. Eleven times I had walked a child toward that dune, and every time I'd told myself the Light simply settled in the young, the way frost settles on low ground.

I've had a long time to think about that, since. I'll tell you what I think at the end. You won't like it.

"Ask *it*," I said at last, and my voice came out so savage that he flinched. I didn't mean it for him. I was looking at the sky. "Go on. Ask it why. It's never once answered me."

He looked up at the eight burning children in the glass. In the dark, the gold in them shone faintly, like coals under ash.

And then he asked me.

He asked it in a very small voice. It was the voice of a seven-year-old sitting on the edge of a well, saying his own name over and over so he wouldn't forget it.

"Kiri," he said. "Is there a way I don't have to die?"

---

I want to tell you about that moment properly. I've told you I can't stand liars. I've watched my own tail go dark for them over and over, and every time, I've thought a little less of them.

I have walked beside eleven bearers. I'd never lied to one. Not once. Not even Malek, when he begged me to.

And I looked at that boy on the glass floor, with the word *ash* written eight times around him under eight small burning faces. I thought: *He's fifteen. He's only just learned to laugh. He has a friend for the first time in his life, and that friend is somewhere in this city right now apologising to the dead in the walls, and he'll come back soon, and they'll eat together and argue about the Embers.*

I thought: *Let him have that. Just a little while. Let him have a little longer before his face looks like theirs.*

So I said, "Yes."

---

My tail went dark.

Not dim. *Dark*. It went out all at once, so that the only light left was the faint gold of the burning children in the glass.

And somewhere in Zayn's pack, muffled in its cloth, Hesset's little bronze bell gave one small, clear note.

*Ting.*

It went up into the dark of the hall and came back from the glass, again and again, smaller each time. *Ting. Ting. Ting.* As if all eleven of them were ringing it.

Zayn was looking at my tail. He'd looked at it before he'd even finished asking. Of course he had.

He didn't say anything.

That was the worst part. I'd been ready for anything else. For him to shout, or call me a liar. But he just looked at my dark tail for a long moment, and then he looked away, up at the eight burning children, and he nodded slightly, as if I'd confirmed something.

Which, of course, I had.

"Okay," he said quietly.

That was all.

I've told a great many hard truths in my life. I've told mothers what happened to their sons. None of it ever hurt as much as that one small lie, and that one small word he gave me back.

I'll be honest with you now, because I owe somebody the truth about it. I don't know whether I lied to protect him, or to protect myself from watching his face when I said *no*.

Both, probably. People are rarely only one thing. Not even foxes. Especially not foxes who are only half of anything.

---

Soren came back with a full water skin and glass-dust in his hair, talking before he was through the doorway.

"There's a cistern under the east side, it's full, it's disgusting, there's something living in it, I *think* it's only a frog, it had the right number of legs. And there are more of them down there. In the walls. Zayn, there's a whole room of them, all sitting in rows, like they were waiting for someone to—"

He stopped.

He looked at Zayn on the floor. He looked at me, with my tail still dark as a dead coal. He looked up, slowly, at the eight burning children in the glass.

"What happened?"

Zayn told him.

He couldn't have done anything else. Lying wasn't open to him. He told him all of it: the dune, the Ash, the word *ash* eight times, the children, and the vision he'd been having since Harrow's End. That it had smell now, and heat. He told it in a flat, quiet voice, staring at the floor.

He didn't tell him what I'd said. He left that out. I've never forgotten that he left that out.

When he'd finished, Soren set down the water skin very carefully. Then he walked to the first of the eight murals and read it. Properly, out loud, every word. His voice came back off the glass, quieter, as if the hall were reading along.

"*The Weaver's Daughter*," he read, "*who in her fifteenth year climbed the Crown Dune under the full Ash and opened her hands, and the grey went away. And of her there remained only*—" His voice caught. "*Ash.*"

He went to the next. *In his fourteenth year.* And the next. *In his twelfth year.* The boy of salt: *In his tenth year.* His voice got quieter and harder with each one. He read all eight. Then he read the first, the ninth and the eleventh. *Refused.* *Refused.* *Refused.*

Then he stood in the middle of the hall with his fists clenched at his sides.

"*Tenth year*," he said. "That one was ten. Zayn. That one was Idan's age."

"Soren—"

"*No*." He swung round. His eyes were too bright in the dark. "No. Look at them. Look at them, they're *babies*. Three of them said no. Three of them *lived*."

"The first one turned into the Hollow," Zayn said quietly. "Kiri told me. This whole city is what happened when he said no. The people in the walls."

"Then he said no *wrong*." Soren's voice rang off the glass. "Eight burned and three ran. That's two ways. Who says there are only two? Who *decided* that? Who decided it had to be *children*? Some sand? A wall?"

"The Weaver."

"Then the Weaver can explain itself to me in person." He jabbed his broken sword at the open roof, at the dark sky and the swelling Ash. "I mean it. I've already had one boy *taken away* from me because someone decided there was only one way things could go. I'm not standing here and letting it happen again. I'm *not*."

My tail flared back to life for him, all at once, so bright it hurt my own eyes. He meant every word.

I'll tell you something. When he jabbed that sword at the sky and shouted *who decided*, I loved him for it. It was the first time in all my years that anyone had shouted it with me.

It didn't help. It never does.

Zayn looked at him for a long time.

And there, on the floor of the City of Glass, something happened I'll remember until the Embers go out. Zayn smiled. Small and tired and sad. But real.

"Okay," he said. "A third way."

He didn't believe it. I could see that. But he'd seen how much Soren needed him to say it, and he'd said the only true thing he could find. He *would* look for one. That was true. My tail glowed faintly gold for him.

---

They slept in the great hall that night, because it was the only place in the city out of the wind. Neither of them would sleep in the streets, with the shadows.

Soren lay with his back to the wall and his broken sword across his chest, facing the doorway. He didn't say much else. Once, in the dark, I heard him say, "Idan would like this place," to nobody. Then, a long while later, so quietly I almost missed it: "No. He wouldn't." Then he was quiet.

Zayn didn't sleep for a long time.

He sat cross-legged on the glass with his pack open in front of him. He took out the broken golden feather and turned it over. In the dim light of the murals, it seemed to me that the break across its middle had closed. Only a little. Only the width of a hair. As if facing one hard truth had mended something in it. I've wondered since what it was mending itself *for*.

He didn't notice. He was looking at the twelfth place on the wall.

There was one. I'd known there would be. At the end of the long curve, after the clouded figure of the Runner, there was one more stretch of smooth, dark glass. Empty. Nothing in it at all.

Not yet.

And past it, if you looked, the wall didn't end. The glass went on, smooth and dark and empty, curving round the hall and away into the shadows by the door. Room for a thirteenth. Room for a great many more. Whoever had made this hall had not been planning for twelve.

Zayn looked at the twelfth place for a long time. Then he lay down beside Soren and closed his eyes.

I sat in the doorway and kept watch, with my tail glowing faintly in the dark. Above the broken roof, the Ash hung half full. It was halfway to the dune.

Some time after midnight, he moved.

He didn't wake. But his right hand came out from under his cloak, and the forefinger, the one with the torn nail, reached out and began to write in the thin film of glass-dust on the floor. Slowly, carefully.

*A. S. H.*

Then again, beside it. *A. S. H.* And again. Moving in a slow ring around himself, the way he'd ringed the well, until he was lying in the middle of a little circle of the word.

And then, at the end, in a hand that wasn't quite his, one more word, very small. Letters Soren hadn't taught him. The same shapes the dunes had written at him, that night on the ridge, when he'd stood in front of them and couldn't read a single one.

He couldn't read it now, either. He was asleep. But his finger knew it.

I wiped it all away before dawn, with my tail, every letter. I didn't tell him. I didn't tell Soren. You'll learn what the dunes were spelling when he did, and not before. That much, at least, I'll let him have.

I counted the night. Night fourteen. I wished I had never learned to count. I wished, for the first time in a very long time, that I had never learned to read.

---

*End of Chapter Ten*
