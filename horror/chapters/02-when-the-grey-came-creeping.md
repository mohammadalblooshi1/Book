# Chapter Two
## When the Grey Came Creeping

> *The grey does not bite. The grey forgets for you. And then it wears what is left.*
> — a warning carved above the doors of old desert wells, most of it worn away

---

Nobody in Harrow's End believed him.

Nobody there had believed anything Zayn said in fifteen years. Never mind that he couldn't lie. They'd never bothered to notice.

He ran into the village before dawn, sand in his hair, and banged on the door of the headman's house until a light came on. He told the headman what he had seen. A line of grey on the horizon, low and flat, and the Embers going out above it one by one.

He didn't tell him about the voice in the wind. There was no way to say it that didn't sound mad.

The headman, a heavy man named Oszkar with a beard like a thornbush, listened with his eyes half closed. Then he said, "Mist."

"There's no mist in the desert," said Zayn.

"Dust, then. A storm."

"It wasn't moving like a storm. It was—" Zayn searched for the word. "Listening."

Oszkar looked at him the way you'd look at a goat that had started reciting poetry. Then something crossed his face, quick and ugly, and he glanced past Zayn's shoulder toward the east. "Go back to your tent, Sandborn," he said, and shut the door. I heard the bar drop across it on the other side.

I was sitting on the wall across the lane. I could have told the headman myself. I could have said in perfectly clear words: *Your village has about one day before the Hollow puts its hand inside it.* Anyone can hear me, if I decide to speak.

But I have rules. The bearer has to see it first. He has to be the one who knows. The times I've broken that rule, things went worse. Or so I tell myself. I've had a great deal of time to practise.

So I said nothing. I'm not proud of that. It's becoming a theme.

---

The grey came the next day.

Not all at once. The Hollow doesn't charge like an army. It *seeps*. It arrives the way cold arrives in a house at night: first in the corners, then along the floor, until one moment you realise you can't remember the last time you were warm. Or what warm was. Or why you'd want it.

It started with the colour.

Auntie Sabba noticed first, though no one asked her. She came out of her house in the morning with a basket of dough on her hip, and stopped. The blue door of the house beside hers, painted the colour of the sky by a man who had died twenty years ago, wasn't blue any more. It wasn't any colour at all. It was the flat, dead grey of old ash.

Above the door, where every house painted the names of its dead so the sand would know whom to remember, there had been three names in white. There were two now. She was sure there had been three. She tried to remember the third and found only a smooth place in her mind, like a tooth socket the tongue keeps returning to.

She went back inside and didn't come out again until evening.

By noon, the whole eastern edge of the village had gone grey.

The red cloth over Garrem's trading post was grey. The date palms were grey. A goat wandered out of the grey part of the village and stood in the square, and its brown coat was the colour of cold porridge, and its eyes were the white of a boiled egg. It stood with its head at a slightly wrong angle, as if it were listening to something underneath the ground, and when a child threw a date at it, it didn't flinch. It simply turned its white eyes, very slowly, toward the child, and kept them there.

The child's mother carried him inside. The goat watched the door all day.

And it was quiet. That was the part that frightened people most, though few of them could have told you why. In the grey part of the village there was no sound at all. No wind in the palms. No dogs. No creak of doors.

People began to gather in the square. They talked in low, nervous voices. An old woman said she'd heard stories from her grandmother's grandmother about a thing that came out of the deep desert and made people forget, and that you knew it was close when the dead started losing their names off the walls. Oszkar told her to stop talking nonsense, too loudly, and didn't once look at the names above his own door.

Zayn stood at the very edge of the crowd, where he always stood, close enough to hear and far enough to run.

His hand was pressed flat against his chest.

The feather mark had been burning since the dune. A slow, low heat, like a coal banked under ash, and it got hotter whenever he looked east. Every time he closed his eyes he smelled honey and hot metal. *My birthmark is warm and I keep smelling something burning* wasn't the kind of thing that made people trust you.

"It's moving," someone said.

Everyone turned.

At the eastern end of the square, the grey had reached the first house. It climbed the clay wall the way damp climbs a cloth. It slid across a child's wooden toy left in the dirt, a little painted camel. The toy had been yellow. It wasn't any more. It had been lying on its side. When the grey had passed, it was standing upright, facing the crowd.

Nobody had touched it.

"Everyone inside," said Oszkar. "Shut your doors. Put out your lamps. Stay away from the windows."

As if a door could stop it.

---

The Fire went down red and swollen over the western dunes. Night came on. And with the night came the Husks.

They were people. That's what you must understand first. Every one of them had been a person once, a herder or a trader or somebody's son, until the Hollow found them and emptied them out. It takes names first, then memories, then all the small invisible threads that tie one person to another. What's left still walks. It still has a face. But the eyes are white and blank as boiled eggs, and the skin is the colour of ash, and the face doesn't do anything.

Except, sometimes, smile.

That's the worst of it. Not the eyes. The smile. Somewhere in what's left there's a memory of what faces are *for*, and every so often a Husk will try. The corners of the mouth go up, too far and too slowly, like a curtain being drawn by someone who has never seen a window. The eyes don't change. Nothing behind them changes. It's a smile the way a footprint is a foot.

And they talk. Not to you. They say the last thing they said before the grey took them, over and over, in their own voices, worn smooth, like a prayer in a language nobody speaks any more. A trader might walk for a hundred years saying *"Two tokens, and that's my last price."* A mother might say *"Come in now, it's getting dark."* It isn't a message. It's what's left when the person is gone: one sentence, rattling around in the empty house like a bead in a gourd.

They are drawn to light, like moths that never, ever stop.

They came out of the grey just after dark. First one, walking slowly up the eastern lane. Then three. Then a dozen, and more behind them, a grey crowd moving between the houses with no sound but the murmur of their dozens of last sentences, all overlapping, like a market heard through a wall.

Zayn saw them from the top of the hill behind his tent. He saw the shapes drifting through the dark streets, stopping at doors, laying their palms flat against the wood and standing there, as though listening for a heartbeat. He saw them turn, all together, toward the one house that had forgotten to put out its lamp.

He was running before he'd decided to.

---

I followed him. Of course I followed him. I'd been following him for fifteen years.

"This is a bad idea," I said, loping alongside him down the slope. "I want that on record. For the funeral. Short speech, I thought: *he was warned.*"

He didn't answer. He didn't even look at me. His whole face was fixed on the village, his knife already in his hand. The scavenged one, rusted along the spine, its edge sharpened on a flat stone.

A knife. Against the Hollow.

I ran faster, and listened to the voices coming up the lane to meet us, and tried very hard not to recognise any of them.

---

The house with the lamp belonged to the well-keeper.

Bram's house.

Zayn came round the corner of the lane and stopped so suddenly that I nearly ran into the backs of his legs.

The Husks had surrounded it. Twenty of them, perhaps more, standing in a silent half-circle around the door, their white eyes all fixed on the yellow light leaking from the shutters. They swayed together, the way grass sways. From inside, Zayn could hear someone crying. A small child, by the sound. And a man's voice, low and frightened, saying *hush, hush, hush*.

And out in the lane, under the Husks' murmuring, three or four of the grey shapes had begun to say it too.

*"Hush. Hush. Hush."*

Not mocking. Just learning it. The way the wind had learned Zayn's name.

In front of the door, between the Husks and the house, stood Bram.

He had a shovel, the heavy iron kind they used to clear sand from the well. He held it like a sword, shaking so badly the blade rattled.

"Get back," he was saying. His voice cracked in half on every word. "Get *back*. I mean it. Get back."

The Husks didn't get back.

One of them stepped forward. Slow. Unhurried. It had been a woman once, a grey woman in a grey dress, with grey beads still threaded in her grey hair. Her lips were moving.

*"Mind the step, love,"* she said. *"Mind the step, love. Mind the step, love."*

She said it kindly. She'd said it to someone once, on some doorstep, and then the grey had come up the path behind her, and her life had stopped on that one sentence like a cart stuck in a rut.

She reached out a hand toward Bram's face. And she smiled.

Bram swung the shovel.

It connected with a dull, hollow thud, like hitting a sack of wet sand. The Husk's head went sideways, much too far sideways, and stayed there. She didn't fall. She didn't stop smiling. She straightened, slowly, her head coming back up with a soft grinding sound, and reached out again, and this time her fingers brushed Bram's cheek.

*"Mind the step, love."*

Bram went rigid.

I watched it happen. The colour drained out of the side of his face where she'd touched it, as if someone had wiped away paint with a wet thumb, and left the clay underneath. His eyes went wide and lost. The shovel slid out of his hands and fell into the sand.

"I—" Bram said. He looked down at his hands. "I was… I was doing something. What was I…"

He had forgotten. Not everything. Not yet. A Husk's touch steals a little at a time: a memory here, a feeling there. I could see him searching for what he'd lost, patting at his own mind the way you pat your pockets for a token that isn't there. He had forgotten *why* he was standing there. He had forgotten there was a child crying behind the door who needed him.

The child cried harder. Bram didn't turn around. He'd forgotten there was anyone to turn around for.

The Husk reached for him again.

And Zayn moved.

---

He didn't think about it. Later people would tell stories about this moment. They'd say a lot of foolish, flattering things, the way people always do about children who are about to be sent up a hill.

He didn't know anything. He was fifteen and terrified, and the boy who'd thrown stones at him since he was five was being erased in front of him one memory at a time. Zayn simply could not stand there and watch.

So he ran into the middle of twenty Husks with a rusty knife.

He slashed at the grey woman's arm. The blade passed through her sleeve and hit something that wasn't quite flesh, cold and dense like packed clay. It left a thin line. No blood. Inside the line, for a moment, there was nothing at all. Not darkness. Nothing. Then it closed.

She didn't even look at him.

But the others did.

All of them, at once. Twenty grey faces turned slowly from the lamp in the window toward the boy in the middle of the lane, and twenty mouths stopped murmuring in the same instant, and the silence that came down was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

Then, one by one, they began to smile at him.

They were drawn to light. I've told you that.

And somewhere under Zayn's shirt, the feather mark had started to glow.

---

He felt it before he saw it. A heat in his chest that wasn't fear, though there was plenty of fear too. It was a *burning*. It started at the centre of the mark and spread outward along his ribs and up his throat and down into his arms, like swallowing a coal and feeling it catch.

The Husks came toward him. Their voices started again, all at once, all different, all close. *"Two tokens and that's my last price." "Come in now, it's getting dark." "Mind the step, love." "Who left the gate open?" "Hush. Hush."*

He backed up until his shoulders hit the wall of the house. The knife shook in front of him, and the burning in his chest was getting worse—

And the light came.

Not gold. Not at first.

It flickered out of his skin, out of the cracks of his clenched fists, and it was *grey*. A thin, dim, sickly grey, like light seen through a dirty window. It sputtered. And the Husks didn't slow. They came faster, the way a cat comes faster at a mouse that's started to limp. Some of them tilted their heads at it, almost tenderly, as if the grey light were a word in their own language.

That's what fear does to the Light. It makes it sound like home to them. I should have warned him.

The grey woman reached for his face. Her fingers were an inch from his eye. He could smell her: dust, and cold stone, and very faintly, underneath, the rosewater she must have worn once.

Zayn shut his eyes.

And behind the door, the child cried out. One word, high and thin and broken.

"*Bram!*"

Something in Zayn's chest *turned over*.

I saw his eyes snap open, and I saw him look past the Husks to Bram. Bram, standing lost and grey-cheeked in the lane, staring at his own hands. A boy who had forgotten someone he loved.

Zayn knew exactly what that was like. He'd spent his whole life being the someone nobody remembered.

He stopped thinking about himself.

And the light went *gold*.

---

It came out of him like the Fire coming up over the dunes.

One moment the lane was dark and grey and full of smiling shapes. The next it was blazing. Golden light poured out of Zayn's hands, out of his chest, out of the cracks between his fingers, out of his eyes. It filled the lane from wall to wall. It lit every face, every grey robe and every white eye, and threw their shadows huge and black against the houses. For one instant I saw the shadows clearly, and they were not the shapes of the people casting them. They had too many joints. They were all leaning toward him.

The Husks *recoiled*.

They didn't scream. They can't. But they flinched back from the light the way your hand flinches from a hot stone, all of them at once, stumbling and lurching away into the dark. The grey woman with the beads in her hair staggered backward. And for one heartbeat, I saw her eyes change. The white faded, and underneath was brown, ordinary brown, the eyes of a woman who had once threaded beads into her hair because she liked how they caught the light.

She looked at Zayn. Her mouth opened. For a moment I thought she would say something new, something that was hers.

"*Where are my*—" she said.

Then the grey closed back over her like water over a dropped stone, and the smile came back, slow and wrong, and she said, *"Mind the step, love,"* and was gone with the others into the night.

I have wondered for a long time what the end of that sentence was. Children, probably. It usually is.

---

The light faded.

It drew back into Zayn the way water drains out of sand.

He slid down the wall and sat in the sand.

His hands were shaking. There were thin golden lines on his palms, like cracks in glazed pottery, faintly glowing. They hurt the way a burn hurts the next day. He stared at them as if they belonged to someone else.

And then it happened. The thing that would happen every time he used the Light, for the rest of his life.

He saw fire.

A full white Ash, enormous, filling half the sky. A dune higher than any dune he had ever seen. And heat, inside him, rising up his throat like something climbing out. He could feel his own skin tightening. He could feel the fine hairs on his arms curl and shrivel. He could taste honey, and under the honey, smoke.

It lasted less than a breath. Then he was sitting in the sand of Harrow's End, gasping, his hands slapping at his own arms to put out a fire that wasn't there.

He thought it was a trick of a tired mind. He would learn otherwise. I sat beside him in the sand and didn't say a word, and I counted, as I always do, and the number was still twelve.

---

The door of the well-keeper's house opened.

Hadric stood there, a lamp in one hand and a carving knife in the other, his face white. Behind him, peeking around his legs, was Bram's little brother, a boy of six or seven, his face streaked with tears.

Around the lane, other doors were opening. A light bright as midday had come through the shutters and brought the neighbours out.

They looked at the lane. At the empty sand where twenty Husks had stood, now marked with footprints that all faced the same way. At Bram, still standing there, blinking slowly, one side of his face grey as ash.

And at Zayn, sitting against the wall with golden cracks glowing in his palms.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Bram turned his head and looked down at Zayn. His eyes were clearing. What the Husk had taken was seeping back. I saw him remembering the shovel, the door, his brother crying. I saw him remembering the light.

He opened his mouth.

I like to think, even now, that it might have been *thank you*.

But he never got the chance, because that was when I decided to open *my* mouth.

---

"Well," I said loudly, from the top of the well-keeper's garden wall. "*That* was overdue."

Every head in the lane turned toward me, including Zayn's.

He stared up at me. His mouth fell open. For fifteen years he'd told himself the voice was loneliness, or the wind. And now forty people were staring at the same fox, their faces as white as the Ash.

They had heard it too.

"Oh, don't look at me like that," I said to the lane in general. "You've all seen worse tonight. Twenty dead neighbours with good manners, a boy who glows, half your village the colour of old porridge. A talking fox is honestly the *least* of your problems. I'd go and check the names above your doors, if I were you. Count them. Then count them again in the morning."

My tail was glowing bright as a lamp. Everything I'd said was perfectly true.

Somebody screamed.

I think it was Garrem. It was a very high scream for such a large man.

And then everyone was shouting at once. Oszkar came barrelling down the lane in his nightshirt bellowing for order. Through all of it, Zayn sat in the sand against the wall and stared up at me with his glowing hands in his lap.

"You," he said. His voice was barely a whisper. "You can… you *talk*."

"I've always talked," I said. "You just never listened properly. Most people don't. It's one of the great tragedies of the world. Not the greatest. I'll get to that."

"*Why didn't you tell me?*"

I hopped down from the wall and sat beside him in the sand. For a moment I didn't say anything. I looked at the golden cracks in his palms, and at the mark on his chest, still faintly warm under his shirt. I looked at his face. It was very young, and very frightened.

I have sat beside eleven children like this. I have watched the moment when they first understood that their life was not going to be the life they'd planned. One of them laughed. The Falconer asked me, very politely, whether he could go home now.

It doesn't get easier. You'd think it would.

"Because," I said finally, quietly, so only he could hear, "once I told you, it would all start. And I wanted you to have a little longer before it did."

He looked at me.

My tail glowed steady and gold in the dark.

---

Somewhere at the edge of the village, out where the grey was thickest, I heard a sound that made the fur stand up along my spine.

It was a child's voice. Small and confused, and calling out into the empty night.

"Mama?" it said. "Bram? Where… where is everyone? Who…"

A pause.

"Who am I?"

And then, from all around it, very softly, from the grey shapes standing out there in the dark, a dozen voices that were learning, said it back.

*"Who am I?"*

*"Who am I?"*

*"Who am I?"*

Beside me, Bram had gone completely still.

Because his little brother, the one who had been crying behind the door, wasn't behind the door any more. In all the shouting, he had slipped out into the lane to find his big brother.

And he had walked straight into the grey.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
