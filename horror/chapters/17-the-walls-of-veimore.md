# Chapter Seventeen
## The Walls of Veimore

> *Behind every wall burns a lantern; behind every lantern, a fear; beneath every street, a knock. Do not answer it.*
> — a saying of the lantern-keepers of Veimore

---

Veimore was red.

Not the warm red of a sunset. The dark, wet red of something that has been bleeding a long time and has forgotten how to stop. The whole city was built of the same blood-coloured stone, and when the Fire sank behind it, the walls didn't reflect the light. They drank it. In the evenings, if you put your paw against them, they were damp, though it hadn't rained in Veimore in living memory.

People call it the City of the Dead and Darkness. Desert people are dramatic, but Veimore people are only accurate. For as long as anyone remembers, the dead of the whole desert have been carried there on slow camels, wrapped in white, and laid in the catacombs beneath its streets. Above them, on every street, the living paint the names of the dead on shrine walls, so that no one is forgotten.

The names don't stay.

That's the part the songs leave out. The paint fades. Not over years. Over days. A name painted at dawn is pale by sunset and gone in a week, as if something on the other side of the stone were slowly drinking it. So Veimore has name-painters the way other cities have water-carriers: hundreds of pale, quiet men with pots of red paint, working the streets day and night, going over and over the letters. Some names have been painted so many times the stone has worn into grooves, and still they fade.

And the lanterns. Thousands of them, on the walls, the rooftops, in every window and under every arch, burning day and night, because the people of Veimore believe that the moment the last lantern goes out, the darkness will walk in. Their smoke hangs over the city so that even at noon the sky is the colour of an old bruise.

We lay at the top of the last dune and watched the grey press against the walls as night came on. It didn't come in. It sat just beyond the lantern-light, patient as a dog outside a butcher's door.

"It's waiting," said Zayn.

"Everything out here is waiting," I said. "That's what the desert is for."

I want you to see him properly, because there isn't much time left to look. He was thinner than in Harrow's End, his hair bleached nearly white. Golden cracks ran along both forearms and up his neck, fading so slowly now that the old ones hadn't gone before the new ones came,. The words on his arm, *DON'T FORGET HIS EYES*, had scabbed over. I'd caught him reading them with a puzzled frown, like a stranger's letter. Soren's broken sword was strapped across his back, the proof inside the hilt. He touched it every hundred breaths, the way he touched his mark.

Behind us, Daruun stood by the pale camel, and I could feel him not saying things. It ran in the family.

"I'll stay out here," he said. "The Unnamed won't come through the gates like honest men. I'll see them first." His voice snagged on the name, the way it still did. "And Zayn. Come back out to me. After. Promise me."

The boy was quiet for a long time.

You know why. He *couldn't* promise. He'd started waking in the night beating at his own chest, at flames that weren't there, the smell of scorched cloth in his nose. If he'd said *I promise*, the mark would have burned him like a coal.

"I'll try," he said.

My tail glowed. It was completely true. It was nothing like enough.

Daruun rode away along the hollow between the dunes, and the wind smoothed out his tracks behind him, the way it does for things the desert intends to keep.

---

Every guard on every gate knew what to look for. *A pale-haired boy, a fox with odd eyes, a broken blade. The Prince-Killer.*

So we didn't use the gates.

"He told me about it," Zayn said. We were crouched in the reeds where the oasis water slid out under the wall through a low arch furred black with moss. "An old water channel. It runs under the city and comes up beneath the palace kitchens. He used to sneak out through it."

"And back in? For what?"

Zayn smiled, small and crooked and full of grief. "Honey cakes."

The water was waist-deep on Zayn, which made it considerably more on me. I rode his shoulder. His mark glowed through his wet shirt and gave us just enough light to see by.

Which was a pity, because then we could see the walls.

Halfway along, the channel ran through the catacombs. The stone on either side was cut into shelves going up into the dark and on into the dark, and on every shelf lay a long white shape, wrapped and still. Above each, a name in faded red. Down here there were no painters. Down here the names had been left to do what names in Veimore do, and most were nearly gone: a ghost of a letter, a smear like a thumbprint.

And the catacombs breathed.

I don't mean the air moved. I mean it went *in*, all of it, down the length of the tunnel, slow and steady, pulling at the fur on my face. A long pause. Then it came back out, warm, smelling of old linen and something sweet underneath, like fruit gone soft in a closed jar. The water rose a finger's width against Zayn's chest on the in-breath and sank on the out.

He read some of the names as we passed. A boy who's only just learned to read reads everything.

"*Hamid*," he whispered. "*Little Dara. Joss the potter. Sefa, beloved.*" As he said *Sefa*, the last red letters slid down the stone like a drop of water and were gone. He stopped. "Did I do that?"

"No," I said. My tail glowed. "They do that by themselves."

Where the channel bent there was an alcove, older than the rest, with eleven shelves in it. Small ones. Too short for a grown man. Child-length. Empty. Above them no names at all, only eleven little painted feathers, so old they were barely brown.

Zayn stood in the water and looked at the eleven short shelves for a long time.

"Kiri," he said. "Who paints the names of the bearers?"

I didn't answer. There was nothing true I could have said that he needed to hear. I'll tell you, though, because you'll need it. Nobody does. Nobody ever has. The desert remembers the *bearers*. Not the children.

Then, from somewhere under us, under the water and the stone floor of the channel, came a knock.

Three knocks. Slow. Patient. The way you'd knock on the door of someone you knew was home.

Neither of us moved. The catacombs drew in a long breath and held it.

The knocking didn't come again. After a while the air went out, warm and sweet, and Zayn walked on much faster, and didn't read any more names.

The channel ended in slippery steps and a wooden hatch. Zayn pushed it up an inch, and through the gap came a smell so wonderful I nearly fell off his shoulder: bread, onions, cinnamon, and honey.

He climbed out into a storeroom full of sacks and jars and hanging garlic, and found himself face to face with a cleaver.

---

The woman holding it was the largest person I had seen in some centuries.

Nearly as tall as Daruun and twice as wide, with forearms like a blacksmith's and a face as round and red as one of the city's bricks. She held the cleaver close to the hatch, at the height of a head coming up out of it, the way you would if you'd been waiting.

"Back down," she said. Her voice could have stripped paint. "Whatever you are. I heard the knocking. You've got a count of three to show me you're breathing on your own—"

Then she saw the sword. The wrapping had come loose in the channel, and the lamplight fell across the hilt.

The cleaver dropped an inch.

"Where," she said, in a completely different voice, "did you get that?"

Zayn stood dripping on her clean floor and told her the truth, because it was the only thing he had ever known how to do.

"It was Soren's. He was my friend. I didn't kill him."

I sat on a sack of lentils and let my tail light up gold, so bright the jars threw shadows.

"His tail does that when someone's telling the truth," Zayn said, a little apologetically.

"It also does it when it's warm and dry," I said. "Which it isn't. I've been waist-deep in your dead. Thank you for asking."

The woman shrieked. Then she clapped a hand over her mouth, and her eyes went wide, and then wet. The cleaver rang on the stone.

"I knew it," she whispered through her fingers. "I *knew* it. I told them, and nobody listens to the cook."

She was Mother Yusa. She'd run the palace kitchens for thirty years and half-raised the king's two sons, because the king was busy being a king and somebody had to make sure the boys ate. She told us this while she sat Zayn on a crate and put lentils and honey cakes in front of him.

"That boy," she said, pointing a floury finger at the hilt as if Soren himself were inside it, "stole honey cakes from my kitchen for ten years. Ten years! Came up out of that drain smelling of the dead, every single time, and thought I didn't know." Her chin wobbled. She steadied it. "A boy like that doesn't kill anyone. And neither do you. Eat."

Zayn ate slowly, the way he'd eaten Auntie Sabba's bundles. When he got to the honey cakes, he held one in his palm.

"He told me about these," he said. "He said nobody in the world made them like you."

Mother Yusa turned round to stir a pot that didn't need stirring, and her big shoulders shook for a while.

---

She hid us behind the oil jars in the back storeroom, great clay things taller than Zayn.

"Don't knock them," she said. "That's three months' light for this whole wing. People in this city have been killed for less oil than you could carry in your mouth."

She wasn't exaggerating. On our way to the walls I'd seen the oil-sellers at the outer gate with their little brass spoons, measuring it out a thumb at a time to silent, grey-faced queues. A thumb of oil for a day's water. In Veimore they don't say *I'd give my right arm*. They say *I'd give my oil*, and they mean it the way other people mean blood.

The king did not eat, she told us, as if that were the worst of it. Since the news of Soren's death he'd sent back every plate, and the only person he would listen to was his vizier.

"Orrin," said Zayn. She spat into the fire.

"Grey eyes. Soft voice. You never hear him coming. He's brought the king Soren's ring. Said he'd taken it back from the Prince-Killer's own hand, in some oasis town. Proof, he called it, that the glowing boy robbed our Soren's body." She looked at Zayn sideways. "The king wears it on a chain now. Over his heart."

Zayn's hand had gone to the hilt.

"They painted Soren's name on the shrine wall in the palace court," she said, lower. "Under the traitors. Orrin's idea. And the painters aren't allowed to touch it up. So it's fading. Every day there's a bit less of him." Her voice thinned. "And the little one, Idan, sits in front of it every night at sunset. He stole a pot of red paint from the painters. He goes over the letters with his finger. He doesn't say a word."

Then she asked the question I'd been dreading.

"How did he die? Our Soren. Did it… did it hurt him?"

Zayn opened his mouth and closed it. He didn't know, and it had been cutting him every night for a turn of the Ash.

"I wasn't there," he said. "I should have been."

My tail glowed. I looked at the floor.

Mother Yusa looked at me. She was a woman who'd spent thirty years knowing which kitchen boy had broken a jar by the set of his shoulders. She didn't ask me anything.

I was grateful. I was ashamed of being grateful.

I'll tell you about that night soon, little one. Not yet. Let me be a coward a little longer.

---

Idan came down after midnight.

Bare feet on the back stairs, and then someone very carefully lifting the lid off the honey-cake jar, exactly the way someone else had probably taught him.

Zayn stood up behind the oil jars. His mark glowed.

The boy froze with a cake halfway to his mouth. He was ten, small for it, with his brother's dark eyes and none of his swagger, and red paint dried under every fingernail, as if he'd been scratching at a wall. The cake fell out of his hand.

"That's Soren's," he whispered.

"Yes."

"They said you killed him."

"I didn't."

Gold, steady and warm, from the tip of my tail. Idan looked at it.

"Your brother tried to eat a scorpion once," I told him. "Was sick for a whole day. Turned the colour of a lizard. I've never been prouder of anyone."

I've outlived too many children to be good with them. But Idan made a small cracked sound, and it took me a moment to understand it was a laugh.

Then he sat down on the floor and cried, and Zayn sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, the way you'd sit beside someone at a fire to share the warmth.

"His name's going," Idan said into his knees. "I painted it three times today and at dinner the S was gone again." He looked up. "Is that what happens? If nobody remembers you hard enough?"

Zayn didn't answer. Under the floor, very faintly, something knocked three times, and the boy didn't even flinch. He'd grown up with it.

Later, Zayn took out the letter. It was soft from handling, the grey wax still holding its little curling mark.

"You can read?" said Idan.

"Your brother taught me. In the sand. With a stick." Zayn touched the letters as if they were still warm. "He was a bad teacher. He kept laughing."

Idan read it by the light of Zayn's mark. I watched his face change halfway down. *On the Night of Embers, when every lantern in Veimore is lit, the king will die. When he falls, put out the lanterns. Then the city.*

"The Night of Embers is tomorrow," Idan said. "And the Ash will be full."

Zayn touched his mark and winced. It was hot. It was hot all the time now.

"Father hears petitions in the morning," Idan said. "I could take you in."

---

The throne hall at the heart of the black-and-red palace had no windows. It was lit only by lanterns, hundreds of them, hanging on long chains from a ceiling so high and dark they looked like Embers in a starless sky. The smoke was thick enough to taste. Every sound went up into the shadows and came back softer and slightly wrong, a word you hadn't said, a cough a beat late, as though the dark were listening and practising.

At the far end, seven steps rose to a chair of black rock, and in it sat the saddest man I have ever seen.

King Absalon had been tall and broad once. You could see the shape of it, the way you see the shape of a house after the roof has gone. A heavy ring hung on a thin gold chain at his neck, and every few breaths his hand drifted up to touch it.

One step below the throne stood Orrin. Silver hair, smooth face, soft robes the colour of sand. His faded grey eyes found Zayn the instant the boy stepped out from behind Idan.

He smiled.

Not the smile of a man who's won. A small one. The smile of a man who sets a trap and comes back in the morning to find the fox in it, exactly where he'd pictured it.

"The Prince-Killer!" someone shouted, and then everyone was shouting. Spears came down. A courtier shrieked "*Monster!*" in a voice like a goose, and I saw the gold on Zayn's skin flicker grey for a breath.

"*Stop!*" Idan had planted himself in front of Zayn with his arms out, shaking. "Father, he has something for you. You have to *listen*."

The king looked at his son a long time. Then he lifted one tired hand, and the spears went up.

Zayn climbed to the bottom step and read the letter aloud. Every word. His voice didn't shake. Soren had made him practise until it didn't.

When he finished, the hall was so quiet I could hear the lanterns hissing, and under the floor, far down, the slow breathing of the catacombs.

"That is Vizier Orrin's seal," said Zayn. "Soren took this from one of the Unnamed and hid it in his sword. They killed him before he could bring it home."

The king looked at the seal for a long time. Then at Orrin.

And Orrin sighed, gently, like a teacher whose favourite pupil has made a small mistake.

"Your Majesty will remember that I reported my seal stolen on the night of the prince's exile, and had a new one cut. It is in the palace records." He spread his soft hands. "I did not wish to believe Soren had taken it. But here it is. A forgery in a broken sword, carried by the boy who killed him, on the very eve of the Night of Embers. It is clever. I'll grant them that."

Long before Soren found that letter, Orrin had made sure that if it was ever found, it would look like a lie. 

"Did you kill Soren?" said Zayn. It came out of him the way the Light did, without permission.

Orrin looked down at him, soft and sad. "I loved that boy," he said, "as if he were my own son."

From the cord at Zayn's belt, Hesset's little bronze truth bell rang.

In that hall it sounded like a stone dropped down a well. Every head turned. And up in the dark above the lanterns, the hall rang it back, faint and wrong, a dozen tiny bells that weren't there.

"A desert trinket," said Orrin, without looking. "Bells ring when boys shake them, Majesty."

"He didn't shake it," said Idan.

"I didn't kill your son," Zayn said to the king, very low. "I left him alone that night. We argued, and I walked away, and when I came back he was gone. That's the worst thing I've ever done. But I didn't kill him."

The mark on his chest didn't burn. My tail blazed so bright the nearest courtiers shaded their eyes.

I watched the king waver, like a dune shifting at night. Believing us meant believing he had exiled his son for a lie and worn his ring over his heart because the man who killed him had handed it to him. No father can look at that straight on. Not all at once.

"Hold the boy," King Absalon said at last. "In the east tower, under guard. After the Night of Embers, I will hear this again. From both of you." He looked at Orrin. "Both."

"Of course, Majesty," said Orrin, and bowed. "Shall we cancel the festival? For safety?"

The king closed his eyes. "No. The people need their lanterns."

As the guards took Zayn's arms, I saw the vizier's face, for an instant, when he thought no one was looking.

He was still smiling.

---

The tower room was small and round, with a window facing east. They took the sword and the bell. Nobody had worked out how you arrest a fox.

The Fire went down. All over the city, doors were barred, hundreds of bolts sliding home one after another like a great slow clock. Then the whispering began from every rooftop and courtyard: the people of Veimore reading the names of their dead off the walls before the paint could fade any further. *Hamid. Dara. Joss. Sefa.* And under the whispering, all across the city, so faint you'd think it was your own heart, the knocking. Three. Pause. Three. From under every street at once.

"They're answering," Zayn said at the window. "Something down there's answering the names."

I didn't tell him he was wrong. My tail wouldn't have let me.

The Ash came up over the dunes. One more night and it would be perfectly round.

A soft sound at the door. Not a guard. Guards stamp.

Orrin's face appeared at the little barred window in the wood. In the dark his eyes looked almost white, like a Husk's.

"Thank you," he said gently, "for coming. I've wondered for a turn of the Ash how to bring you inside these walls on the Night of Embers, under the full Ash. And you came by yourself, up through the dead, with a letter in your hand." He sounded almost fond. "The desert really does write the sands, doesn't it? It wants you here as much as I do. Ask yourself why, bearer."

Then, softer still: "Tomorrow night, the lanterns go out."

My tail glowed bright gold in that little room, and Zayn saw it, and I saw him understand. Everything Orrin had said was true. That was what made it so terrible.

The footsteps went away down the stair.

Zayn went to the window, and I hopped onto the sill. Below, the city lay under its haze, ten thousand small flames holding back the dark, each one bought a thumb at a time. And on the roof opposite, at the very edge of the lantern-glow, a figure in a grey robe crouched with two grey blades across his knees. His mask was a smooth white face with a crack running down the middle, brow to chin.

He was looking up at our window.

And very faintly, under the whispering of all those names and the knocking beneath them, so faint I might have been the only one in the world who could hear it, he was humming.

---

*End of Chapter Seventeen*
