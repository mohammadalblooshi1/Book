# Chapter Eighteen
## The Night of Embers

> *On the Night of Embers, every lantern is a name. When the lanterns go out, so do the names.*
> — painted above the shrine gate of the palace court, Veimore; repainted daily

---

Once a year, on the night the Ash comes up perfectly round, the whole desert lights lanterns for the lost bearers.

You'll see it soon enough. In the small villages it's a few clay lamps on doorsteps. Everyone goes to bed feeling vaguely holy and slightly relieved, the way you feel after a funeral that wasn't yours.

In Veimore they did it the Veimore way. Every family carried one extra lantern through the streets at dusk, filled with oil they had saved for a year, a thumb at a time, and hung it on the shrine walls where the names of the dead were painted, so that for one night the names glowed and nothing could drink them. And on the great black stairs of the palace, eleven bronze lanterns of coloured glass were set out in a row. One for each bearer who had come before.

I sat on the sill of the tower window and watched them being lit, one by one, by a priest with a long taper.

The Shepherd. The Weaver's Daughter. The Smith. The Twin Who Stayed. The Last King.

I knew every one of them. I knew which ones had wet themselves on the last night and been ashamed, and which ones had cried for their mothers. The Boy of Salt was nine. Nobody in that city knew any of their names. That's the joke. Not one person down there could tell you who they were remembering, or how old, or what they'd smelled like burning.

The Stone Singer. The Boy of Salt. The Falconer. The Blind One. The Dreamer. The Runner.

Eleven.

Behind me, Zayn sat against the wall with his eyes shut. He wasn't asleep. The mark was glowing steadily, brighter than I'd ever seen it, as though it knew what night it was and was looking forward to it.

"How many?" he asked.

"Eleven," I said.

He was quiet a moment.

"There should be another one," he said. "Tomorrow."

I didn't say anything. My tail said it for me.

---

The first lantern went out just after the Ash cleared the walls.

It was on the far western wall, one tiny flame among thousands, and then it simply wasn't there. Then the one beside it. Then the next. A gap of darkness opened along the top of the wall, small at first, then spreading, like a black thread being drawn slowly through a golden cloth.

Down in the city, the whispering stopped.

Every night of their lives, the people of Veimore had read the names of their dead into the dark. They had never once stopped. Now, all at once, ten thousand voices went quiet, and ten thousand faces turned west toward the black gap in the wall.

In the silence, from under every street in the city, came the knocking.

Not three knocks. Not patient. Hundreds of fists, all at once, hammering on the underside of the city like people trapped beneath ice.

Then somebody screamed, and every lantern on the southern wall went out together.

"Kiri," said Zayn. He was on his feet.

"I see it."

There were shapes on the walls. Grey robes, white masks. The Unnamed walked the battlements in a line, and as they passed each lantern they cupped a hand over it, almost tenderly, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes, and it died. They were humming the wordless chant from the ship.

And where the light died, the grey came in.

It poured over the top of the walls like fog over a cliff edge and slid down into the streets. Where it touched the shrine walls, the names didn't fade. They *ran*. The red paint went liquid and slid down the stone in long streaks, a hundred years of names bleeding off the walls at once into the gutters, and the grey lapped them up as they went.

Then the grates in the streets began to lift.

The iron grates over the catacomb shafts. All over the city they rose, slowly, pushed from below, and out of them came hands. Then arms. Then figures. Grey, white-eyed, wrapped in rotted white linen that trailed behind them. They had been down there a long time, knocking, waiting to be let in.

The people of Veimore saw them climbing out of the ground in grave-wrappings and thought their dead were rising.

Some of them were right.

That was when the real screaming started.

---

Someone was at the door.

There had been two guards on the stair, and I'd heard nothing from them for some time. The bolt slid back. The door swung open.

Brother Seven stood in the doorway.

Through the crack in his mask I could see a thin line of his real face: the edge of one pale eye, a stripe of cheek so white it had never seen the Fire.

He had Soren's sword in one hand and Hesset's bell in the other.

He held them out.

Neither brother moved. The only sound was the screaming below and the knocking under it.

"Tamir," said Zayn.

The figure flinched as if struck. The sword and the bell clattered to the floor. He stepped back, then again, and his hands came up to his mask and gripped the edges of it, and pulled. I saw the skin of his jaw stretch where the mask's rim met it. It didn't come away. It had been on fifteen years. It had grown there. He let go, shaking, with a sound like a hurt dog.

He hummed two notes. Just two. Not the cult's chant. The beginning of the phoenix song.

Then he turned and ran.

Zayn picked up the sword. His hands were trembling.

"He's still in there," he said.

"Yes," I said. My tail glowed. I'd known since the first night he let Zayn go. I put that on the pile with the rest.

---

The streets were full of grey.

We ran down through them into the lanes where the families had gone to hang their lanterns. Most of the lanterns were out. The ones still burning lay smashed on the stones, oil spreading in burning pools, and people were on their knees beside them scooping the burning oil up in their bare hands to save it, screaming as they did it, and not stopping.

And everywhere, Husks.

They moved through the crowds slowly, reaching out, touching. Every touch, someone stopped running. Stood in the street with their hands at their sides and their eyes going white and lost. And then, a moment later, the emptied ones began to speak. Each of them said the last thing they had said before the touch. Over and over, in a flat, pleasant voice, smiling with faces that didn't know how to smile anymore.

*Bar the door. Bar the door. Bar the door.*

*Mama, I'm here. Mama, I'm here.*

*Where's the oil, where's the oil, where's the oil.*

At a shrine wall halfway down the lane, a little girl stood alone. She couldn't have been more than six. A Husk had touched her cheek, and the grey was spreading from it across her face like a stain through cloth, and she was looking up at the wall where her family's names had been. There was nothing on it now but long red streaks running down into the gutter.

"Whose names were those?" she said to no one. "I can't… whose were they?"

Then she said it again, in exactly the same voice. And again. The grey had reached the corner of her mouth.

Zayn was on his knees in front of her before I could stop him.

"You'll bring every Hound in the city down on us," I said.

"Yes," said Zayn, and put his hands on her face.

The light came gold. It always did, by then, when it was for someone else. It poured over her cheek, and the grey shrank back from it like frost from the Fire. Her eyes cleared. On the wall behind her, a single name glowed faint and gold for a moment where the paint had run. *Mira.* Her grandmother. Then it faded. But the girl remembered it. I watched her remember.

And Zayn saw.

Her past: a woman's wrinkled hands braiding her hair. Her future: a grown girl reading the names aloud on this same wall.

And then his own death came, the way it always came now. Not a picture anymore. A place he was *in*.

The full Ash, close enough to touch. The cold sand of the Crown Dune under his bare feet. And the heat. Starting in his chest, under the mark, the way a coal starts in kindling. He felt his skin split along the golden lines. He smelled it: hair, cloth, and something under them that was himself. A small boy's hand slipping out of his. His father on his knees in the sand. Grey hands plunging into the gold. Letters, far below, moving across the dunes, a word he could almost read. And behind the letters, something else, at the very edge of the world, something that was watching the fire the way a man watches a pot come to the boil.

He came back screaming.

He'd never done that before. He knelt on the stones with his hands beating at his own chest, at flames that weren't there, and the little girl stared at him, and I put my paws on his face and said his name until he stopped. The golden cracks had spread along his jaw. There was a smell of scorching coming off his shirt. Real scorching. The cloth over his mark had browned in the shape of a feather.

The girl's mother found her and dragged her away into the dark without a word.

"I saw a word," Zayn said. His voice was raw. "In the sand. And something past it."

"Later," I said, because across the lane three Hounds had come out of the grey with their ember eyes fixed on him, and their lips were peeling back, and the one in front was saying *Zayn, Zayn* in a little wet child's voice.

---

They never reached us.

A spear came out of the dark and took the first through the chest, and it burst into black sand. The pale camel came down the lane like a ghost, and on its back was Daruun, his veil torn half away, his spear already swinging for the second. He was always going to come. Fifteen years late, but always.

"*The palace!*" he shouted. "They're inside! Go!"

The third Hound sprang at Zayn's throat.

It met me instead.

I don't like to talk about the other shape. But it happened on that street, in front of a dozen people of Veimore, and I didn't care who saw. I broke out of my small fox body like a coal bursting from a fire. I was huge and black and burning, my eyes were embers, and the silver half of me that belongs to the Hollow howled with joy. That's the part I hate. Not the size. The joy. The Hound came apart in my jaws like smoke, and it tasted like home.

The people in the lane screamed at me the way they'd screamed at the things in the grates. They weren't wrong to.

The old burn on my side, where Zayn's light had caught me in the storm, tore open.

Behind us, the grey was rolling down the lane like a wave, full of smiling faces.

"Go," I said to Zayn. My voice shook the shutters. "I'll hold them. *Go.*"

He looked at me. At the monster I was, the thing I'd hidden from him and from every bearer before him.

He wasn't afraid of me. He hadn't been for a long time. I hadn't noticed when it changed.

"Don't die," he said.

"I'm a great deal harder to kill than you," I said. "That's sort of the whole problem."

He ran.

---

The palace stairs were black stone, wide enough for twenty men abreast, rising in one long climb from the court to the great doors. The eleven bronze lanterns stood along them. Seven had gone out. The other four guttered, throwing coloured light across the steps: red, then green, then gold. In the court below, Soren's name was gone from the shrine wall, the stone perfectly clean, Idan's little pot of paint smashed beneath it.

Brother One was waiting at the top.

He was the largest man I've ever seen, and I had recently met Mother Yusa. He stood before the doors with his feet planted wide and a grey blade as long as Zayn was tall. His mask was smooth and white and perfect. He had never once, in all his years at Orrin's side, been heard to speak.

He didn't speak now. He came down the stairs.

Zayn met him halfway.

The first blow would have taken Zayn's head if he hadn't dropped flat onto the steps; it struck the stone and threw sparks. The second came straight down. Zayn rolled, and the edge skimmed across his shoulder.

It didn't cut skin. Grey blades don't need to. But the light dimmed where it touched, and Soren's face was gone.

Not blurred. Gone. He knew he'd had a friend. He knew the friend had been important, more important than anyone. He reached for the face and found a blank, smooth space where it should have been, as smooth as the mask above him. He reached for the eyes, for *DON'T FORGET HIS EYES*, and there was nothing to not forget.

He lay on the stairs and couldn't remember, and worse, he could feel himself beginning not to mind.

Then, out of the grey, a voice. Not the face. Just a voice, laughing at him by a fire a long way back.

*A short blade means you have to get close. Most people are afraid to get close. So be the one who isn't.*

Soren had said that on the third night, waving half a sword and showing off. Zayn had rolled his eyes. He remembered rolling his eyes. And from that one small, stupid memory the rest came back, all at once, like water into a footprint: the scarred jaw, the crooked grin, the dark eyes always laughing. *Dark.* His eyes were dark. *There* you are.

Zayn got up.

Brother One swung again, wide, to keep him away. Zayn didn't go away. He went *in*. Under the swing, up inside it, so close the long grey blade was useless, so close he could hear the man breathing behind the mask, quick and shallow, like someone very frightened.

He raised Soren's broken sword.

And the light came.

Down his arm, into the hilt, along the broken blade, and where the steel ended it kept going. For one heartbeat Soren's sword was whole again, the missing half made of gold as bright as the Fire. It met the grey blade where hilt joined steel.

The grey blade shattered.

The pieces fell down the black stairs, ringing like bells. Brother One stumbled. His heel caught the edge of a step, and he fell, a long way, all the way down to the bottom, where he lay still beside the last guttering lantern.

His mask had cracked in half. Under it was a tired grey-bearded man with his eyes closed. He was breathing. His lips were moving. He was trying, I think, to say a name. He'd been silent so long he'd forgotten how.

Zayn stood on the stairs with the golden half-blade fading in his hand.

Then, from inside the throne hall, a child screamed.

---

The great doors stood open, and inside, the dark had won.

All those hundreds of lanterns on their chains had gone out but one. It hung high above the throne, a single small flame swinging slowly, and by its light I could see grey robes and white masks standing in rows along the walls. Unnamed. Dozens of them. Humming. And at the top of the seven steps, beside the black stone throne, Orrin.

He had Idan.

He held the boy against his chest with one arm, quite gently, the way you'd hold a sleepy child. In his other hand was a grey blade, small and thin as a reed.

And on the steps below him lay the king.

Zayn ran to him. Nobody stopped him. That was the worst of it.

King Absalon lay on his back on the black steps with one hand still stretched toward his son. There was no blood. Grey blades don't do blood. The grey was spreading out from his chest like ink through water, and as it spread, it took him. His beard was going white in streaks. The colour was leaving his lips. His eyes were open, and they were fading from the edges in, the brown draining out of them toward the middle.

"He stepped in front," said Orrin softly, from above. "The blade was meant for the boy, to make him easier to carry. His Majesty stepped in front of it. I didn't expect that. He hasn't moved quickly in years."

Zayn put his hands on the king's chest.

The light came, gold, and the grey drank it. That's what a grey blade does. It was like pouring water into sand. He pushed harder, and harder, until the cracks on his own skin blazed and split wider and began, at the edges, to smoke. The king's eyes cleared for a moment.

And Zayn saw. The king, young, swinging a small dark-haired boy onto his shoulders. The same boy dragged out of this hall in chains while the king didn't look up. Then no future. Only dark.

And then his own death again, and this time he didn't scream, because he didn't have the breath.

"Soren," the king whispered. "I didn't listen to him. I should have… listened."

"I know," said Zayn.

"Tell him." The king's hand fumbled at the chain around his neck and found the ring. He pulled, and the chain broke, and he pressed the ring into Zayn's palm. "Tell him I'm sorry. And give this to Idan. When he's king."

And Zayn said, "I'll tell him."

My tail glowed.

I was in the doorway by then, small again, limping, bleeding from the old burn. I saw my tail light up bright gold in that dark hall, and I understood what it meant, and I wanted to be sick. The boy couldn't lie. He believed, with his whole heart, that before the night was over he would see Soren to tell him.

The king's eyes went past Zayn to his younger son. He smiled at Idan, a small, broken, loving smile, and the grey reached his eyes as he did it, and the smile stayed on his face after the rest of him had gone. It stayed there, fixed and pleasant, a Husk's smile on a king's mouth.

Idan stopped screaming.

---

"How touching," said Orrin.

High above the throne, the last lantern in the hall went out.

In the dark, Zayn's light was the only light. It came off him in waves, and it wasn't gold anymore. It had gone grey, dim and flickering, because he was angrier than I had ever seen him, angry enough to tear that hall down stone by stone and the people in it.

That was what Orrin wanted. The light goes grey when the bearer hates. Grey light is easy to take.

"*Zayn,*" I said. "*Don't.*"

He heard me. He shut his eyes and breathed, and slowly the grey went out of him. Not all the way. Enough.

When he opened them, Orrin was at the back of the hall, by the open door to the eastern balconies, with Idan under one arm. Beyond came the hiss of sand lizards in the court and the howling of Hounds.

Idan wasn't crying. He wasn't making any sound at all. He was humming. Two notes. The chant. Orrin had been holding him for only a few minutes, and already he was humming.

"The Crown Dune, bearer," said Orrin. His soft voice filled the dark the way it always had. "Under the full Ash. You know the way. You've been there every time you've closed your eyes. Come and give me your name. Or the boy will give me his. He's already halfway to forgetting it."

Then he was gone, and the masks along the walls went with him like smoke.

By the time we reached the balcony, the lizards were out through the eastern gate and running, a dark knot of riders with the Hounds loping around them. Orrin rode at the front with Idan before him. And at the very back, a lone rider with twin blades turned in his saddle and looked up at the palace.

He looked a long time. Then he rode after the others.

---

We stood on the eastern balcony, the boy and the fox, and a moment later Daruun came up the stairs behind us with grey dust on his clothes and his spear dark to the elbow. Below us the city was black. Every lantern was out. In the streets, the Husks had stopped moving, and stopped speaking. They stood in the lanes like statues, every white face turned the same way.

East.

We turned to look.

The desert had moved in the night.

It stood a little way out from the walls, so close you could have walked there before the Ash went down. A dune, higher than any dune in the world. Higher than the mountains where Daruun had hidden for fifteen years. It rose out of the flat sand in one long, impossible curve, and as I watched, the sand on its flanks moved. Slowly. Up. Like the side of something breathing. At its very top, white and enormous and perfectly round, sat the full Ash.

The Crown Dune.

It had come to fetch him. Things that are hungry enough don't wait for their food to walk.

Daruun made a sound beside me that I have heard only a few times in all my life. It's the sound a father makes when he understands he can't stop what is coming, and that when he ran from it at sixteen, all he did was keep it waiting for his son.

Zayn looked down at the ring in his hand. Soren's ring. He closed his fingers round it and put it on the cord at his neck, beside the infinity knot. His knuckles were cracked with gold. Where his fingers touched the ring, it smoked.

"It's time," he said.

Neither of us answered. There was nothing left that was true and kind at the same time.

---

*End of Chapter Eighteen*
