# Chapter Eight
## The Singing Wells

> *Drink from the wells, and the past will drink from you. It is always thirstier than you are.*
> — a saying of the water-finders, older than any town that uses it

---

The Ash was thin the night we found the Singing Wells.

Thin as the smile of a trader who has just cheated you and would like you to come back tomorrow. Thin as a cut before it starts to bleed. It hung low over the western dunes, a pale scratch on the dark, and gave almost no light at all.

That suited Zayn. He'd had enough of being seen.

He had barely spoken since the beast came down out of the black sky and the caravan was simply *gone*. Since he'd held up a bubble of gold with sixty empty faces pressed against it, and dug one boy out of the sand with his bare hands, and poured the Light into him until his own arms cracked, and the boy had opened his eyes and looked at those glowing hands and said the word.

The cracks were still fading on his forearms, thin lines of dull honey under the skin. Every so often he'd look down at them and then look away fast. Every so often he'd lift his wrist to his nose and sniff it, and I'd pretend not to see.

"You can talk to me, you know," I said. "I'm told I'm an excellent listener."

"Who told you that?"

"No one. Everyone I've listened to is dead. I was hoping you'd be the first to say it out loud."

The corner of his mouth moved. It wasn't a smile, but I've worked with less. I've worked with skulls.

His water skin had been empty since the afternoon before. His lips had split. He kept his hand pressed to the feather mark as though it were the only thing holding him upright, and once, in the dark, I heard him say very softly, "*Mama, it's dark*," and then stop, appalled, as if someone else had used his mouth.

Then he stopped walking.

"Do you hear that?" he said.

I did. I'd been hearing it for some time and hoping he wouldn't.

It was a hum. Low and steady and very large, as if the whole desert were humming to itself with its mouth closed. When Zayn stood still you could feel it come up through the soles of your feet and into your teeth, and then further, into the bones of your skull, until it was hard to tell whether the hum was outside you or whether you'd always been humming and had only just noticed.

"Water," Zayn whispered.

"Unfortunately," I said.

---

You'll want to know why a fox would be unhappy about water in the middle of the Destiny Desert.

The Singing Wells are older than Veimore, older than the glass city, older than most of the names on the map. Seven stone well-mouths sit in a ring among date palms, and the water in them hums. The water-finders will tell you it's the voice of the Weaver. Or the ghosts of everyone who ever drowned in them, still singing on the way down. Water-finders will tell you anything if you buy them a drink. Never buy them one *there*.

What I know is this. The water is sweet and cold, and it remembers. Drink it, and it will hand you back something you had forgotten so completely you didn't know there was a hole where it used to be.

People think that sounds like a gift.

People have never had a hole filled in with something they'd buried there on purpose. I've seen a man drink from the third well and remember, all in one swallow, what he'd done to his brother when they were boys. He walked into the dunes that night and didn't take any water with him. I've seen a woman come up from the water laughing, and keep laughing for six days, and on the seventh she stopped and never made another sound. The wells don't give you your past. They give your past *you*.

"Zayn," I said. "Before you go running in there with your tongue out—"

But he'd stopped again, and this time it wasn't the hum.

From somewhere ahead, beyond the dark shapes of the palms, came a snarl like tearing cloth. Then a clang of steel. Then a sound I had not expected to hear anywhere within three days' walk of the Hollow, a sound so out of place it made the fur on my neck rise worse than the snarl had.

Someone was laughing.

---

We came over the last low dune and looked down into the grove.

The palms stood black against the Embers. The seven well-mouths glowed faintly between them, a soft silver light rising off the water like breath on a cold morning. And in the middle of all that quiet, beautiful wrongness, a young man was fighting five Hollow Hounds by himself.

I've described the Husks to you. Let me tell you about the Hounds.

They are wolves, or the memory of wolves, made of shadow the way smoke is made of fire: solid in the middle and fraying at the edges, so you can never quite see where they end, and when they're close you feel the cold of them on your skin before you see them at all. Their eyes are embers. Not the gentle Embers in the sky. The angry red coals at the bottom of a fire that's burned too long, the kind you'd find under the ash of a house where somebody didn't get out. They make one sound only, that low, wet snarl. And when they bite you, they don't take flesh. They take whatever you were thinking about at the time. Men bitten by Hounds wake up not knowing what they were afraid of, only that they're very, very afraid.

They hunt the Light. That's what they're for.

The young man didn't seem to care.

He was tall, a few years older than Zayn, with dark hair tied back and a jaw that had been badly cut once and healed into a pale seam. He wore a plain, dusty desert robe. But he held his sword the way I've only ever seen kings' guards hold them, high and easy, the point never wandering. Or the half of the point he had left. The sword was broken. Snapped a hand's width and a little more above the guard, so that what remained was barely longer than a long knife, with a jagged end like a broken tooth.

He moved like water poured from a jug.

"No, not you," he was saying, sidestepping a lunge. "You, the ugly one. Yes. You know who you are."

He spun. The broken blade went through the nearest Hound's throat. It didn't bleed. It burst, the way a dropped sack of flour bursts, into a cloud of fine black ash that drifted down onto the sand. Inside the cloud, for half a breath, I heard something. A small surprised voice. Somebody's last thought, let go. Then nothing.

"Four," said the young man cheerfully. "Who's next? Don't all volunteer at once."

---

Zayn had dropped flat on his belly behind the crest of the dune. His hand was on his knife.

"Not our business," I said.

"He's alone."

"He seems to be enjoying it."

That was true. My tail glowed with it. But the young man was also bleeding from a long scratch on his left shoulder, and he was breathing harder than his voice let on, and one of the four remaining Hounds had begun, very quietly, to circle behind him while the others kept him busy.

"They're not here for him, you know," I said. I said it before I could stop myself. "Hounds hunt the Light. They've been smelling you since the caravan. They were waiting here at the water for you. He just got in the way."

Zayn turned and looked at me.

I wish I hadn't said it. But it was true, and my tail was glowing so bright it lit the sand between us. Everyone who stands near him gets in the way, sooner or later. I've never found a gentler way to say it.

He got up.

---

Here's the thing you need to understand about Zayn and the Light by this point in the story. He didn't trust it. Worse, he'd started to be afraid of it.

It had pulled Bram's little brother out of the grey. It had held off the beast. It had mended a boy from the caravan. And every time, the people it saved had looked at him afterwards with the same face, and that face had a word in it. And every time, it had shown him the dune, and the dune was getting closer, and now it came with the smell.

So when he ran down that slope toward the Hounds, the first thing he felt wasn't courage. It was a cold, sick little voice in his head, a voice with a shepherd's patience in it, saying: *He'll see. He'll see what you are, and then he'll look at you like they all do. And then he'll be gone too. Everyone near you goes.*

The light came, and it came grey.

It flickered out of his fists, thin and dim as dishwater, and the Hound circling behind the young man stopped. It turned its ember eyes toward Zayn. It lowered its head and grinned, the way only shadows can, a grin that went too far back along the jaw.

Fear does that. I've told you. It turns the Light into something the dark can chew.

The Hound sprang. Zayn threw himself sideways and felt its teeth snap shut beside his ear, and for a moment, just a moment, he couldn't remember where he was. The palms, the wells, the reason he'd come down the dune: gone, bitten clean out, a gap like a pulled tooth. He knelt in the sand blinking at his own knife as if someone had handed it to him. Then it came back, all of it, in a rush. Most of it. I've never been sure it was all of it.

Over the Hound's smoking back he saw the young man stagger as a second Hound caught his wounded shoulder and dragged him half off his feet.

The young man didn't cry out. He set his teeth and swung the broken sword one-handed. He kept standing up.

And Zayn thought, with no words at all, *Not him too.*

The light went gold.

---

It didn't blaze the way it had in Harrow's End. He was too worn through. But it came up out of his chest warm and steady, like the Fire climbing over a dune, and it ran down his arm and into the rusted blade of his scavenged knife until the metal shone like honey.

The Hound in front of him flinched.

Zayn lunged, and for the first time in his life his knife went *into* a creature of the Hollow instead of sliding off. The Hound burst into ash. Zayn ran through the falling cloud of it, and slammed his shoulder into the Hound that had the young man's arm, and the light went through it like lamp-oil going up.

Then there was a back against his back.

A warm, solid weight, someone else's shoulder blades pressed against his own. In fifteen years nobody had ever stood with their back to his. He felt the other boy's breathing. He felt the heat of him through two robes. The last two Hounds paced in a slow circle, snarling their tearing-cloth snarl, and the young man shifted his feet so the two of them could turn together, like the hands of a water clock.

The young man glanced back over his shoulder. He looked at Zayn's face, and Zayn's arms, and the gold pouring out of Zayn's skin and lighting up the palms, the wells and the falling ash.

"You glow," he said.

A pause, while a Hound feinted and drew back.

"That's weird."

"I know," said Zayn.

"I didn't say it was *bad*." The young man turned forward again. "Duck."

Zayn ducked. The broken sword whistled through the air where his head had been and took the leaping Hound clean across the chest. Ash rained down on both of them, warm, like the ash that falls after a pyre. The last Hound looked at the young man, then at the boy made of light, and seemed to do some quick arithmetic. It turned to run.

Zayn's light reached it first.

---

The grove went quiet. Only the wells were still humming, low and patient, the way they'd hummed for a thousand years. Waiting for someone to get thirsty.

The gold drained back into Zayn's chest and left him swaying, with new cracks running up both forearms, brighter than the old ones. And then it came: the thing that always came. It didn't wait any more for him to close his eyes. It came in daylight now. It came in the middle of sentences.

The full Ash, white and enormous.

The Crown Dune, so high its top was lost in the sky.

Heat. Not light: heat, real heat, climbing up through the soles of his feet as if the sand itself had become a cooking stone. His own arms, opening, and the skin of his palms blistering as he watched. The smell again.

And something new. A sound, somewhere below him on the dune. Someone shouting. Someone shouting a *name*, over and over, in a voice cracked down the middle like a dropped bowl, and he couldn't hear what the name was. He leaned toward it with everything in him, with his hair already burning, and he couldn't hear it.

"Hey. *Hey.*"

A hand on his shoulder. Zayn blinked, and the desert came back: palms, wells, Embers, the thin scratch of Ash in the west. He was sitting in the sand. He didn't remember sitting down. His palms were stinging. He looked at them. There was nothing there. He looked again, to make sure. The young man was crouched in front of him, his broken sword across his knees, watching him with frank, dark-eyed interest.

"You went somewhere," the young man said. "Your eyes did a thing. And you were saying *it's hot, it's hot*. It isn't. It's freezing. I've got goosebumps on my goosebumps."

Zayn looked up at the sky, at the Ash. It was thin. It was so very thin. He let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

I didn't tell him how fast an Ash fills up. It seemed unkind. I've learned that kindness and cowardice wear the same robe in the dark.

"Does it hurt?" the young man asked.

Zayn stared at him.

He nodded at the golden cracks on Zayn's arms. As if it were a perfectly normal question to ask someone who'd just turned into a lamp in front of you.

In fifteen years of being Zayn, no one had ever asked him that.

"Yes," said Zayn.

My tail glowed. The young man glanced at it, then back at Zayn, and nodded slowly.

"Thought so," he said. "Your face looked like mine does when I've been punched." He stood and held out a hand, and when Zayn took it, he pulled him to his feet in one easy motion. "Right. Excuse me a moment. I need to collect my wages."

---

He took a clay jar from a pack by the nearest palm, knelt in the sand, and began to scoop up Hound-ash with a flat wooden spoon.

Zayn watched him for a long time.

"What," he said finally, "are you doing?"

"Making a living." The young man didn't look up. "Glassblowers in the free towns pay a water token a pinch for this. Makes black glass that won't break. Some of them say if you hold a cup of it to your ear you can hear the Hound still in there, growling. I've never tried. I'm brave, not stupid." He tapped the jar. "A good Hound's worth a week of water. You just doubled my income, glowing stranger. I'd thank you, but I'm going to have to give you half, and I'm still sad about it."

"You can keep it."

"Absolutely not. I have principles." He considered. "One. It's that one."

He sealed the jar and sat back on his heels. In the silver light from the wells, I saw the ring on his right hand for the first time. Heavy and gold, too heavy and too gold for a dusty young man who scraped up monster-ash for a living. It was carved with a crest I'd seen before, on banners and on great red city gates, hung with lanterns.

I kept that to myself. I keep a lot of things to myself. You may have noticed. I've started to think of myself as a kind of well.

"Soren," said the young man, and pointed at his own chest.

He said it out loud. Right into the open night, with the wind moving in the palm fronds over his head.

Zayn flinched as if he'd dropped a lamp. He looked round, wildly, at the dark between the palms, as if something would come out of it. "You shouldn't— The wind— It *learns* them. There's a valley, it—"

"The wind and I have an understanding," said Soren. "It's never done me a single favour, and I've never asked it for one." He raised his eyebrows. "You've got one too, I assume. A name. Or do I keep calling you glowing stranger? I don't mind. It's got a ring to it."

Zayn's hand went to his chest. He looked at the wind moving in the palms for a long time. I knew what he was hearing. Red stone, and a child's voice, tasting him. *Zayn. Zayn. We'll wait.*

Then he leaned in, close to Soren's ear, and said it so quietly I only just heard it. "Zayn."

"Zayn," Soren repeated, at a perfectly normal volume. "Good. Easy to shout when you're about to be eaten." He looked down. "And who's this?"

That was me. I'd trotted down from the dune and was sitting by the nearest well, washing ash off my front paw with considerable dignity.

"Kiri," I said. "Yes, I talk. No, it isn't a trick. Yes, I've heard every joke. No, I'm not possessed. Not entirely."

Soren looked at me, then at Zayn.

"Your fox talks," he said to Zayn.

"He's not my fox," said Zayn.

"I'm not his fox," I said, at exactly the same time.

Soren took this in. Then he shrugged. "Fine," he said. "I've had a strange year."

He didn't scream. He didn't make the sign against evil, which is what a man two hundred years ago did right before he fell into his own well. He just went back to tying up his shoulder with a strip of cloth, one end held in his teeth.

I decided I liked him. I didn't say so. I have standards. Also, I've learned what happens to the people I like, and I'd rather not draw the desert's attention to him.

It was already too late for that. It usually is.

---

Zayn's knees chose that moment to remember he hadn't had water in a day and a half. He sat down rather suddenly beside the nearest well.

The water inside was black and still, and the hum was so loud this close that the stone lip shivered under his hand. He could see his own face in it, faintly silver. Then he couldn't. For a moment, just a moment, he could have sworn the face looking up at him from the water was smaller. Younger. Not his.

"Careful," said Soren. He'd stopped tying his bandage. "You know what these are?"

"The Singing Wells," said Zayn. "Kiri says they remember."

"Kiri's right." Soren crouched beside him. For the first time his voice wasn't joking. "First drink's the worst. It'll hand you something back. Something you didn't know you'd lost." He looked down into the black water. "I drank once. My first night out here. I saw my mother's face. She died when I was three. I didn't know I remembered her at all." He was quiet a moment. "I saw her dying. That's the part they don't tell you. You don't get to choose which bit you get back." Then, lighter, because he couldn't stand to leave it there: "Now I strain it through cloth and boil it, and it still hums at me a bit. But it doesn't sing."

Zayn looked at the water.

I knew what he was thinking. A boy found alone in the sand had nothing back there to remember: no face, no voice, nothing but a fox sitting a little way off, watching. Whatever the well gave him, it couldn't be worse than the emptiness he already had.

He was wrong about that. Emptiness is the kindest thing the desert ever gives anyone. It's only the full things that can break.

"Zayn," I said.

He looked at me.

I could have stopped him. One word would have done it.

I didn't say it. I've thought about why, many times, and I'll let you decide whether it was kindness.

He cupped his hands, dipped them into the Singing Well, and drank.

---

It was cold enough to ache in his teeth, and sweeter than any water he'd ever bought with a clay token. He swallowed, and the humming came inside him, filling his chest and his skull until it wasn't humming anymore.

It was singing.

And the desert went away.

He was small. Smaller than he had ever been, wrapped tight in something soft, warm all the way through for the first time in his life. It was dark, and he wasn't afraid of the dark. He couldn't see faces, only the orange flicker of a fire somewhere close. And beyond the fire, outside, very far off, a sound he didn't have a word for yet. Many voices, chanting. No words in it. Just breath, rising and falling, rising and falling, coming closer.

But he could feel a hand.

It was a child's hand. Small, a little sticky, very warm, and it had hold of his entire fist. It wasn't letting go. It squeezed, gently and then tighter, the way you'd squeeze something to make sure it was real, the way he himself pressed the feather mark on his chest when he was afraid. Someone was holding on to him. Someone small had decided he was worth holding on to, and was not going to let go, whatever came through the door.

And above him, somewhere in the warm dark, a man was singing.

His voice was low and rough and a little out of tune. It was a song about fire, and about wings, and about something that falls into the ashes and rises up out of them again. Zayn didn't know the words. They slid away like water through his fingers. But the tune went into him and stayed, the way a fish-hook stays.

The chanting outside got louder. The man's voice shook, and kept singing.

The small hand squeezed harder. And then—

Then it was gone. Not let go. *Pulled*. Torn out of his fist, the fingers dragging across his palm, and there was a scream, a child's scream, high and furious, getting farther away. And the singing stopped.

He knew that tune.

He'd heard it before. Not long ago. He'd heard it hummed, out in the dunes, behind a smooth white mask, by a man with twin grey blades who had cornered him in the dark and then, for no reason at all, let him go. He'd heard it on the ridge, as the Veilbeast came down.

---

Zayn came up out of the water gasping.

He was on his knees by the well, his face wet, and not all of it was well-water. He pressed both hands to his chest as if he could keep something in there. His right fist was clenched so hard his nails had cut half-moons in his palm. He couldn't open it. He sat there trying, and it wouldn't. As if it were still holding on to something that had been taken out of it fifteen years ago.

Soren didn't say anything.

That was the second thing he did that night that no one had ever done for Zayn. He didn't ask questions. He didn't laugh, or stare, or tell him to stop. He sat down in the sand beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the palms, and waited. After a while, without a word, he reached over and began gently prying Zayn's fingers open, one at a time, the way you'd help an old man with a stiff hand.

When the last finger came loose, Soren said, "Good one or bad one?"

Zayn wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"I don't know," he said.

My tail glowed. It was the truest thing he could have said.

Later, when Soren had built a fire between two of the wells, Zayn came and sat by me. He didn't look at me. He kept his eyes on the flames.

"Kiri," he said softly. "That song. Do you know it?"

The hum of the wells filled up the silence.

"Lots of people sing about fire," I said at last. "It's the desert. There isn't much else to sing about."

My tail glowed, because every word of that was true.

That's the trouble with the truth. You can hide a whole child in the spaces between it, and no tail in the world will ever give you away.

---

Soren shared his flatbread, hard as roof tiles, and the boiled, strained water from his tin pot. It only hummed a little. Zayn drank it with his eyes shut, braced, and nothing came.

"You can stay, if you like," Soren said, poking the fire with the end of his broken sword. He said it lightly, as though it didn't matter. It mattered a great deal. I could hear it in the spaces between the words. "Fire's big enough for two." He glanced at me. "Three."

"I eat scorpions," I said. "I won't be touching your bread."

"Then you're cheap company. Even better."

Zayn lay down on the far side of the fire with his patched cloak pulled over him. And I watched him do something I had never once seen him do in fifteen years. He fell asleep with his knife lying in the sand beside him, instead of in his hand. His right hand was curled loosely on the sand instead, palm up, fingers half closed, as if waiting for someone small to take it.

Soren sat up a while longer, turning the heavy gold ring on his finger and looking north, toward a city he couldn't see. Then he lay down too.

I stayed awake. Someone has to.

The wells hummed. Around midnight, the hum changed key, the way it does sometimes, and for an hour it sounded almost exactly like breathing. Seven sleepers, very large, under the stone. I sat with my back to the fire and watched the black water and did not let either of the boys go near it.

The Embers turned. And low over the western dunes, the thin Ash hung like a white thread, like the first stitch in a shroud that would take exactly one turn of the sky to finish sewing.

I didn't count the nights that first night. I should have. If I'd started from the beginning, I'd have known exactly how many they had, and I could have told you every one.

I started the next night instead.

I've never been able to stop. Some nights I still lie awake counting them, and every time I count, there's one less than I remember.

---

*End of Chapter Eight*
