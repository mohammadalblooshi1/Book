# PART ONE: THE NAMELESS


---

# Chapter One
## The Boy the Sand Forgot

> *When the grey comes creeping and the names are lost,*
> *A child of the feather will carry the cost.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born, as sung in the desert villages

---

He is burning.

The Ash hangs full and white above the Crown Dune, so close it looks like you could reach up and smudge it with your thumb. The highest sand in the world sits beneath it, and on that sand a boy stands with his arms open, as if he's about to catch something falling from the sky.

The fire doesn't start at his hands. It starts in his chest, right where the mark is, a small golden feather that has glowed in the dark his whole life. It cracks open like a seed. Light pours through the cracks in his skin. It runs down his arms and up his throat and out of his eyes, and the boy doesn't scream.

That's the part I always remember. He doesn't scream.

The light keeps growing until it isn't a boy anymore. It's a shape, two great wings of gold folding out across the dark, wide enough to cover the dunes from one edge of the sky to the other. Then it falls. Gold rains down over the desert, and everywhere it lands, the grey goes away.

Somewhere below, someone is shouting a name.

---

But that is the end.

Let me begin where the boy had no name.

---

You'll want to know who I am, I suppose. Everyone always does, and they never like the answer.

I am a fox. Small, sand-coloured, with ears tipped black like they've been dipped in ink. My tail glows like a coal when someone tells the truth, which makes me the worst possible company at dinner. I have one amber eye and one silver eye. We'll get to the silver one later. Don't look at it too long.

I'm older than every city in this desert. I'm older than the city that turned to glass. I have walked beside eleven children who carried the Light, and I have watched every one of them face the same dune under the same full Ash. Some of them were brave. Some of them ran. I'm still angry at the ones who ran. I'll tell you about them too, eventually, if you're good and stop wriggling.

This is the story of the twelfth.

He was the one I loved most. Don't tell the others.

---

The village was called Harrow's End, which tells you most of what you need to know about it.

It sat on the last strip of hard ground before the Destiny Desert began, forty clay houses crouched around one deep well, like old men guarding a single coin. To the north and east, the dunes rolled away gold and endless, and they moved every night. Not a little. Not the way sand shifts in wind. They *moved*, whole mountains of them, rewriting the paths across the desert while everyone slept. A trail that led to an oasis one night might lead off a cliff the next. The people of Harrow's End feared the sand the way you'd fear a large animal that hadn't decided yet whether to eat you.

They feared the boy a little more.

He was fifteen that year, as far as anyone could guess. The desert had dropped him on the edge of the village after a sandstorm, a baby wrapped in grey cloth that no weaver in Harrow's End could name. There was no mother, no father, no tracks leading in or out. Only a baby, crying in the sand.

And a fox, sitting a short way off, watching.

I'll admit nothing about that.

The village kept him the way you'd keep a stray dog that wandered in during a storm. They fed him when they remembered and kicked him when they didn't. They called him *Sandborn* when they were feeling kind and *the Nameless* when they weren't, which was most days. Nobody ever gave him a real name.

So he gave himself one. He was seven years old, sitting alone on the edge of the well, and he decided he would be called *Zayn*. He didn't know where the word came from. He just liked how it sounded, like wind over the top of a dune. He told no one. A name was the only thing in the world that belonged to him, and he had already learned that the people of Harrow's End took things.

---

On the morning this story begins, Zayn was underground.

There were ruins north of the village, half-swallowed by the dunes: the bones of some town so old that even the stones had forgotten its name. Every few weeks the sand shifted and opened a new room, a new staircase, a new collapsed hall full of rust and dust and the occasional bit of something worth trading. The village men wouldn't go near them. They said the ruins were cursed, which was convenient, because it meant the only person willing to dig through them was the boy they already thought was cursed.

Zayn was a scavenger. It was the only work anyone would give him.

He crouched now in a chamber no taller than a goat, his scavenged knife between his teeth, prying at a strip of copper that ran along the base of a wall. Light fell in thin gold blades through the cracks above. Every time he moved, sand hissed down from the ceiling like something breathing.

"You know," I said from the doorway, "most people your age are asleep at this hour. Or eating. Or doing anything that isn't crawling around inside a dead building that very much wants to fall on them."

He didn't answer. He never did when I talked. It wasn't that he couldn't hear me. Back then he simply thought I was a strange fox that hung around his tent and made noises, and that the words he heard were his loneliness playing tricks on him. I let him think it. It was easier than the alternative.

The copper came loose with a crack. Zayn grinned around the knife, the brief, crooked grin of a boy who doesn't get many reasons to smile and doesn't waste them.

Then the wall groaned.

It was a long, low, unhappy sound, the sound of a great weight changing its mind. Dust poured from the ceiling. Zayn froze. His hand went to his chest, to the spot just under his collarbone where the mark sat beneath his shirt. He always did that when he was afraid. Pressed it, the way you'd press a bruise to make sure it was still there.

The mark was a feather. That's the simplest way to describe it: a birthmark the colour of honey, shaped like a single feather, with fine lines in it like the barbs of a real one. On dark nights it glowed. Faintly, so faintly you could pretend you hadn't seen it. Zayn had spent fifteen years pretending he hadn't seen it.

The wall held. The groaning stopped.

He let out a breath, tucked the copper into his bag, and crawled out into the Fire.

---

The Fire is what we call the sun out here. The moon is the Ash, and the stars are the Embers: all the little bits of light left over after the sky burns down each night. It sounds dramatic. Desert people are dramatic. When the sky is the only thing that never moves, you get attached to it.

By the time Zayn walked back into Harrow's End, the Fire was high and white and merciless, and his bag held enough copper to buy water for three days.

He got water for one.

"Copper's cheap this month," said old Garrem at the trading post, sliding a single clay water token across the counter. Each token was worth one cup from the village well. It was the only money anyone in the desert really trusted, because you can't drink gold. "Too many scavengers."

There was exactly one scavenger in Harrow's End, and both of them knew it.

Zayn didn't argue. He looked at the token for a long moment, then picked it up and turned to go. Arguing only made it worse. He had learned that at about the same time he learned to walk.

He made it halfway across the square before someone said, "Look. The Nameless is back from his grave."

Bram.

There's one in every village. Bram was Zayn's age, broad-shouldered and quick-tongued, the son of a well-keeper, which made him almost a prince in a place where water was money. He sat on the edge of the well with three other boys around him like flies around a date, and he had been making Zayn's life miserable for as long as either of them could remember.

"Find any ghosts down there, Nameless?" Bram called. "Find your mother?"

The other boys laughed. Zayn kept walking.

"Maybe that's where they found you." Bram hopped down from the well. "In a hole. With the rest of the rubbish."

Zayn kept walking.

There was a scar on Zayn's back, a pale, puckered line just below his left shoulder blade. He'd been six. A group of children had decided, the way children sometimes do, that the strange boy with the too-light eyes was a demon. And demons, everyone knew, could be driven out with stones. One of those stones had been sharp. Bram had been five years old that day and standing in the front. Neither of them ever spoke of it. Neither of them ever forgot.

"Hey." A rock hit the sand by Zayn's foot. "I'm *talking* to you, monster."

That stopped him.

It always did. He could take *Nameless*. He could take *Sandborn*, *rubbish* and *grave-rat*, and he'd heard them all. But there was something about *monster* that went into him like a hook. Maybe because, on the dark nights, when the feather glowed under his shirt, a small and frightened part of him wondered whether it was true.

He turned around.

Bram was grinning. Waiting. Hoping.

And Zayn said, very quietly, "I'm not a monster."

He said it because it was true. That was the thing about Zayn, and it's the first clue in this story, so pay attention: **he could not lie.** He'd tried, of course. Every child tries. But whenever a lie got as far as his tongue, the feather mark on his chest would burn like a coal had been pressed against it, sharp enough to make his eyes water. So he had stopped trying. He told the truth, always, even when the truth got him hit. Mostly *because* the truth got him hit.

"Not a monster," Bram repeated, in a high, mocking voice. The boys laughed again.

Zayn turned and walked away, his hand pressed flat against his chest, and nobody followed.

From the shadow of a doorway, I watched Bram's grin fade a little once Zayn was gone. He looked down at his hands for a moment, as though he wasn't sure whose they were.

My tail flickered. People are rarely only one thing. Remember that, too.

---

Zayn's tent sat a little way outside the village, on a patch of hard ground between the last house and the first dune. It was patched in nine colours, because every piece of it had been scavenged from somewhere else. It was small, and the wind came through the seams at night. It was his.

There was a bundle waiting outside it.

It always appeared the same way, every few days, with no footprints leading to or from it, or none that the wind hadn't already brushed away. A square of faded blue cloth, knotted at the top. Inside was flatbread, still faintly warm from the baking stones. And sometimes, on the good days, a twist of waxed paper holding four sticky dates rolled in honey.

Today was a good day.

Zayn sat down in the doorway of his tent and held the dates in his palm for a long time without eating them. Dates and honey were the finest thing he knew of in the whole world. He saved them. He always saved them, for the hardest days, as if he could store sweetness the way you store water.

He didn't know who left the bundles. He had decided, when he was small, that it must be the Weaver: the creator, the one who "writes the sands", who the village elders said guided every grain of the desert to where it was meant to fall. He'd decided the Weaver had made a mistake with him, felt bad about it, and left bread to apologise.

He was wrong about that. It was Auntie Sabba.

She was the old widow who baked bread for half the village in a clay oven behind her house. She never once spoke to Zayn in public. She never once defended him, not when the children threw stones and not when the men cheated him at the trading post. She was old and alone and afraid, and in a place like Harrow's End, kindness out loud could cost you everything. So she was kind quietly, at dawn, and left before anyone could see.

I think about her more than you'd expect. The world is held together by people like Auntie Sabba: people too frightened to be brave, who are good anyway in small, secret ways. Zayn never learned her name in all his years in that village. But the sand remembers. That's what he used to tell himself on the worst nights, lying alone in his patched tent and listening to the wind.

*The sand remembers.*

He didn't fully believe it. But he said it anyway, and sometimes that's what faith is.

---

When the Fire went down and the first Embers came out, Zayn went to see Old Hesset.

Hesset's tent sat at the far edge of the village, even farther out than Zayn's, as though even the other outcasts wanted their distance from him. It was hung inside and out with dried herbs, bitterleaf and sand-mint and the little grey thistle that stops a fever, and with dozens of tiny bronze bells on threads. The bells were silent most of the time. They rang only when someone in the tent told a lie.

Hesset said they were very good for business. People who came to a healer and lied about what was wrong with them tended to get the wrong medicine.

He was the oldest man anyone in Harrow's End had ever seen. His skin was brown and creased like a dry riverbed, his beard was white as salt, and his eyes were milky and pale. He was blind, and had been for longer than anyone could remember. The village called him a madman, a hermit, a healer, sometimes a witch, depending on how sick they were. They came to him for medicine and left as fast as they could.

Only Zayn came for the stories.

"You're late," Hesset said, before Zayn had even pushed aside the tent flap. "And you smell like copper and disappointment. Garrem cheated you again."

"One token," said Zayn.

"For three days' worth of copper." Hesset clicked his tongue. "Sit. There's tea."

There was always tea. It tasted of smoke and mint, and Zayn drank it every night and hated it every night, and would have died before he admitted that. He sat cross-legged on the worn rug. The single lamp threw shadows up the walls, and the bells turned slowly on their threads.

"Which one tonight?" Hesset asked.

"The Phoenix-Born," said Zayn. He asked for that one every time.

Hesset smiled. It was a strange smile, and I've never quite been able to describe it. Fond and sad at once, the way a man smiles at an old letter.

"Again?" he said. "You know it better than I do."

"Tell it anyway."

So he did.

He told of the time before time, when the Weaver first wrote the sands, and of how a darkness came, born from fear, that made people forget who they were. He told how, whenever the darkness grew too strong, the Weaver would place a piece of its own light inside one child. That child would carry the light across the desert, through the dunes and the storms and the creatures of the grey, to the highest dune in the world. There, under the full Ash, the child would set the light free and drive the darkness away.

"The Phoenix-Born," Hesset said softly. "That's what the old songs call them. They rise like the fire-bird from the ashes. They save everyone."

Zayn's eyes were shining. They always shone at this part.

You have to understand what that story meant to a boy like him. He was nameless, thrown away and called a monster. The story said that sometimes the forgotten ones are chosen. It said that sometimes the strangest child in the village is the one the whole world is waiting for. He had never told anyone his secret dream. Not Hesset, not me, not the wind. But I knew it the way you know the shape of your own paw.

He wanted to be a hero.

Not rich. Not powerful. Not even loved, though he wanted that too, so badly he couldn't look straight at it. He wanted to *matter*. He wanted the people who threw stones to look up one day and understand that they had been wrong about him.

"What happens to them?" Zayn asked. "After? When the darkness is gone?"

He had never asked that before.

It was a small question, and Hesset went very still. On their threads, the bronze bells waited.

"They are remembered," Hesset said at last.

No bell rang. It was true, after all. Just not the whole truth.

I have walked with eleven bearers. I've learned that the whole truth and *true* are two very different animals, and the space between them is where most of the pain in the world lives. Hesset knew exactly what happened to the Phoenix-Born. He knew better than anyone alive. He chose not to say it. I've been angry at him about that for a very long time. I'm less angry now. But that's later.

"It's late," Hesset said, and the moment passed. "Go home. And Zayn—"

The boy paused at the tent flap.

"Remember the rule," said the old man. "Never say your name to the wind."

It was the oldest rule in the desert, the one every child learned before they could walk. *Never say your name to the wind.* The elders said the darkness had ears, and the wind carried everything to it eventually, and a name spoken carelessly into the open air could be found by things that collect names.

Zayn nodded. He stepped out into the night.

And because he was fifteen, and lonely, and because the only thing in the world that was truly his was a name nobody else would ever say, he walked to the top of the nearest dune, faced the open desert, and whispered it.

"Zayn."

The wind took it, gently. It carried it away north and east over the moving sand, toward the dark.

He stood there a long while afterward, his hand pressed to his chest, feeling oddly lighter, as though he'd sent a letter to someone who might one day write back.

Then he saw it.

Far out on the horizon, where the gold of the dunes met the black of the sky, there was a line of grey. It was low and flat, like mist rising from water, except there was no water in the Destiny Desert. It hadn't been there last night. It hadn't been there in fifteen years of nights. It wasn't moving fast. It didn't need to.

Above it, the Embers seemed dimmer. As if the grey was not only on the ground, but reaching up into the sky, quietly putting out the stars one by one.

The feather on Zayn's chest began, very faintly, to burn.

He did not see me sitting at the foot of the dune behind him. I was watching him the way I had watched him since the day the desert dropped him in the sand. My tail glowed steady and bright, because everything about that moment was true.

"Well," I said to no one, to the wind, to the Weaver if it cared to listen. "Here we go again."

The grey was coming.

And this time, it had heard his name.

---

*End of Chapter One*

# Chapter Two
## When the Grey Came Creeping

> *The grey does not bite. The grey forgets for you.*
> — a warning carved above the doors of old desert wells

---

Nobody in Harrow's End believed him.

That shouldn't surprise you. Nobody in Harrow's End had believed anything Zayn said in fifteen years. Never mind that he couldn't lie. They'd never bothered to notice.

He ran into the village before dawn, sand still in his hair, and banged on the door of the headman's house until a light came on. He told the headman what he had seen. A line of grey on the horizon, low and flat, swallowing the Embers.

The headman, a heavy man named Oszkar with a beard like a thornbush, listened with his eyes half closed. Then he said, "Mist."

"There's no mist in the desert," said Zayn.

"Dust, then. A storm."

"It wasn't moving like a storm. It was—" Zayn searched for the word. "Waiting."

Oszkar looked at him the way you'd look at a goat that had started reciting poetry. "Go back to your tent, Sandborn," he said, and shut the door.

I was sitting on the wall across the lane. I could have told the headman myself. I could have hopped down, trotted up to his door, and said in perfectly clear words: *Your village has about one day before the Hollow eats it.* He'd have heard me. Anyone can hear me, if I decide to speak.

But there are rules about these things. Not the Weaver's rules exactly. Mine. A fox who talks to everyone gets questions from everyone, and I have found over the centuries that most questions are a waste of breath. Besides, the bearer has to see it first. He has to be the one who knows. That's how it has always worked.

So I said nothing. I'm not proud of that either. It's becoming a theme.

---

The grey came the next day.

Not all at once. That's not how the Hollow works. It doesn't charge like an army or crash down like a wave. It *seeps*. It arrives the way cold arrives in a house at night: first in the corners, then along the floor, until one moment you realise you can't remember the last time you were warm.

It started with the colour.

Auntie Sabba noticed first, though no one asked her. She came out of her house in the morning with a basket of dough on her hip, and stopped. The blue door of the house beside hers, painted the colour of the sky by a man who had died twenty years ago, wasn't blue anymore. It wasn't any colour at all. It was the flat, dead grey of old ash.

She stood and stared at it a long time. Then she went back inside and didn't come out again until evening.

By noon, the whole eastern edge of the village had gone grey.

The red cloth hung over Garrem's trading post was grey. The green of the date palms by the well was grey. A goat wandered out of the grey part of the village and stood in the square looking confused, and its brown coat was grey, and its eyes were the white of an egg.

And it was quiet. That was the part that frightened people most, though most of them couldn't have told you why. In the grey part of the village there was no sound at all. No wind in the palms. No dogs barking. No creak of doors. Silence, like a held breath that never lets go.

People began to gather in the square. They talked in low, nervous voices. Somebody said it was a sickness. Somebody else said it was a curse. A woman said she'd heard stories, from her grandmother's grandmother, about a thing that came out of the deep desert and made people forget. Oszkar told her to stop talking nonsense, but he said it too loudly, and he kept glancing east.

Zayn stood at the very edge of the crowd, where he always stood, close enough to hear and far enough to run.

His hand was pressed flat against his chest.

The feather mark had been burning since dawn. It was a slow, low heat, like a coal banked under ash, and it got hotter whenever he looked east. He didn't tell anyone. He wouldn't have known how. *My birthmark is warm* wasn't the kind of thing that made people trust you.

"It's moving," someone said.

Everyone turned.

At the eastern end of the square, the grey had reached the first house. They watched it climb the clay wall like water soaking up through a cloth. It crept over the doorstep and through the open window. It slid across a child's wooden toy left in the dirt. The toy had been painted yellow. It wasn't anymore.

"Everyone inside," said Oszkar. "Shut your doors. Stay away from the windows."

As if a door could stop it.

---

The Fire went down red and swollen over the western dunes. Night came on. And with the night came the Husks.

You'll want me to describe them. I wish I didn't have to.

They were people. That's what you must understand first. Every one of them had been a person once, a herder or a trader or somebody's son, until the Hollow found them and emptied them out. It takes everything. Names first, and then memories, and then all the small invisible threads that tie one person to another. What's left still walks. It still has a face. But the eyes are white and blank as boiled eggs, and the skin is the colour of ash, and the face doesn't do anything. No fear. No anger. Nothing.

And they are drawn to light. The way moths are, if moths were silent and patient and never, ever stopped.

They came out of the grey just after dark. First one, walking slowly up the eastern lane. Then three. Then a dozen, and more behind them, a grey crowd moving without sound between the houses.

Zayn saw them from the top of the hill behind his tent. He'd climbed up to look, because he couldn't stand not knowing. He saw the shapes moving through the dark streets. He saw them turn, all together, toward the one house that had forgotten to put out its lamp.

He was already running before he'd decided to.

---

I followed him. Of course I followed him. I'd been following him for fifteen years.

"This is a bad idea," I said, loping alongside him down the slope. "I just want that on record. For later. When people ask."

He didn't answer. He didn't even look at me. His whole face was fixed on the village, his knife already in his hand. It was the scavenged one he'd pulled out of the ruins two summers ago, rusted along the spine, its edge sharpened on a flat stone until it could cut leather.

A knife. Against the Hollow.

I could have told him how useless that was. I could have told him a great many things. Instead I just ran faster.

---

The house with the lamp belonged to the well-keeper.

Bram's house.

Zayn came round the corner of the lane and stopped so suddenly that I nearly ran into the back of his legs.

The Husks had surrounded it. Twenty of them, perhaps more, standing in a silent half-circle around the front door, their white eyes all fixed on the yellow light leaking from the window. The door was shut. From inside, Zayn could hear someone crying. A small child, by the sound. And a man's voice, low and frightened, saying *hush, hush*.

And in front of the door, between the Husks and the house, stood Bram.

He had a shovel. Just a shovel, the heavy iron kind they used to clear sand from the well. He held it in both hands like a sword, and he was shaking so badly the blade rattled.

"Get back," he was saying. His voice cracked in half on every word. "Get *back*. I mean it. Get back."

The Husks didn't get back.

One of them stepped forward. Slow. Unhurried. It had been a woman once, a grey woman in a grey dress, with grey beads still threaded in her grey hair. It reached out a hand toward Bram's face.

Bram swung the shovel.

It connected with a dull, hollow thud, the way you might hit a sack of sand. The Husk staggered a step. Then it straightened, and reached out again, and this time its fingers brushed Bram's cheek.

Bram went rigid.

I watched it happen. I've watched it happen a thousand times, and it never gets easier. The colour drained out of the side of his face where the Husk had touched it, as if someone had wiped away paint. His eyes went wide and lost. The shovel slid out of his hands and fell into the sand.

"I—" Bram said. He looked down at his hands, confused. "I was… I was doing something. What was I…"

He had forgotten. Not everything. Not yet. A Husk's touch steals a little at a time: a memory here, a feeling there. But I knew what he'd lost, because I could see him searching for it. He had forgotten *why* he was standing there. He had forgotten there was a child crying behind the door who needed him.

The Husk reached out to touch him again.

And Zayn moved.

---

He didn't think about it. I want to make that very clear, because later, much later, people would tell stories about this moment. They'd say the boy was brave. They'd say he must have known what he was. They'd say a lot of foolish, flattering things.

He didn't know anything. He was fifteen and terrified, and the boy who'd thrown stones at him since he was five years old was about to be erased. Zayn simply could not stand there and watch.

So he ran into the middle of twenty Husks with a rusty knife.

He slashed at the grey woman's arm. The blade passed through her sleeve and hit something that wasn't quite flesh, cold and dense like packed clay. It left a thin line. No blood. She didn't even look at him.

But the others did.

All of them, at once. Twenty grey faces turned slowly from the lamp in the window toward the boy in the middle of the lane. Their white eyes found him.

They were drawn to light. I've told you that.

And somewhere under Zayn's shirt, the feather mark had started to glow.

---

He felt it before he saw it. A heat in his chest that wasn't fear, though there was plenty of fear too. It was a *burning*. It started small, right at the centre of the mark, and spread outward along his ribs and up his throat and down into his arms. It was like swallowing a coal and feeling it catch.

The Husks came toward him.

He backed up until his shoulders hit the wall of the house. He held the knife out in front of him and it was shaking, and his breath was coming fast and thin, and the burning in his chest was getting worse and worse—

And the light came.

Not gold. Not at first.

It flickered out of his skin, out of the cracks of his clenched fists, and it was *grey*. A thin, dim, sickly grey, like light seen through a dirty window. It sputtered. It wavered. The Husks didn't even slow down. If anything they came faster, the way a cat comes faster at a mouse that's started to limp.

That's what fear does to the Light. I should have warned him. I didn't know, then, how badly he was afraid. I'd forgotten what it was like to be fifteen and alone and certain that you are a mistake.

The grey woman reached for his face.

Zayn shut his eyes.

And behind the door, the child cried out. One word, high and thin and broken.

"*Bram!*"

Something in Zayn's chest *turned over*.

I don't know how else to describe it. I was watching, and I saw his eyes snap open, and I saw him look past the Husks to Bram. Bram, standing lost and grey-cheeked in the lane, staring at his own hands. A boy who had forgotten someone he loved.

Zayn knew exactly what that was like. He'd spent his whole life being the someone nobody remembered.

He stopped thinking about himself.

And the light went *gold*.

---

It came out of him like the Fire coming up over the dunes.

One moment the lane was dark and grey and full of silent shapes. The next it was blazing. Golden light poured out of Zayn's hands, out of his chest, out of the cracks between his fingers, out of his eyes. It filled the lane from wall to wall. It lit up every face, every grey robe and every white eye, and threw their shadows huge and black against the houses behind them.

The Husks *recoiled*.

They didn't scream. They can't. But they flinched back from the light the way your hand flinches from a hot stone, all of them at once, stumbling and turning and lurching away into the dark. The grey woman with the beads in her hair staggered backward. For one moment, for one heartbeat, I saw her eyes change. The white faded, and underneath was brown, ordinary brown, the eyes of a woman who had once threaded beads into her hair because she liked how they caught the light.

She looked at Zayn. She looked at him like someone waking from a long dream and not quite recognising the room.

Then the grey closed back over her, and she was gone with the others into the night.

---

The light faded.

It didn't go out all at once. It drew back into Zayn slowly, the way water drains out of sand, until it was only a faint glow at the centre of his chest, and then not even that.

He slid down the wall and sat in the sand.

His hands were shaking. There were thin golden lines on his palms, like cracks in glazed pottery, faintly glowing. They hurt. He stared at them as if they belonged to someone else.

And that was when it happened. The thing that would happen every time he used the Light, for the rest of his life. He didn't know it yet. He thought it was just a strange flash behind his eyes, the kind you get when you stand up too fast.

He saw fire.

A full white Ash, enormous, filling half the sky. A dune higher than any dune he had ever seen. Gold, rising. And a terrible, beautiful heat, all around him and inside him, as if he were standing at the heart of the Fire itself.

It lasted less than a breath. Then it was gone, and he was sitting in the sand of Harrow's End again, gasping.

He didn't understand what he'd seen. He would. I wish I could tell you otherwise.

---

The door of the well-keeper's house opened.

Bram's father stood there, a lamp in one hand and a carving knife in the other, his face white. Behind him, peeking around his legs, was Bram's little brother, a boy of six or seven, his face streaked with tears.

And around the lane, slowly, other doors were opening. Faces peered out. The neighbours had heard. Not much. A few cries, a thud. Then silence, and then a light so bright it had come through their shutters and lit their rooms like midday.

They looked at the lane. At the empty sand where twenty Husks had stood. At Bram, still standing there, blinking slowly, one side of his face grey as ash.

And at Zayn, sitting against the wall with golden cracks glowing in his palms.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Bram turned his head. He looked down at Zayn. His eyes were clearing. Whatever the Husk had taken from him was coming back, little by little. I could see him remembering the shovel, the door, his brother crying. And I could see him remembering the light.

He opened his mouth.

I don't know what he would have said. Maybe *thank you*. Maybe something else. People are rarely only one thing, as I told you before. I like to think, even now, that it might have been *thank you*.

But he never got the chance, because that was when I decided to open *my* mouth.

---

"Well," I said loudly, from the top of the well-keeper's garden wall. "*That* was overdue."

Every head in the lane turned toward me.

Including Zayn's.

He stared up at me. His mouth fell open. For fifteen years he'd been hearing a voice that came from a small sand-coloured fox, and for fifteen years he'd told himself it was loneliness, or the wind, or the heat. And now forty people were staring at the same fox, their faces as white as the Ash.

They had heard it too.

"Oh, don't look at me like that," I said to the lane in general. "You've all seen stranger things tonight. Twenty Husks, a boy who glows, half your village turned the colour of old porridge. A talking fox is honestly the *least* of your problems."

My tail was glowing bright as a lamp. Everything I'd said was perfectly true.

Somebody screamed.

I think it was Garrem. It was a very high scream for such a large man.

And then everyone was shouting at once. Doors slammed, children wailed, and Oszkar came barrelling down the lane in his nightshirt bellowing for order. Through all of it, Zayn sat in the sand against the wall and stared up at me with his glowing hands in his lap.

"You," he said. His voice was barely a whisper. "You can… you *talk*."

"I've always talked," I said. "You just never listened properly. Most people don't. It's one of the great tragedies of the world."

"*Why didn't you tell me?*"

I hopped down from the wall and sat beside him in the sand. For a moment I didn't say anything. I looked at the golden cracks in his palms, and at the mark on his chest, still faintly warm under his shirt. I looked at his face. It was very young, and very tired, and very, very frightened.

I have sat beside eleven children like this. On eleven different nights, in eleven different places, I have watched the moment when they first understood that their life was not going to be the life they'd planned.

It doesn't get easier. You'd think it would.

"Because," I said finally, quietly, so only he could hear, "once I told you, it would all start. And I wanted you to have a little longer before it did."

He looked at me.

My tail glowed steady and gold in the dark.

---

Somewhere at the edge of the village, out where the grey was thickest, I heard a sound that made the fur stand up along my spine.

It was a child's voice. Small and confused, and calling out into the empty night.

"Mama?" it said. "Bram? Where… where is everyone? Who…"

A pause.

"Who am I?"

Beside me, Bram had gone completely still.

Because his little brother, the one who had been crying behind the door, wasn't behind the door anymore. In all the noise and the shouting and the slamming of doors, he had slipped out into the lane to find his big brother.

And he had walked straight into the grey.

---

*End of Chapter Two*

# Chapter Three
## Monster

> *Beware the child who glows; the desert has plans for him.*
> — a saying of the desert grandmothers, told to children who stay out after dark

---

Bram ran first.

I'll give him that. Whatever else he did, before and after, Bram was the first one into the grey that night. He heard his brother's voice asking the dark who he was, and he ran toward it.

"*Tobin!*"

The grey swallowed the name. That's the worst thing about it, the part nobody believes until they've stood inside it. Sound doesn't carry in the Hollow. You shout, and the shout goes out of your mouth and simply stops, like a stone dropped into thick mud. Bram shouted his brother's name three times, and each time it died a hand's width from his lips.

Zayn was on his feet before he knew it. His legs were shaking. His palms still burned where the golden cracks ran across them. He looked at the edge of the grey, at the flat, dead place where the lane stopped being a lane and became nothing at all, and he took one step toward it.

"No," I said.

He took another.

"I said *no*. You're half dead on your feet, and that is the Hollow. The thing itself. You walk in there tonight and you might not walk out."

He looked down at me. "He's seven," he said.

And then he went in after Bram, and I went in after him, because apparently that's who I am.

---

The grey feels like nothing.

It's the feeling of a room after everyone you love has left it, spread over the whole world. The colour goes first. Then the sound. Then, if you stay long enough, the reasons you came.

Zayn walked with his hand flat against his chest, and the feather mark under his palm was the only warm thing for a hundred paces in any direction. Every few steps he whispered something to himself, too low for the grey to steal. I didn't need to hear it. I knew what he was saying.

*The sand remembers. The sand remembers.*

We found Bram on his knees at the end of the lane.

Tobin was sitting in front of him in the sand, cross-legged, the way small children sit when they've been told to wait somewhere and haven't been told for how long. He wasn't crying anymore. That was what frightened me most. His face had gone the colour of old ash, and his eyes were open and calm and terribly, terribly white.

Bram had him by the shoulders. He was shaking him. Not hard. Gently, desperately, the way you'd try to wake someone who's slept too long.

"Tobin. Tobin, it's me. Look at me. *Look* at me."

Tobin looked at him. He looked politely, the way you'd look at a stranger who had stopped you in a market to ask for directions.

"Who are you?" he said.

Bram made a sound I've heard many times in many places, and I still don't have a word for it. It's the sound of something tearing that isn't cloth.

---

They got him out of the grey between them. Bram carried him and Zayn walked behind, his glowing palms held up like lanterns, and the grey drew back from them a little as they went. Not much. Just enough.

Hadric, the well-keeper, was waiting at the edge with a lamp. He took one look at his younger son's face and sat down in the sand as if his legs had been cut from under him. Behind him people were gathering: Oszkar in his nightshirt, Garrem with a cudgel, neighbours clutching shawls.

Bram laid his brother down on the hard ground outside the house. He knelt there with his hands hovering over the small grey face, not knowing what to do with them.

Then he turned around and looked at Zayn.

It wasn't hatred. It was the look of someone at the bottom of a deep pit, seeing a rope for the first time and hating that it was this particular rope.

"You did something," Bram said. His voice was hoarse. "Before. The Husks. You did something with the light and that woman's eyes went *brown*. I saw it."

Zayn said nothing.

"Do it again."

"I don't know how."

It was true. My tail glowed with it, a soft gold in the dark. Bram didn't know what that meant yet, but he saw it, and something in his face crumpled.

"Please," said Bram.

I don't think he'd ever said that word to Zayn before. I don't think he'd said it to anyone. It came out of him rusty and broken, like a gate that hadn't been opened in years.

"Please. He's my brother. *Please.*"

Zayn knelt.

---

I want to tell you he laid his hands on that child like the heroes in Hesset's stories, calm and certain and shining.

He didn't. His hands were shaking so badly that when he set them on either side of Tobin's face, the boy's head rocked a little. His breath came short. He was thinking of the Husks, and the grey, and forty people watching him from behind their lamps. He was thinking of what they'd say about him. He was thinking about himself.

So the light came grey.

It leaked out of his palms thin and sickly, the colour of the thing he was trying to fight. It lay over Tobin's face like dirty water and did nothing at all.

"I can't," Zayn whispered. "I can't, I can't, it won't—"

"Stop looking at them," I said. I was sitting right by his knee. I kept my voice low. "Stop thinking about what they see. They're not the ones who need you."

"I don't know how to—"

"Look at *him*."

He looked.

And I watched it happen for the second time that night. I watched him stop being a frightened boy surrounded by people who hated him, and become a boy looking at a seven-year-old child who had forgotten his own name. A child who was, in that moment, exactly as alone as Zayn had been every day of his life.

The light went gold.

---

It came up out of him gently this time, softer and warmer than the blaze in the lane. It flowed from his chest down his arms and out through his fingertips and into Tobin's face, like honey poured into water, and the grey began to lift.

And Zayn saw.

He saw a baby born in the hot season, red-faced and furious, while a boy of eight pressed his nose to the doorframe to see. He saw that same boy, a little older, carrying the baby on his shoulders to the top of the dune behind the well so he could point at the Embers and make up names for them. *That one's the Goat. That one's the Fat Man. That one's you, see, the little one, right next to me.* He saw Bram teaching Tobin to lower a bucket into the well without spilling, and Bram sneaking him extra bread, and Bram sitting alone behind the house one night after their father had shouted, crying silently into his knees where nobody could see.

Nobody but the small boy watching from the window.

Then the pictures turned and ran forward, and Zayn saw things that hadn't happened yet. Tobin taller, with his brother's broad shoulders, laughing at something across a fire. Tobin's hands, older, holding a lantern. The pictures were blurred, like a painting left out in the rain. The future always is. But they were warm. They were full of colour.

And then, behind all of it, the other vision.

It was clearer this time. The first time, in the lane, it had been a flash: fire, a dune, a full white Ash. Now it held. He saw the Ash enormous above him, so close it filled half the sky. He saw sand beneath his own bare feet, sand higher than any dune he'd ever climbed, falling away on every side into the dark. He saw his own arms, opening. He saw light running out of the feather on his chest, cracking him open from the inside, and he felt the heat of it. A terrible, beautiful heat. It didn't hurt, not exactly. It was like standing at the centre of the Fire itself.

Somewhere below, someone was shouting.

He couldn't hear what.

---

Tobin gasped.

It was a huge, ragged breath, the breath of someone who's been held underwater and has just broken the surface. The colour flooded back into his face, brown and pink and alive. His eyes cleared. White to grey to the dark, ordinary brown of a well-keeper's son.

He blinked up at the sky.

Then he saw Zayn.

I understand why he screamed. Imagine you're seven years old and you've just come back from the place where there is nothing, not even yourself. The first thing you see is a face bent over yours, a face that's burning. Golden cracks running up its neck and across its cheeks like a pot about to shatter. Eyes full of light, light pouring out of them like tears. And a fox beside it, staring at you with one amber eye and one silver.

Tobin screamed. He screamed and twisted out from under Zayn's hands and scrambled backward across the sand until he hit his brother's legs, and he clung to them and kept screaming.

Zayn's light snapped out like a blown lamp.

He knelt there with his hands still raised. The golden cracks were glowing on his arms, his neck, his jaw. Tears were running down his face. Real ones. He hadn't noticed them start. Some of them were his and some of them belonged to the vision, and he couldn't have told you which were which.

And across Tobin's cheeks, where Zayn's fingers had been, lay two thin golden lines, faintly shining.

Bram saw them.

He looked down at his brother, clinging and sobbing, with gold scored across his face like a burn. He looked at Zayn, cracked and glowing and crying light. He looked at the talking fox.

He was fifteen. He'd been terrified for hours. His brother had forgotten him, and been given back, and the price of it was written in gold across that small face, and Bram didn't understand any of it. He was a boy who had been taught his whole life to fear the strange thing at the edge of the village. In the end, that's what fear does. It reaches for the oldest word it knows.

"*Monster!*"

It cracked out of him like a whip.

"Get away from him! You *monster*, get away from my brother, get *away*—"

---

It went into Zayn the way it always did. Like a hook.

He flinched. That was all. Just a flinch, as if he'd been slapped. Then he said, quietly, the way he'd said it at the well, the way he'd said it his whole life:

"I'm not a monster."

My tail burned bright. Nobody was looking at it.

They were looking at the light still flickering in the cracks of his skin. And somewhere out in the grey, at the edge of hearing, something howled. A long, thin sound that rose and broke and was answered by another.

"He's calling them," said Garrem. His cudgel was trembling in his fist. "You hear that? He's *calling* them."

"The grey came when he did," said a woman behind him. "Two nights ago. I saw him. Up on the dune. He was saying something to the wind."

A murmur ran through the crowd: the ugly sound of frightened people discovering they all agreed on something.

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

Because she was right. He had whispered his name to the wind, and the grey had come. He couldn't say she was wrong. Every denial that came to his tongue, the feather burned before it reached his lips.

So he said nothing. And to the people of Harrow's End, his silence sounded exactly like guilt.

"Oszkar," said Garrem.

The headman looked at Zayn for a long moment. I'll say this for Oszkar: he didn't enjoy it. He had the hopeless look of a man who has decided to sacrifice one thing to save the rest.

"Get out, Sandborn," he said. "Get out of Harrow's End, and don't come back."

The first stone came from somewhere at the back of the crowd.

---

It hit Zayn's shoulder. Not hard. A test.

He didn't move. He was still on his knees in the sand, looking at Tobin, who had stopped screaming and was staring at him over his brother's leg with huge wet eyes.

The second stone hit his ribs, and the third his cheek, and after that I lost count.

"*Run*," I snapped. "Up, now, you stubborn sack of bones, *run*!"

He got up. He didn't run. He walked, backward at first, then turned and walked away through the crowd with stones coming at him from every side. The crowd parted for him, then closed behind him, the way a fist closes. A rock struck his back just below the left shoulder blade. I heard the sound it made. It hit within a finger's width of the old scar, the one he'd carried since he was six years old.

He staggered. He kept walking.

He never raised his hands. Not once. I want you to know that.

I bit a man's ankle. I'm not ashamed of that.

---

At the edge of the village, where the last houses gave way to hard ground and the dunes began, there was a small clay house with an oven behind it.

The door opened as Zayn passed.

Auntie Sabba came out. She was very small, with a face folded up like a dried fig and a shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders. The crowd was behind us, still shouting, but the stones were falling short now. She didn't look at them.

She walked straight up to Zayn and pressed something into his hands.

It was bread. Flatbread, still warm from the stones, wrapped in a square of faded blue cloth knotted at the top.

He knew that cloth. He'd seen it outside his tent every few days for as long as he could remember. He stared down at it, and then up at her, and I watched him understand.

"Eat, child," said Auntie Sabba. Her voice was dry and thin and fierce. "The sand remembers kindness too."

Then she went back inside and shut the door. I imagine she sat down at her table and shook for a long time.

Zayn stood there holding the bread.

It was the first time in his life anyone in Harrow's End had touched him gently. He stood at the edge of the desert, bleeding from a dozen places, holding that bread against his chest as if it were made of gold.

"Come on," I said, as softly as I've ever said anything. "Come on. We can't stay."

He walked out into the dunes. The Embers were very bright above him. Behind us the lamps of Harrow's End went out one by one, as if the village had closed its eyes.

---

The Hounds found us before midnight.

I heard them first. Foxes always do. A low sound under the wind, like breath through teeth. Then a howl, close this time, from somewhere behind the dune to our left. Then another, ahead.

"Fox," Zayn said.

"Yes," I said. "I know. Keep walking. Don't run. Running makes them happy."

They came over the dune like smoke poured from a jug.

Hollow Hounds. Six of them, shaped like wolves, if a wolf were made of the darkness between the Embers. Long and low, their bodies fraying into black mist at the legs and tail. Their eyes were coals, ember-bright, the only colour in them. They didn't pant or snarl. They spread out in a slow circle around us, and the sand didn't move under their feet.

They track the Light. Every time a bearer heals, it rings out across the desert like a bell, and the Hounds lift their heads and follow. Zayn had healed a child three hours ago. He might as well have lit a bonfire on a hill.

"What do they want?" he whispered.

"You," I said. My tail glowed. I wished it hadn't.

He pulled his knife. The rusty scavenged one, with its edge sharpened on a flat stone. He held it out in front of him, and his other hand went to his chest, and he tried, I watched him try, to call the light.

It came grey. A sputter, a sickly flicker in the cracks of his palms, and then nothing. He was too frightened. Too tired. Too full of stones and screaming and the word *monster*. The Hounds saw it falter, and they came in closer, their coal-eyes brightening.

The first one sprang.

Zayn slashed at it. The knife passed through its shoulder like a hand through smoke, and the Hound hit him full in the chest and knocked him flat on his back in the sand. Its jaws opened above his face. There was no mouth inside, only more darkness, and a cold that came out of it like the breath of a deep well.

I went for its throat. I'm small. I'm not stupid. My teeth closed on something cold and slippery, and the Hound shook me off with a twist of its head and sent me tumbling down the slope.

Rolling through the sand, I felt something stir in the silver half of me. Something huge and hungry, uncoiling. I didn't let it out. Not that night. That's a story for later, and you won't like it.

I didn't need to, as it turned out.

Because that was when the spear came out of the dark.

---

It came from nowhere. One moment the Hound was crouched over Zayn's chest with its cold jaws spread. The next, there was a long shaft of pale wood standing straight up out of its back, and the Hound was coming apart.

It didn't bleed. Shadow things don't. It simply crumbled into a drift of black ash that fell over Zayn's face and chest like soot from a chimney.

Then the hoofbeats.

They came from the north, out of the dark between the dunes, a heavy, rolling drumming that I felt through my paws before I heard it. A camel came over the crest of the dune at a full run. It was pale, almost white in the Ember-light, like something carved out of bone. On its back rode a figure wrapped from head to foot in grey cloth. There was no face to see. Only wrappings, and a veil, and a dark slit where the eyes should be.

The rider leaned from the saddle, pulled the spear from the sand in one smooth movement, and drove it through the second Hound without slowing. The third leapt at the camel's flank. The rider took it out of the air.

The last three ran, their coal-eyes winking out one by one in the dark.

---

The pale camel slowed. It turned in a wide circle and came to a halt at the top of the dune, perhaps twenty paces away.

The rider looked down at us.

Zayn was sitting up in the sand, black ash all over his chest, his knife still clutched in his hand. He was staring up at the figure on the camel with his mouth slightly open. In the dark, the golden cracks along his jaw were faintly glowing.

The rider didn't move or speak. But I could feel the weight of that look from where I lay, and it wasn't a hunter's look. It went on a long time. Longer than it should have.

"Who are you?" Zayn called.

The wind answered him. Nothing else did.

Then the rider turned the camel and rode away north into the dark, the long spear held upright like a banner. Soon there was nothing left but three drifts of black ash and a set of tracks the dunes were already filling.

"Who was that?" said Zayn.

I licked the sand off my paw and said nothing.

Who the rider was is a story for its proper time. If I tell it now you'll skip ahead, and then where will we be? Be patient. He was.

---

Zayn sat in the sand for a long time after that, holding Auntie Sabba's bread in his lap.

The golden cracks on his face and arms were fading slowly, the way the colour goes out of a coal. His back was bleeding where the stone had hit.

He looked back toward Harrow's End. At its eastern edge the grey lay over the houses like a sheet over a dead man. Only one light still burned in that whole black huddle of clay: a single, stubborn lamp at the far edge of the village, glowing through the walls of a tent hung with herbs and bells.

Hesset was awake.

Zayn looked at that lamp for a long time.

"He knew," he said at last. He wasn't asking me. He was saying it to the dark. "All those stories. The Phoenix-Born. He *knew*."

I didn't answer that either. But my tail, lying in the sand beside him, glowed a steady, quiet gold.

Zayn stood up. He tucked the bread inside his shirt, against the feather mark, where it was warm. He picked up his knife.

Then he started walking back toward the light.

---

*End of Chapter Three*

# Chapter Four
## The Bell and the Feather

> *The bell rings for the liar, and the liar is often you.*
> — words stamped around the rim of an old healer's bell

---

His tent was gone.

Not burned. I'd half expected burning, and so had he, I think. But the people of Harrow's End were too frightened that night even for fire. Fire makes light, and light, they had just learned, brought things out of the dark.

So they had simply torn it down. Somebody had pulled up the stakes and slashed the patched walls in nine colours, and then trampled the whole thing flat into the sand, the way you'd stamp on a scorpion you weren't brave enough to pick up. His blanket was gone. His water skin was gone. The little clay jar where he kept his water tokens had been smashed, and the tokens were gone too, of course. Fear doesn't stop people from being practical.

Zayn stood looking at it for a while in the dark.

"Well," I said. "At least you won't have to pack."

He didn't laugh. I hadn't really expected him to. He knelt in the wreckage and began to dig through it with his hands, quietly, methodically, the way he dug through the ruins. A scavenger to the last.

He found the twist of waxed paper first. Four dates rolled in honey, the ones he'd been saving since the day before all this began. Somebody had stepped on them. They were squashed flat and full of sand. He brushed them off carefully and put them inside his shirt with Auntie Sabba's bread.

Then he found the cloth.

It had been buried under the collapsed tent pole, half sunk in the sand. It was a square of grey fabric, softer than anything else he owned, woven with a pattern of small strange marks that no weaver in Harrow's End had ever been able to name. The desert had wrapped him in it fifteen years ago, when it left him on the edge of the village. He'd slept with it under his head every night of his life.

He held it against his face for a moment. Then he folded it small and tucked it away with the rest.

"That's everything," he said.

It wasn't much. A knife, a cloth, some bread, four ruined dates. Fifteen years in Harrow's End, and that was everything.

---

Hesset was waiting for us.

Of course he was. The lamp was still burning when we reached his tent, the bells turning slowly on their threads in the warm air that spilled out through the flap. There was tea already poured. Two cups. Hesset sat cross-legged on his worn rug, his milky eyes fixed on nothing, his white beard falling over his folded hands.

"Sit," he said, before Zayn had even ducked through the doorway. "You're bleeding on my herbs."

Zayn didn't sit.

He stood in the doorway with the lamplight on his face, and the golden cracks along his jaw were faded now, almost gone, just a faint shimmer like the trail a snail leaves on stone. His shirt was torn at the back and stiff with blood. He was shaking. Not from cold.

"You knew," he said.

Hesset turned his face toward the sound. For a long moment he said nothing.

Then he said, "Sit down, child. Let me see to your back. And then you can shout at me, and I promise to listen to every word."

The bells were silent. Every word of it was true.

---

He sat, in the end. I think he was too tired not to.

Hesset's hands were old and dry and very gentle. He peeled the torn shirt away from Zayn's back and washed the wound with water from a clay bowl, and then he packed it with a paste that smelled of bitterleaf and smoke. Zayn hissed through his teeth. He didn't cry out.

"Close to the old one," Hesset murmured. His fingers moved lightly over the pale, puckered scar under Zayn's left shoulder blade, the one from when he was six. "The sand does like to write in the same place twice."

He turned his blind face toward the corner of the tent, where I was sitting with my tail curled over my paws.

"And you," he said. "You've finally decided to talk, I hear."

"News travels fast," I said.

"Garrem screams very loudly." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's been a long time, fox."

"Not long enough."

Zayn twisted round to stare at us both. "You *know* each other?"

Hesset and I said nothing at the same moment, which I suppose was its own kind of answer.

I'll tell you this much, since you're listening and he wasn't: yes. We knew each other. We had known each other for a very long time, longer than the boy could have guessed, and the last time we'd been in the same place it had ended badly. That's Hesset's story, and he never told it to the boy. So I won't tell it to you. Not yet.

"Drink your tea," said Hesset, and pressed the cup into Zayn's hands.

Zayn drank it. Smoke and mint, bitter as always. He hated it as always. I think it was the first thing that whole night that felt like home.

---

"What am I?" he said at last.

The tea was finished. The lamp had burned down a little. Outside, the wind had come up and was whispering along the walls of the tent, and somewhere far off a dune was groaning as it moved in the dark.

"You know the story," said Hesset.

"The Phoenix-Born." Zayn's voice was flat. "The Weaver puts a piece of its light inside a child. The child carries it across the desert to the highest dune in the world and sets it free and drives the darkness away."

"Yes."

"That's a story. It's just a story you tell."

Hesset said nothing.

"I'm not one of them," said Zayn. "I'm not—"

And he stopped. His face twisted. His hand went to his chest, to the feather mark, and pressed hard, and I saw him bite down on the inside of his cheek.

It was burning.

He'd been about to say *I'm not one of the Phoenix-Born.* He'd wanted it to be true so badly he'd nearly said it. And the mark on his chest had caught the words before they reached his lips and set them on fire.

He sat there with his hand pressed to his chest and his eyes squeezed shut, breathing through his nose, waiting for the pain to fade.

"Ah," said Hesset softly. "Yes. That's the trouble with you, isn't it? You can't even lie to yourself."

---

Hesset rose. It took him a while. His knees cracked like dry sticks. He felt his way along the wall of the tent, past the hanging bitterleaf and the bunches of sand-mint, to an old wooden chest in the far corner. Zayn had seen that chest a thousand times. He'd never once seen it open.

Hesset opened it now. He reached inside without hesitating and brought out a small bundle wrapped in oiled leather.

"When the desert left you on the edge of this village," he said, "you were wrapped in that grey cloth you carry. And tucked inside the cloth, against your chest, was this."

He put the bundle in Zayn's hands.

"Oszkar's father would have sold it. He'd have got a hundred water tokens for it, perhaps more. So I took it before anyone else saw, and I kept it. I've been keeping it for you for fifteen years. Open it."

Zayn opened it.

It was a feather.

It was made of gold, or something like gold. It was as long as his hand and so fine that you could see the lamplight through the edges of it. Every barb was perfect, every tiny line, exactly like the feather of a real bird. But it was broken. It had been snapped clean across the middle, the two halves still clinging together by a few bent barbs, and many of the barbs along one side were twisted and crushed, as if something had tried to tear it apart.

And it was warm.

Zayn held it in both palms and felt the warmth soak into his skin. It was the same warmth as the mark on his chest. The same shape. The same fine lines. He looked from the feather to the mark and back again, and he didn't say anything for a long time.

"It's broken," he said finally.

"Yes," said Hesset.

"Can it be fixed?"

The old man was quiet a moment. "Some things mend themselves," he said, "when they're ready."

That was true too. The bells didn't stir. My tail stayed bright.

I've thought about that feather a great deal since. I watched it, all through the journey that followed. I'll tell you now, because it doesn't spoil anything: it did mend. Slowly. One barb at a time. Every time the boy learned something true about himself, something hard and painful, the feather grew a little more whole.

Every time, I hated it a little more. You'll understand why, eventually.

---

There was a second gift.

Hesset reached up without looking and unhooked one of the bells from its thread, the smallest one, no bigger than the tip of Zayn's thumb. It was bronze, worn smooth with age, and strung on a cord of braided leather.

"This one," Hesset said, "is for you."

He put it around Zayn's neck himself, his old fingers fumbling at the knot.

"You cannot lie. You know that by now. The mark won't let you. But the rest of the world can, and the rest of the world will. This bell will ring when someone lies to you. Not loudly. Not always in time. But it will ring."

Zayn touched it. It lay against his chest just above the feather mark, cool against his skin.

"How do I know it works?"

Hesset lifted his cup and smiled. "My tea," he said, "is the finest in the whole desert."

The bell rang.

It was a small, clear, sweet sound, like a single drop of water falling into a silver cup. And at exactly the same moment, my tail dimmed, the glow going out of it like a breath blown across a lamp, and then coming slowly back.

Zayn looked at the bell. Then he looked at my tail. Then he looked at me.

"Your tail," he said slowly. "When you lie, it…"

"Yes, yes. Glows for the truth, goes dark for a lie. Anybody's lie, not just mine. Very embarrassing. Imagine trying to play cards." I curled the offending thing tighter around my paws. "Now you've got two ways of knowing when somebody's lying to you. Most people don't get one. Try not to waste them."

"And when *you* lie?"

"I don't," I said.

My tail glowed.

He was still staring at it. I could see him thinking back over everything I'd said to him that night, every word, trying to remember whether it had glowed.

It had. All of it.

Later, much later, that would matter more than either of us knew.

---

"Would you die for them?" Hesset asked.

He said it quietly. He'd sat back down on his rug, and he was turning his empty teacup slowly between his palms, and he didn't raise his voice at all. He might have been asking whether Zayn wanted more tea.

Zayn looked up. "What?"

"The people of this village. Oszkar, who called you Sandborn. Garrem, who cheated you every week of your life. The children who threw stones when you were six, and the men who threw them tonight. Bram." He paused. "Would you die for them?"

The tent was very quiet.

Zayn opened his mouth.

I watched him. I think I'll always see it. He opened his mouth to say *yes*, because it was what a hero would say, because it was what the Phoenix-Born said in every story Hesset had ever told him. The feather burned before the word was out. He flinched.

He closed his mouth, and opened it again, to say *no*. Because they had stoned him. Because they had called him monster and torn down his tent and stolen his tokens. Because he didn't owe them anything at all.

That burned too.

He sat there with his hand pressed hard against his chest, his eyes wet, his mouth half open. Neither word would come. Both of them were lies, or both of them were too true to say. I don't know which. I'm not sure he did either.

"I don't know," he whispered finally.

My tail glowed. The bell was silent.

Hesset nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd expected.

"Good," he said. "That's honest. Hold on to it. It won't be your answer forever."

"Why are you asking me that?"

Hesset didn't answer. He turned the empty cup in his hands, round and round.

---

It was almost dawn when Zayn asked the question that mattered.

"Where do I go?"

"The City of Glass," said Hesset.

Zayn blinked. "That's a story too."

"Most things are, until you walk into them. It's real. It lies deep in the desert, farther than anyone from Harrow's End has ever gone. It's the place where the first bearer said no to the Light, and the sand around him melted into glass." His blind eyes moved, as though he were looking at something a very long way off. "The walls there remember. Everything you need to know about what you are is written on them. Older than me. Older than the fox, almost."

"Steady on," I said.

"How do I find it?"

"You don't," said Hesset. "You walk, and the dunes find it for you. They'll open a way if you're meant to reach it. You've lived beside the desert all your life. You know how it moves."

Zayn turned the broken feather over in his hands. "Will you come with me?"

Something passed across the old man's face then. It was quick, and it was gone almost before I saw it, but I've known Hesset a long time, and I knew what it was. It was shame.

"I don't leave the edge of the desert," he said. "I can't. The desert and I have an agreement. It's an old one, and I made it a long time ago, and I'm still paying for it."

True. The bell didn't stir.

Zayn was quiet. Then he asked it. The same question he'd asked two nights ago, when everything had still been ordinary and his tent had still been standing.

"What happens to them? The Phoenix-Born. When they get to the dune, and they set the light free, and the darkness goes away. What happens to them, after?"

I held my breath.

And Hesset, who had been the oldest and wisest man in Harrow's End for longer than anyone could remember, who had sat among his truth bells for more years than the boy could have guessed and never told a lie that mattered, looked at the boy with his blind eyes and said:

"Go to the City of Glass. The answers there are older than mine, and truer. You'll understand it better when you see it written."

The bell didn't ring.

Of course it didn't. Every word was true. My tail stayed gold. There was nothing in what he'd said for either of us to catch.

But I looked at Hesset across that tent, and Hesset, who could not see me, turned his face toward me anyway. And we both knew.

He had not lied to the boy. He had done something worse. He'd stood beside the truth with a cloth over it and let the boy walk past.

I've told you there's a gap between *the whole truth* and *true*. That night, in that tent, an old man I had once loved like a son pushed a fifteen-year-old boy into that gap, and smiled at him, and wished him well.

I didn't say anything either. Remember that, when you're judging him. I was sitting right there, and I could have said it, and I didn't.

---

"May the sand remember you," said Hesset at the tent flap.

The Embers were fading. The sky in the east was turning from black to the deep, bruised blue that comes just before the Fire. Somewhere behind us, beyond the clay houses of Harrow's End, the grey lay low and flat and patient along the edge of the world. It hadn't moved in the night. It hadn't needed to.

"Will it follow me?" Zayn asked. He was looking toward it. "If I go. Will it leave them alone?"

"I don't know," said Hesset.

The bell was silent. He truly didn't.

Zayn nodded. Then he did something I'd never seen him do in fifteen years. He stepped forward and put his arms around the old man, quickly and clumsily, like someone who's never been taught how. Hesset went very still. Then his dry old hands came up and rested on the boy's back, just for a moment, either side of the new wound.

"Go," Hesset said roughly. "Before I make you drink more tea."

We went.

---

We walked north, into the deep desert, as the dunes were finishing their night's work.

If you have never seen the Destiny Desert move, I'm sorry for you. It's the most beautiful thing in the world, and one of the most frightening. All night the dunes had been shifting, and now, in that last grey hour before dawn, they were settling into their new places. Whole hills of sand slid slowly sideways with a sound like a vast animal breathing. Ridges folded. Hollows opened. Paths that had been there yesterday were gone, swallowed, and new ones lay smooth and fresh between the dunes, untouched by any foot.

One of those new paths opened right in front of us.

It ran straight out from the edge of Harrow's End, between two great golden slopes, as smooth as a swept floor. It hadn't been there an hour ago. Zayn knew every inch of the ground around the village, and he stopped at the start of it and stared.

"It's for me," he said.

"Apparently," I said.

He looked back once.

Harrow's End sat behind us in the blue light, small and dark and huddled around its well. He could just make out the shape of Auntie Sabba's oven, and the flattened patch of sand where his tent had been. At the far edge, a thin thread of smoke rose from Hesset's tent and went straight up into the still air.

And along the eastern side of the village, grey.

He touched the feather mark. He touched the bell. He touched the folded grey cloth inside his shirt, where the broken feather now lay wrapped against Auntie Sabba's bread.

"The sand remembers," he whispered.

Then he turned his back on the only home he'd ever had and walked out into the desert, and the dunes closed softly behind us like a door.

---

About an hour later, as the Fire came up red over the eastern dunes, I found a scorpion.

It was a fat one, the colour of old copper, scuttling out from under a stone to warm itself in the first light. I caught it neatly behind the stinger and sat down in the middle of the path to eat it.

I took my time about it. I chewed with my mouth open. The shell made a wonderful noise, a wet, splintering crunch that echoed off the dunes on either side, and I made sure to crunch every single leg individually.

Zayn stopped walking and turned around.

"Do you have to?" he said.

"I do, actually," I said, with my mouth full. "It's very nutritious. You should try one."

"That's disgusting."

"*You're* disgusting. You smell like bitterleaf and old blood. At least my breakfast was alive this morning."

He stared at me. And for a moment, just a moment, something happened at the very corner of his mouth. It wasn't a smile, not yet. He wouldn't truly laugh for a long time, not until he met someone who could make him. But it was the place a smile would go, one day, if things went well.

He turned and kept walking. I finished my scorpion, and licked my whiskers, and trotted after him.

My tail was glowing. I'll let you work out why.

---

*End of Chapter Four*

# PART TWO: THE DESERT THAT WRITES


---

# Chapter Five
## Where the Dunes Speak

> *The sand writes; the wise read; the rest are buried.*
> — a proverb of the old caravan scribes

---

The desert doesn't care about you. That's the first thing you learn out there, and the last thing most people forget.

It isn't cruel. Cruelty takes attention. The desert simply goes on being what it is, hot by day and freezing by night, moving when it likes and staying still when it likes, and if you happen to die in it, it covers you up with the same patience it covers everything else.

Zayn knew that. It was the one advantage he had.

He'd spent fifteen years at its edge, digging through ruins it had swallowed, and he knew things about it that the people of Harrow's End had never bothered to learn. He knew to walk in the cool hours, before the Fire was high and after it had gone down. He knew to lie up through the white glare of noon in the shadow of a dune's steep side, with his scarf over his face. He knew that where a little grey-green tuft of thornweed grew at the bottom of a slope, there was sometimes damp sand a forearm's depth beneath it, and if you dug and waited, a little brown water would seep into the hole. He knew to turn flat stones over at dawn and lick the dew from their undersides.

"That's revolting," I said, the first morning I saw him do it.

He wiped his mouth. "You eat scorpions."

"Scorpions are a *meal*. That is a *rock*."

"It's water."

I had no answer to that.

---

We walked for six days.

The path the dunes had opened for us outside Harrow's End lasted only the first morning. After that, every night, the desert moved, and every dawn the way ahead looked different. Zayn would stand on the highest ridge he could find and turn slowly in a circle until something told him which way to go. He couldn't have explained what. A feeling in the feather mark, maybe. A tug. Some mornings it was strong and he'd set off without hesitating. Some mornings he stood there a long time.

He asked me questions. Not many. He'd never had anyone to ask questions of, and he wasn't sure how it worked.

"How old are you?"

"Older than you."

My tail glowed. He frowned at it.

"How much older?"

"Much."

It glowed again. He gave up on that one.

"Do you have a name?"

Nobody had asked me that in a very long time. "Kiri," I said.

He tried it out under his breath. *Kiri.* He didn't say it to the wind. He'd learned that much.

"Why did you watch me? All those years. In the village."

I thought about that for a while, padding along beside him in the cold blue hour before dawn.

"Because somebody had to," I said.

That one glowed brightest of all, and he didn't ask anything else for the rest of the day.

He touched the bell around his neck a lot. I don't think he knew he was doing it. And the feather mark, and sometimes, at night, the folded grey cloth inside his shirt where the broken golden feather lay. The golden cracks on his skin from healing Tobin had faded to pale lines, like old scars, and then to nothing. He checked them every morning anyway.

On the third night, he finished Auntie Sabba's bread. He ate the last of it very slowly, sitting on a dune with the Embers spread out over him, and he didn't say anything at all. The four squashed dates he left where they were. He wasn't there yet. He knew harder days were coming.

He was right about that, at least.

---

The dunes spoke on the fifth night.

We'd stopped at the foot of a long, curving ridge, and Zayn had scooped out a hollow in the sand to sleep in, the way he always did, so the cold wouldn't reach him so quickly. The Ash had risen late and lopsided, a little less than it had been the night before. I lay with my nose on my paws and watched him not sleep.

Then the sand began to move.

Not the great slow sliding of the night's shifting. Something smaller. Closer. On the long smooth face of the dune in front of us, the sand was rippling, the way water ripples when something passes underneath it. Fine lines were drawing themselves across the slope. Curves and hooks and straight strokes, crossing and joining. And as they formed, they began, very faintly, to glow.

Gold.

Zayn sat up so fast he sprayed sand everywhere.

The marks went on writing themselves. A line of them, and then a second line beneath it, glowing soft and steady across the dark face of the dune like Embers fallen out of the sky. They weren't pictures. They weren't random. Anyone could see they meant something. They had the shape of meaning, the way a voice in the next room has the shape of words even when you can't make them out.

Zayn got up and walked toward them. He stood at the foot of the slope with the gold light on his face, and he looked at them for a long, long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "I can't read."

I knew that, of course. Reading was for rich people: scribes and merchants and the lords of the oasis towns. Nobody had ever thought to teach a nameless scavenger his letters, and it had never mattered before.

It mattered now. I could see it matter. I could see it go into him like the word *monster* did.

"Kiri," he said. "What does it say?"

I didn't answer.

He turned around. "You can read it. Can't you."

"Yes."

My tail glowed. There was no point lying about that.

"Then tell me what it says."

"No."

He stared at me. In the light from the dune his face was very young and very angry. "*Why not?*"

"Because it isn't written for me," I said. "It's written for you. That's how this works. The Weaver writes the sands, and the bearer reads them. Not the fox. Never the fox. If I read it to you, it's just a fox telling you things, and you've had fifteen years of being told things by people who didn't know what they were talking about."

"It's written for me," he said, "in a way I can't understand. So it's written for nobody."

He said it flatly, without heat. That was worse.

"The Weaver forgot me in the sand when I was a baby," he went on. "And now it writes me letters I can't read. That's very funny. That's the funniest thing I've ever heard."

He turned his back on the dune and walked back to his hollow and lay down in it, facing away from the light.

The writing glowed on behind him for a long time. Then, slowly, the sand smoothed itself over, and the gold faded, and it was gone.

---

I'll tell you something now that I didn't tell him.

I read it. Of course I read it. I couldn't help it. I've been reading the sands since before there were cities in this desert.

The bottom line was short. A single word, written large.

I'm not going to tell you what it was. It wasn't mine to read, and it isn't mine to tell. But I lay awake the rest of that night with my nose on my paws, watching the place where it had been, and I don't think I've ever hated the Weaver quite so much as I did right then.

If he'd been able to read it that night, I wonder sometimes whether anything would have been different.

No. I know better. Nothing would have been different. It never is.

---

The Sandwraith came the next afternoon.

We were crossing a wide, flat basin between two lines of dunes, the kind of place where the sand lies smooth and pale as spilled flour. It was the hottest hour. We should have been lying up in shade, but there wasn't any shade in that basin, and Zayn had decided to push across it rather than wait.

I was the one who felt it first. A tremor in the sand under my paws. Small. Then again, stronger.

I stopped.

"Don't move," I said.

He stopped. To his credit, he didn't ask why. He'd learned something about me in six days.

The sand ahead of us bulged.

It rose up slowly, in a long low ridge, perhaps forty paces long, the way a blanket rises when something is moving beneath it. Then it sank. Then it rose again, closer, and this time I saw the sand on top of it darken, the pale gold going black as if something were soaking up through it.

"What is that?" Zayn whispered.

"Run," I said.

He ran.

The ground behind us *erupted*.

It came up out of the basin like a fountain of black sand, rearing, impossibly long, a serpent made of darkness and dust. It had no scales. It had no eyes. Its body was the black sand itself, endlessly pouring and re-forming, hissing as it moved, and where its head should have been there was only a wide blunt shape that opened in the middle like a split seam.

It was taller than any house in Harrow's End. It was still coming up out of the ground.

Sandwraith. They swim beneath the dunes the way fish swim beneath water, and when they find something warm, they come up and swallow it, and then they go back down.

Zayn had something warm inside him. Something that, as far as every creature of the Hollow was concerned, burned like a beacon.

It came down at us like a falling tower.

Zayn threw himself sideways. The wraith struck the place he'd been standing and went straight down into the sand, and the ground heaved. A wave of sand rolled out from the impact and knocked him off his feet. He rolled, came up spitting, his knife already in his hand.

"Keep *running*!" I shrieked.

The sand rippled to his left. He ran right. It burst up in front of him, a black wall of hissing dust, and he skidded, fell, scrambled back. It was playing with him, almost. Herding. The way a cat herds a mouse into a corner. It knew this basin, and he didn't.

The feather mark was burning. I could see it glowing through his shirt. He held out his hand, the way he had in Harrow's End, and the light came.

Grey.

Of course it came grey. He was terrified. A thin, sputtering, sickly light that flickered in his palm like a candle in a draught. The wraith didn't even pause. It reared up over him, blotting out the Fire, the split in its head opening wide.

And that was when I did something stupid.

---

I went for it.

I'm not a fighter. I want that understood. I'm small, and old, and I've survived as long as I have by being somewhere else when the teeth come out. But it was coming down on him, and I didn't think. I ran straight up the slope of loose sand at the base of its body and bit it.

It tasted like nothing. Like dust and cold.

The wraith twisted. A coil of black sand whipped round and caught me, and suddenly I was off the ground, wrapped in something that was pouring and squeezing at the same time. Sand filled my ears, my nose, my mouth. I couldn't breathe. I felt myself being pulled downward, toward the ground, toward the darkness under the dunes where the thing lived.

I heard Zayn shout my name.

Through a gap in the black sand, I saw him get to his feet.

And I saw the light change.

It's the same every time. You'd think I'd get used to it. One moment it was grey, sputtering, the light of a boy afraid for himself. The next, it wasn't. Because he wasn't afraid for himself anymore. He was looking at me, at a small rude fox being dragged into the sand. He forgot about his own fear completely, the way you forget about a splinter when the house is on fire.

The light went gold.

It roared up out of his chest like the Fire coming over the dunes. It poured down his arms and out of his hands, and he didn't throw it, exactly. He just *reached* for me, and the light reached with him.

It hit the Sandwraith square in the body.

Where the gold touched the black sand, the sand stopped pouring. It went still. It turned glassy and dark and then, with a sound like a thousand tiny bells breaking, it shattered. The coil around me burst apart. I fell, landed hard, rolled. Above me, the light raced up the Sandwraith's body the way fire races up a dry palm. The whole vast shape stiffened and shrieked. It was the first sound it had made, a high, thin, whistling scream. Then it collapsed in a great slithering rain of black glass and ash that pattered down over the basin for a long time.

When it was over, Zayn was on his knees in the sand.

Golden cracks were spreading up his forearms to the elbow, glowing, and down his neck from the line of his jaw. He was breathing in great ragged gasps. His hands were shaking.

He looked at me.

"Are you hurt?" he said.

I spat out a mouthful of sand. "*Am I hurt.* I've just been half eaten by a sand worm and saved by a boy who can't light a candle when he's frightened, and you want to know if I'm *hurt*."

"Are you?"

"No," I said.

My tail glowed. He let out a long breath and sat back on his heels.

"Good," he said.

And I had to look away from him for a moment, at the black glass scattered across the sand, because there was something in my eye. Sand, probably. There was a great deal of sand.

---

He didn't hear the humming until it was very close. Neither did I. I'll never forgive myself for that.

The wraith had made a lot of noise, and the light had made more, and light and noise bring things. We should have moved. Instead we sat in the sand among the black glass, catching our breath, and the first I knew of it was a shadow falling across Zayn's shoulders.

He looked up.

A figure stood on the ridge above us, against the white glare of the Fire.

Grey robes, wrapped close, the hems stirring in the hot wind. A hood. And under the hood, where a face should have been, a mask. Smooth and white and perfectly blank, with no mouth and no nose and no expression of any kind. Just two narrow slits for the eyes, and behind them, darkness.

In each hand the figure held a blade. They were long and thin and slightly curved, and they were grey. Not the grey of steel. The grey of the Hollow. Dull and dead, like light seen through dirty water. They didn't shine in the Fire at all.

The Unnamed. That's what they call themselves, the men in white masks. They burn their own names in a fire and take numbers instead. They worship forgetting the way other people worship the Weaver, and they hunt the bearer of the Light the way the Hounds do. But the Hounds are animals, and the Unnamed are not.

This one, we'd learn later, they called Brother Seven.

He came down the slope.

---

He moved like water running downhill. No wasted step, no sound, the grey robes barely stirring. Zayn scrambled to his feet with his rusty knife out in front of him, and he didn't even have time to set himself before the first blade came at him.

He blocked it. Somehow. More luck than skill. Steel met grey with a strange dull sound, like a stone dropped on sand. The second blade was already coming low, at his ribs. He twisted. It missed his side by a breath and sliced his sleeve open from wrist to elbow.

It didn't touch his skin. It didn't need to.

Zayn staggered. For an instant his face went blank and puzzled, as if he'd walked into a room and forgotten why. I saw him reach for something in his mind and find it gone.

That's what the grey blades do. They don't cut flesh so much as memory. Even a whisper of one, passing close, can take something with it.

Later, much later, he told me what it was. It was the taste of Hesset's tea. Smoke and mint. He'd drunk it every night for years and hated it every night and loved it, and he never remembered the taste of it again.

He swung at the masked figure, wild and desperate. Brother Seven stepped inside the swing, hooked his ankle, and put him on his back in the sand. One grey blade came down and rested against his throat.

Zayn froze.

The feather mark was blazing under his shirt, and the light was in his hands, but it was grey again, flickering, useless. He'd spent everything on the wraith. And grey blades, I should tell you, are the only thing in the world that can cut the Light. He couldn't have burned that blade away if he'd been rested and fearless and ten years older.

I flew at the figure's legs. A grey-robed knee caught me in the ribs without even looking and sent me tumbling.

When I got up, nothing had changed. Zayn on his back. The white mask looking down at him. The blade against his throat.

Brother Seven didn't move.

---

He didn't press down. He didn't draw back. He simply stood there, a blade at the boy's throat and the other held loose at his side, and looked at Zayn through the slits of that blank white mask. I couldn't see his eyes. I don't know what was in them.

It went on for a long time. Long enough for the hot wind to drop. Long enough for me to stop breathing.

And then, under the mask, very softly, he began to hum.

It wasn't loud. You could barely hear it over the hiss of the settling sand. A few notes, low and slow, climbing upward. Then a pause. Then the same notes again, and a little higher, like something lifting off the ground. Like wings.

Zayn's eyes went wide. I don't think he knew why. I don't think he knew he'd ever heard it before. But something in his face changed, the way the grey woman's eyes had changed in Harrow's End. Something deep down and long buried turning over in its sleep.

I knew that song.

I'm not going to tell you where I'd heard it, or who used to sing it, or to whom. Not yet. I knew it the moment the first note left that mask, and I sat in the sand with my fur standing up along my spine, and I said nothing. Not then, and not for a long, long time.

Add it to the list. It's getting long, I know.

The humming stopped.

Brother Seven took the blade away from Zayn's throat.

He stepped back. One step, then another. He slid both grey blades into the sheaths at his back with a single smooth motion. He looked down at the boy for one moment more, the blank white mask tilted very slightly to one side, as if he were puzzled by something. As if he were asking himself a question he didn't know how to answer.

Then he turned and walked away up the ridge, and over it, and was gone.

---

Zayn lay on his back in the sand for a long time, staring up at the sky.

"He let me go," he said finally.

"Yes."

"*Why?*"

"I don't know," I said.

My tail glowed. It was true, as far as it went. I didn't know why. I knew a great many other things about that masked figure. But why he had lowered his blade that day, why he would lower it again and again in the days to come, I truly didn't know. I'm not sure he did either.

Zayn turned his head and looked at me. He was learning, by then. He was learning to listen to the shape of what I said as well as the words.

"Do you know who he is?"

I got up and shook the sand out of my fur, very thoroughly, for a long time.

"It's going to be dark soon," I said. "We should find somewhere to sleep that isn't the middle of a wraith's hunting ground."

He looked at me a moment longer. I saw him notice that I hadn't answered. I saw him decide not to push.

He got up. His sleeve flapped open where the grey blade had cut it. He picked up his knife from the sand, and he started walking, and after a while, very softly, almost without meaning to, he began to hum. A few notes, low and slow, climbing.

He got it wrong. He didn't know the rest.

I walked beside him and didn't correct him, and the Fire went down red behind us over a basin full of black glass.

---

*End of Chapter Five*

# Chapter Six
## The Valley of Echoes

> *Shout your name in the valley, and it will shout back your fear.*
> — a warning scratched on a cairn at the valley's mouth, in a hand no one living can read

---

The dunes ended on the ninth day, and the ground fell open.

One moment we were walking along the crest of a long ridge with the desert rolling away gold on every side. The next, the sand simply stopped, the way a cloth stops at its hem, and below us was rock. Red rock, the colour of old blood and dried clay, split down the middle by a canyon so deep that the Fire couldn't reach the bottom of it.

It ran north as far as I could see. Its walls were sheer and banded, red and rust and pale orange, carved by some long-dead river into ledges and hollows and strange curved shapes like the insides of shells. At its mouth, half buried in drifted sand, stood a small cairn of piled stones with something scratched into the topmost one.

Zayn looked at the scratches for a while. Then he looked away. He'd stopped asking me to read things for him. He'd stopped asking since the night of the dunes.

"We have to go through," he said. "Don't we."

It wasn't really a question. The feather mark was tugging north, straight down into that red throat of stone. And on every other side, the dunes had closed in during the night, steep and soft and impossible to climb.

"The desert does like to make its point," I said.

---

"Listen to me," I told him, at the top of the path that wound down into the canyon. "Listen properly, for once in your life. This is the Valley of Echoes."

"Another story?"

"Every story is a story until it eats you. Listen. Whatever you do down there, don't talk. Don't shout. Don't call out. If you trip, swear inside your head. If you see something wonderful, keep it to yourself."

"Why?"

"Because the valley answers," I said. "Not with your voice. With what's underneath it. The things you're most afraid of. The things you'd never say out loud." I paused. "It's very good at finding them."

He looked at me. My tail was glowing in the shade of the canyon's lip, steady and bright.

"Have you been here before?" he asked.

"Yes."

"What did it say to you?"

I started down the path without answering. After a moment, I heard him follow.

---

It was cool at the bottom. That was the first surprise. After nine days of the Fire, the shade of the canyon was like stepping into water. The air smelled of stone and old damp. Somewhere far ahead, a trickle of something dripped from a ledge into a pool, and the sound of it went on and on, *tik, tik, tik*, echoing away up the walls until it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

We walked in silence.

The walls rose higher as we went. The strip of sky overhead narrowed to a ribbon of white-hot blue. Our footsteps echoed, every one, sent back to us faintly from the stone. Zayn walked carefully, setting his feet down soft as he could. I padded beside him without a sound. Foxes are very good at not making noise. It's one of our few real talents, besides being right.

For most of the morning, it worked.

Then, around midday, the path narrowed to a ledge along the canyon wall, with a long drop down to a dry streambed on one side. And a loose stone turned under Zayn's foot.

He caught himself. He didn't fall. But he gasped, the small sharp intake of breath anyone would make, and with it, before he could stop himself, came a word.

"*Ah*—"

It went up the canyon wall like a bird.

We both froze.

The echo came back. It should have come back as *ah*. That's what echoes do. They return what you give them.

This one didn't.

"*Thrown away*," said the valley.

---

It wasn't loud. That was almost the worst of it. It was soft, like someone whispering just behind his ear, and it was in his own voice. Zayn's voice, but younger. The voice of a small child.

"*Thrown away*," it said again, from farther up the canyon, and again, fainter, from somewhere ahead. "*Thrown away. Thrown away.*"

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

I could see him trying not to breathe. Trying not to make any sound at all. His face had gone very still and very pale under its sunburn, and his eyes were wide, and he was pressing so hard on the feather mark that his knuckles were white.

"*Mistake*," said the valley.

This voice was different. Older. Deeper. It was a man's voice, and I didn't recognise it. But it said the word the way you'd say it about a pot that had come out of the kiln cracked. Without anger. Just disappointment. A thing that hadn't turned out right and wasn't worth keeping.

"*Mistake. Mistake. Should have left him in the sand.*"

"Keep walking," I breathed. As quietly as I could. "Don't answer it. *Walk.*"

He walked. One foot in front of the other along the narrow ledge, with the drop on his left and the wall on his right and the voices coming from everywhere.

"*No name*," it said. "*No name. Nobody's. Nothing.*"

Then came a voice I *did* recognise. Everyone in Harrow's End would have recognised it. Sharp-tongued and cracking, a voice he'd heard at the well every day of his life.

"*Monster.*"

Zayn stopped walking.

---

"*Monster*," said Bram's voice, high up on the canyon wall. And then other voices joined it, dozens of them, from every ledge and hollow and crack in the stone, the whole village of Harrow's End shouting together, the way they had shouted with the stones in their hands.

"*Monster. Monster. Monster. Monster.*"

He turned to face it.

I knew what was going to happen. I'd known since the top of the path. I've walked with eleven bearers, and I've learned that there are some things you can warn a person against all you like. They'll do them anyway, because they have to. Because the alternative is to let the thing stand unanswered, and some people would rather die than do that.

"*I'm not!*" Zayn shouted.

It tore out of him, loud and raw. It slammed off the canyon walls and went racing away up the valley, and the valley caught it and turned it inside out and threw it back.

"*NOT A HERO*," the valley roared. "*NOT A HERO. NOT A HERO. NOBODY. NOBODY. FORGOTTEN.*"

It was his own voice again. His own voice now, not a child's, and that was the cruellest thing of all. Because none of the others had been true. Not *thrown away*, not *mistake*, not *monster*. Those were other people's words, and he'd heard them so often that they'd soaked into him like water into a cloth. But this one was *his*. This was the fear he'd kept in the deepest, darkest place in him, under the dreams of being a hero, under the stories of the Phoenix-Born, under everything.

That he'd do it all, every terrible thing, and in the end it wouldn't matter. Nobody would remember him at all.

"*FORGOTTEN*," howled the valley. "*FORGOTTEN. FORGOTTEN. FORGOTTEN.*"

He went down on his knees on the ledge, both hands over his ears.

---

I bit him.

Not hard. On the hand, just enough to sting. He yelped and jerked back and stared at me, and the yelp went up the canyon wall too, and the valley sent back something wordless and wet, like someone crying.

"Up," I said. I said it very low, almost no voice at all. "Get up and walk, or I'll bite you somewhere that'll make it hard to sit for a week."

He got up. He walked.

And I'll tell you something I'm not proud of. I had kept my own voice so low that whole time, so careful, so quiet. But when I'd said *up*, just that once, I'd forgotten myself. Only for a moment. Only a fraction louder than I meant to.

The valley heard me.

"*Again*," it whispered.

It was my voice. Old and thin and very tired.

"*Again. Again. Again.*" And then, so faint I almost didn't hear it, so faint I've sometimes told myself since that I imagined it: "*Too late.*"

Zayn turned his head and looked at me.

I didn't look back at him. I kept my eyes on the path and my mouth shut, and I walked, and after a while he walked too.

He never asked me about it. I've always been grateful for that. I'm not sure what I'd have told him.

---

The valley let us go a little before sunset.

The walls opened out, slowly, the way a fist unclenches. The ribbon of sky widened. The red rock gave way to scree and then to sand, and the canyon spilled us out onto a wide stony plain where a few thorn trees grew crooked and black against the sky. There was a pool there, under an overhang of rock. Real water, brown and cool and tasting of iron. Zayn drank until he was sick, and then drank again, more carefully.

Neither of us said anything for a long time.

The Fire went down. The Embers came out. The Ash rose late again, a little thinner than it had been the night before, a pale curl of white low over the canyon's mouth.

Zayn sat with his back against a rock and the grey cloth from Harrow's End wrapped round his shoulders. He took out the broken golden feather and turned it over in his hands, looking at it in the faint light. Then he put it away again.

"Is it true?" he said at last.

"Is what true?"

"What it said. In the valley. That I'll be—"

He stopped. He couldn't say the word. Not because it would burn, but because he was afraid it wouldn't.

"The valley doesn't tell the truth," I said. "It tells you what you're afraid of. That isn't the same thing."

My tail glowed. He looked at it for a long time, as if he were trying to decide whether to believe it.

Then he lay down on the stony ground, pulled the cloth up over his ears, and slept.

---

He dreamed.

He was standing on a plain, and the plain was grey.

Not the grey of the Hollow at the edge of Harrow's End, not exactly. Softer than that. Quieter. A wide, flat, endless grey that stretched away to every horizon, without a single dune or stone or blade of grass to break it. There was no sky. Just more grey, overhead, pressing down like a lid. There was no sound.

And there were sheep.

They moved slowly across the plain around him, dozens of them, grazing on nothing. They were made of grey mist. When they turned their heads, he could see through them. They had no eyes.

Among them, leaning on a long crook, stood a boy.

He was small. Smaller than Zayn. Perhaps twelve or thirteen, thin as a stick, in a patched brown robe that was the only colour anywhere in that whole grey world. It was too big for him, as if it had belonged to someone older once. His feet were bare. His hair was dark and tangled, and his face was turned away, and he was watching the sheep.

"They always come back," the boy said. "Have you noticed? No matter how far they wander. You just stand still, and wait, and they come back."

His voice was soft and ordinary. A little hoarse, as if he hadn't used it in a long time.

Zayn tried to speak, and found he could.

"Who are you?"

The boy thought about it. He thought about it for a long time, frowning slightly, the way you'd frown trying to remember where you'd left something.

"I don't remember," he said at last. He didn't sound upset about it. "I used to. I'm almost sure I used to." He shrugged his thin shoulders. "It doesn't matter. That's what I wanted to tell you, really. It doesn't matter at all."

He turned his head.

His face was a boy's face, ordinary, thin and brown and dusty, with a narrow chin and a crooked front tooth. All except his eyes. Where his eyes should have been, there were two small round holes, and through them Zayn could see the grey. The endless, empty grey.

"I heard you in the valley," the boy said gently. "*Forgotten.* You're right to be afraid of it. You should be. I'm sorry. I wish someone had been honest with me, when I was where you are."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" The boy smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the terrible thing. It was the kindest smile anyone had given Zayn in his whole life, except perhaps Auntie Sabba's, at the edge of the village. "They've told you the stories. The Phoenix-Born. They rise like the fire-bird, they save everyone, they're remembered forever." He shook his head slowly. "No one remembers the bearers."

The grey sheep had stopped grazing. They had all turned their eyeless faces toward Zayn.

"They light lanterns for them once a year, out in the towns," the boy said. "Do you know that? On the Night of Embers. A whole sky full of little lights. And not one person holding a lantern could tell you a single bearer's name. Not one. Not their faces. Not what they loved, or who they were, or whether they were frightened at the end." He tilted his head. "They don't remember the bearers. They remember the *light*. That's all you are to them. Something to be used up."

"That isn't true."

The words came out of Zayn's mouth, and as they did, the feather mark on his chest gave a single sharp throb. Not quite a burn. Not quite. A warning.

The boy saw it. His hollow eyes went to the mark.

"Isn't it?" he said softly. "You can't even say it without it hurting."

He came closer. He walked across the grey ground without leaving footprints, and the mist-sheep parted around him like water, and he stopped an arm's length away and looked up at Zayn with those two holes full of nothing.

"Ask the fox," he whispered. "Ask the fox what he isn't telling you."

And he reached out one small, dusty hand toward Zayn's chest, toward the feather, and where his fingers came near, the light under Zayn's skin began to go grey.

---

Zayn woke with a shout.

He was sitting bolt upright on the stony ground with the grey cloth tangled round his legs, and his hands were glowing. Not gold. Grey. A dim, cold, sickly light, like the light on that endless plain. It flickered in the cracks between his fingers and then went out.

The Embers were fading. Dawn was close.

And I was standing over him with every hair on my body standing on end.

"What did you see?" I said.

He told me. I could see that he didn't want to, but he couldn't help it. He never could help telling the truth. The grey plain. The sheep made of mist. The boy with the crook and the patched brown robe and the eyes like holes. *No one remembers the bearers.* *Ask the fox what he isn't telling you.*

I listened to all of it without a word.

When he'd finished, I was quiet for a moment. Then I said:

"Don't listen to him."

My voice came out strange. Low and rough, as if something had got caught in my throat.

"Kiri—"

"Don't listen to him," I said again. "Whatever he says to you. However kind he sounds. However sad. Don't you *dare* listen to him." The fur along my spine was standing straight up. I could feel my silver eye burning. "I knew him. He's a liar."

My tail blazed. Brighter than I think he'd ever seen it, brighter than the lamp in Hesset's tent, so bright it threw my shadow out long across the stones.

Zayn stared at it. Then at me.

"You *knew* him?" he said. "Who is he? What is he? He said he didn't remember his own name. He said—"

"That's all," I said.

"But—"

"That's *all*."

I turned my back on him and walked away across the stony plain, up onto a tall flat rock at the edge of the canyon, and I sat there with my tail wrapped round my feet.

He didn't follow. He had learned that much about me, too.

---

I sat on that rock until the Fire came up.

I looked at the sky the whole time. Not at the boy. At the sky, pale and empty and paling further, where the last few Embers were going out one by one. I don't know whether the Weaver lives up there. I don't know whether it lives anywhere. But it's where I look when I need to be angry at something that never answers.

*You let him in,* I thought at it. *You let him walk right into the boy's sleep. Already. Nine days out of Harrow's End, and you let him in.*

The sky said nothing. It never does.

I'll tell you what I didn't tell Zayn, since you're listening.

His name was Malek.

He was a shepherd. A small, poor, ordinary shepherd boy, a long time ago, before the City of Glass was glass, before there was any Veimore or any Harrow's End. He had a crooked front tooth and a patched brown robe that had been his father's, and he was frightened of the dark, and he used to talk to his sheep because nobody else would listen.

Nobody except me.

He was the first. The very first child who ever carried the Light. And I was his friend. His only friend, really. I walked beside him the whole way, and I loved him the way you love the first of anything, before you've learned to be careful.

He called me a liar once, too. At the end. Just before.

I've told you the valley says what you're afraid of. It isn't always the truth. But on that ledge, when it whispered *again* in my own voice, it wasn't wrong. It had found the thing I was most afraid of, and it had said it perfectly.

That I would walk beside one more child to that dune. And I'd watch it happen again. And again. And I wouldn't be able to stop it, any more than I'd been able to stop it the first time, when the first boy I ever loved turned his back on the Light and walked into the dark.

Behind me, I heard Zayn get up. I heard him fold the grey cloth and tuck it away. I heard him come and stand at the foot of my rock, quietly, not saying anything.

After a while I climbed down.

"North," I said. My voice was almost normal. "Come on. There's a caravan road out past those thorn trees, unless the dunes have eaten it. You need water, and you need food that isn't dew off rocks and squashed dates, and you need to be somewhere with other people for a while, so you'll stop having conversations with things in your head."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"Kiri," he said. "Was he really a liar?"

"Yes," I said.

My tail glowed.

He nodded slowly. Then he shouldered his small bundle of everything he owned, and we walked out across the stony plain together toward the thorn trees, with the Fire rising red on our right and the Valley of Echoes silent at our backs.

It was silent. I listened very carefully. But I could have sworn, just as we passed out of earshot, that somewhere deep in the red stone, something whispered: *forgotten.*

---

*End of Chapter Six*

# Chapter Seven
## The Caravan and the Beast

> *When the sky goes dark at noon, pray to the Fire or run.*
> — a caravan saying, older than any caravan still on the road

---

You smell a caravan long before you see it.

Camel, mostly, which gets into the back of your throat and sets up house there. Then dung smoke, and spice, and lizard, and sixty people who haven't washed in a month because water is money and money is for drinking.

To Zayn, it smelled like a city. He'd never seen one.

We found the road in the middle of the morning, a line of hard-packed ground marked with stone cairns, running north and west between the dunes. And on it, crawling along like a great brown caterpillar, was the caravan of Master Dagan.

Forty camels, roped nose to tail, groaning under bales and water jars. A dozen wagons on wide wooden wheels. Six sand lizards, long and low and scaled the colour of dust, their riders perched on high saddles, ranging along the flanks like dogs around a flock. Hooded falcons rode on perches above the lead wagon. Every now and then one was thrown into the air with a roll of paper tied to its leg, and went climbing away over the dunes with news for the oasis towns ahead.

Zayn stood on the ridge above the road and watched it pass with his mouth slightly open.

"Close your mouth," I said. "You'll swallow a fly. Or a camel."

---

Master Dagan was a broad, bald, sunburnt man with a beard dyed orange with henna and a voice that could have cracked rock. He looked Zayn up and down the way he'd look at a camel he suspected of being lame.

"Name?"

Zayn hesitated. Then he said the only true thing he could. "In my village they called me Sandborn."

"That's not a name, that's a weather report." Dagan grunted. "Doesn't matter. One of my boys ran off at the last well and I'm short a pair of hands. You can load, you can dig, you can shovel dung?"

"Yes."

"You'll work for water and food and a place by the fire. No tokens. You don't like it, the desert's that way." He jerked a thumb at the horizon, then squinted down at me. "What's that?"

"A fox," said Zayn.

"It's *my* fox," I said, before I could stop myself. "And it has a name, thank you."

There was a long pause.

"Huh," said Master Dagan at last. "Talking fox. Saw a two-headed goat in the salt towns once." He turned away, already shouting at someone about a loose rope. "Keep it out of the food stores."

That was all. Caravan men have seen everything, or claim to have. It's one of the things I like about them.

---

Zayn worked.

He was good at it. He loaded bales at dawn and dug wagon wheels out of drifted sand at noon and walked the column at dusk with a shovel and a sack. He didn't complain. He kept his scarf wound high around his throat and his shirt tied close across his chest, so nobody would see the faint glow of the feather mark after dark.

He was happy. I don't think he knew it. But I knew what his face looked like when nobody was throwing anything at it.

On the second night, a boy came and sat beside him at the fire.

His name was Pell. He was the youngest lizard rider in the caravan, sixteen or seventeen, with a gap between his front teeth and a long scar down one forearm where his lizard had bitten him the first week. She was a big female called Dustmother, and he talked about her the way some people talk about their grandmothers.

"She'll go three days without water," he told Zayn. "Three! A camel only does that if it's got nowhere better to be. Dustmother does it for spite."

He'd brought dates. Plain ones, not honeyed, but he held out the cloth to Zayn without being asked, and after a moment Zayn took one.

"You're from the edge villages," Pell said. "I can tell. You walk like the sand's going to bite you."

"It does, sometimes."

Pell laughed. It was an easy laugh, loud and unworried. Zayn looked at him as if he'd never heard one up close before.

They talked late into the night. Or Pell talked, and Zayn listened: about the oasis town where Pell's family kept lizards, and how you trained falcons, and the rumours along the road. There was a lot of grey out east this season, Pell said. Whole villages gone quiet. And up in Veimore, the great red city, the old king had thrown his own eldest son out into the desert for dark magic. Hiding a Hollow-touched boy in the palace, people said, and trying to cure him.

"Cure him!" Pell shook his head. "You can't cure the grey. Everybody knows that."

Zayn said nothing. His hand had gone to his chest.

I lay by the fire with my eyes half closed and said nothing either. My tail stayed very still.

---

On the third day, at noon, the falcons wouldn't fly.

Dagan's falconer threw the lead bird into the air, and it went up three wingbeats and came straight back to its perch and sat there with its feathers flattened, shivering. He tried another. The same.

The camels started groaning, all down the line. Then, one by one, without being told, they began to kneel.

"Kiri," Zayn said quietly.

"I know," I said.

The wind had stopped.

Out here, the wind never stops completely. Now there was nothing. The air hung heavy and hot and dead. The Fire was high and white above us, and the sky was clear blue in every direction.

Except to the east.

To the east, low on the horizon, there was a line of black.

At first it looked like a sandstorm, and I saw the caravan men relax a little. A storm they knew. Dagan was already bellowing orders. Circle the wagons, rope the camels down, cover the water.

But it wasn't moving like a storm.

Storms run *with* the wind. This was coming against it, out of the dead air, rising higher as it came, and it wasn't brown or yellow like sand. It was black, like the inside of a closed fist.

And it was getting dark.

Noon, the Fire at its height, and the light was going out of the world. Quickly. As if something were drinking it.

"When the sky goes dark at noon," said Pell beside us, very softly. All the laughter had gone out of his voice. "Pray to the Fire or run."

"Run," I said. "Run now. All of you. Leave the wagons, cut the camels loose, *run*—"

Nobody listened. Nobody ever does.

---

It hit us like the end of the world.

The wind came first, a wall of it, so hard it knocked Zayn flat. Sand came screaming sideways with it. A camel broke its rope and went bolting past us into the dark. Then it was impossible to hear anything over the roar.

Then the darkness came down over the caravan like a lid.

And inside it, something moved.

I've seen most of the creatures of the Hollow, including some I won't tell you about because you're too young. I've seen nothing like the Veilbeast. Nobody has. Nobody who's seen it all at once and remembered.

It was the storm, and it was something *inside* the storm, and you couldn't tell where one stopped and the other began. It was vast. Bigger than the caravan. Bigger than Harrow's End. Its body was the black wind itself, coiling and folding, and in the folds of it were shapes. Limbs, perhaps. Or the suggestion of limbs, huge and slow, reaching. High up, so high you had to tip your head back to see, there were lights in the dark. Red ones. Dozens of them, scattered across the black like coals blown out of a fire.

Eyes.

And beneath them, in the very heart of the storm, a hole. A great round silent place where even the darkness was darker. Where the roaring wind went in and didn't come out.

It lowered itself over the caravan.

---

The front of the caravan went first: the lead wagon, the falcons, Dagan's own camel. The dark closed over them, and when it lifted there was only smooth sand where they'd been.

Then the camels behind, one after another, roped nose to tail. The dark took the rope and pulled them in like beads off a string.

The people screamed. Then the screams cut off, one by one, not in pain but in *forgetting*, as if each of them had stopped halfway and couldn't remember why they'd started.

That's what the Veilbeast does. It doesn't eat you. It swallows you, and inside it is the Hollow, all of it at once, deep and total. What comes out the other side, if anything does, doesn't know its own face.

Zayn was on his feet. I don't know how. His scarf had torn loose, and the feather mark was blazing through his shirt, so bright it lit the sand around him.

And the Veilbeast's red eyes, high up in the storm, turned toward it.

All of them. At once.

"*Down*," I screamed at him. "Get *down*, it can see you, it can *see* you—"

The light in his hands came grey.

It came grey and thin and terrified. Zayn stood in the screaming dark with a grey candle in his hands and the eyes of the Veilbeast fixed on him, and I watched the fear go all the way into him.

Then, through the sand, I saw Pell.

His lizard was down. Dustmother lay on her side in the sand, kicking, and Pell was pinned beneath her, one leg trapped under her weight. He was screaming Zayn's name. Not his name. *Sandborn.* It was the only name he knew.

And the edge of the dark was coming toward him.

Zayn ran.

He ran straight into the wind, toward the boy who had shared dates with him, and somewhere in those ten or twelve steps the light changed, grey to gold, like a coal catching. He threw himself down over Pell and the struggling lizard, his arms spread wide, and the light came out of him in a dome. A shell of gold, a bubble of it, closing over the three of them like cupped hands.

The darkness came down on top of it.

---

I was outside the light. I want you to know that. I wasn't brave. I'd gone flat in the lee of an overturned wagon, flat as a lizard, and I stayed there.

But I could see him through the sand. A small gold glow in the middle of a world gone black. The Veilbeast pressed down on it, and the gold bent, and buckled, and flickered. It held.

And then, slowly, like a tide going out, the darkness lifted.

The black wall of the storm went rolling away west across the dunes, still against the wind, its red eyes winking out into the distance. The light came back. It was still noon. It had all taken less time than it takes to eat a meal.

I got up.

The caravan of Master Dagan was gone.

Forty camels, a dozen wagons, six sand lizards, the falcons, the water, and some sixty people. The road was swept clean. No bodies. No blood. A few bales half buried in the sand, and a single wagon wheel, still spinning slowly on its side.

Far off across the dunes, small grey figures were walking away, slow and aimless, the way Husks walk. One of them had a beard dyed orange with henna. He didn't look back.

In the middle of that great swept emptiness, a small golden glow went out.

---

Zayn was lying in a drift of sand with his arms still spread. The golden cracks were everywhere: hands, arms, throat, face, running across his cheeks like the glaze on an old pot. He was shaking so hard his teeth clattered.

Of Pell, there was only a hand sticking up out of the sand.

The storm had buried him, him and the lizard both. Zayn dug. He had nothing left, and he dug anyway, with his bare cracked hands, flinging sand back between his knees like a dog, until he found a shoulder, and a face, and dragged the boy out by the collar.

Dustmother was dead beneath him. She'd died with her head turned toward her rider.

Pell was alive. Mostly.

His leg was broken. And the left side of him, arm and shoulder and face, had gone grey where the edge of the dark had touched him before the gold closed over. His left eye was white. He was mumbling names, half finished, trailing off into nothing.

Zayn dragged himself onto his knees and looked at him for a long time.

"Don't," I said. "Look at you. You've nothing left. You do this now and it'll bring every Hound for a day's ride."

He laid his hands on Pell's face anyway.

---

It came gold this time without being asked. Just warmth, flowing out of him and into the boy, and the grey began to lift.

He saw Pell's life. A small boy at an oasis town, tossing scraps to baby lizards the size of his hand. A first ride, a first fall, a first bite, and Pell refusing to cry about it. Then, forward: Pell older, grey at the temples, sitting by a fire with children around him, telling a story. Zayn couldn't hear what story. He could only see Pell's hands moving, describing something huge.

And then the other vision.

Longer, now. Clearer. The full Ash above him, so vast and white it seemed to be falling. The Crown Dune under his bare feet. He knew the name of it now, though nobody had ever told him. He felt the wind up there, cold and clean. He felt his arms rise, and the fire start in his chest and spread, and this time it didn't stop at a flash. It went on. He felt himself coming apart into light.

Below him, someone was shouting a name.

He could almost hear it. It was right there on the edge of the wind, and it was one he didn't know.

Then it was gone.

---

Pell opened his eyes. Both of them brown.

The grey was gone from his face and arm. His leg was straight. He took a breath, a huge shuddering one, and looked up.

At Zayn, cracked all over with glowing gold, light still leaking from his eyes. At the fox with one silver eye. At the swept, empty desert where an hour before there had been a caravan and everyone he knew.

I watched him put it together. I watched him do the sum, the way frightened people always do, quick and wrong.

"It looked at you," Pell whispered. "I saw it. It came, and it looked straight at *you*."

Zayn didn't answer.

"It came for you." Pell was scrambling backward through the sand, away from him, his newly healed leg kicking. "Didn't it? You brought it. You're the thing it was looking for. Everyone's gone, Dagan's gone, they're all *gone*, and you—"

His face twisted.

"*Monster.*"

Zayn flinched. Like a slap.

"I'm not a monster," he said.

My tail glowed. It glowed bright and steady and true. But I watched it closely, all the while Pell was talking, and here's the thing I've never been able to forgive.

When Pell said *it came for you*, my tail didn't dim.

Not even a flicker.

Pell got to his feet, swaying on the leg Zayn had mended. Then he turned and ran, stumbling, out across the empty sand, and he didn't look back. Not once.

Zayn knelt where he'd left him and watched him go.

---

The ground began to move under us.

A trembling, then a long, slow sliding, the sand beginning to sink and turn like water going down a drain. Where the caravan had been, a hollow was opening, wider and deeper.

Far off to the west, at the edge of the sky, the black storm had stopped moving.

Its red eyes had turned back toward us.

"*Up*," I said. "Zayn. Up. *Now*."

He tried. He'd given everything to the dome and the rest to Pell, and there was nothing left. He got halfway up and the sand slid out from under him, pouring now, pulling him toward the centre of the hollow.

I grabbed his collar in my teeth and pulled. I felt the silver half of me straining, wanting to come out, wanting to *grow*, and I almost let it.

The sand was at his waist. At his chest.

Then came the hoofbeats.

---

The pale camel came over the edge of the hollow at a dead run, and the rider in grey leaned so far out of the saddle I was sure he'd fall. A wrapped hand closed on Zayn's arm. Then a heave, a grunt, and Zayn came up out of the sand like a cork from a bottle and was thrown across the saddle face down.

"The *fox*," Zayn gasped. "Kiri—"

The rider didn't hesitate. He scooped me up by the scruff with the butt of the long spear, a trick I'd like to say I appreciated, and dropped me on top of the boy, and the camel was already turning, already running, out of the hollow and away from the storm.

Behind us, the Veilbeast was coming back.

I saw it over the rider's shoulder, rising black against the sky. And on the ridge between us and it, a grey robe, flapping. A smooth white mask.

Brother Seven was standing in our path.

He drew one grey blade as the camel thundered toward him and swung it at the boy slung across the saddle. A clean, fast, perfect stroke. It went past Zayn's throat a finger's width away and cut nothing but air.

Brother Seven doesn't miss by accident. I've seen him fight. He stood there on the ridge and watched us go, the blade hanging at his side, and the storm came down on the place where he stood and he didn't move.

Then I lost sight of him in the sand.

---

The rider didn't stop until the Fire was going down.

By then the storm was a smudge on the horizon behind us, and the camel was blowing hard. The rider slid Zayn off onto firm ground and set me down beside him. He crouched over the boy without touching him and looked at the golden cracks on the boy's face for a long, long time.

Then he stood and pointed with the long spear. North and west.

"Wait," Zayn croaked. "Who are you? Why do you keep—"

The rider mounted, and in a few moments was gone into the dusk, the way he'd gone the first time, without a word.

"That's twice," Zayn whispered.

"Yes," I said.

"Kiri, who *is* he?"

I didn't answer that. You know by now that I didn't.

---

We walked the way the spear had pointed. We had nothing else.

Almost nothing. When I went to sniff at the place where the rider had crouched, I found a water skin lying in the sand. Half full. He hadn't said a word about it. He'd simply left it there, the way Auntie Sabba used to leave her bundles.

Zayn made it last as long as he could. By the middle of the next afternoon the skin hung flat and empty against his hip, his lips had split, and he stumbled every few hundred steps. The golden lines on his skin faded slowly to a dull shimmer. Near dusk he took out the twist of waxed paper from Harrow's End, the squashed dates rolled in sand and honey, and ate one. Just one. He held it in his mouth a long time.

He didn't say anything about Pell, or the caravan, or Dagan's orange beard walking away into the dunes, or my tail when Pell said *it came for you*. He'd seen it. I know he had. He didn't say a word.

The Ash came up late that night. It was thin, thinner than I'd seen it since we left Harrow's End, a pale scratch of white low over the dunes. It hardly gave any light at all.

And somewhere ahead of us, so faint that only a fox could have felt it, something was humming.

I felt it first in my teeth. Then in the pads of my feet. A deep, low, steady note rising out of the dark to the north and west, exactly where the spear had pointed, as if the desert were singing itself to sleep.

I knew that sound, and what it did to people who drank from it. And I knew the rider had not pointed us at it by accident.

I didn't tell Zayn. He was walking with his head down and his hand pressed to the feather mark, and he hadn't heard it yet. In a little while he would.

After eleven bearers, you'd think I'd know what waits beyond every dune. I don't. That night I didn't know that the desert had saved one more thing for him, out there in the dark by the singing water. Something that wasn't a monster, or a vision, or a stranger in grey who rode away without a word.

If I had known, I'd have made him walk faster.

---

*End of Chapter Seven*

# Chapter Eight
## The Singing Wells

> *Drink from the wells, and the past will drink from you.*
> — a saying of the water-finders, older than any town that uses it

---

The Ash was thin the night we found the Singing Wells.

Thin as the smile of a trader who has just cheated you and would like you to come back tomorrow. It hung low over the western dunes, a pale scratch on the dark, and gave almost no light at all.

That suited Zayn. He'd had enough of being seen.

He had barely spoken since the beast came down out of the black sky and the caravan was simply *gone*, camels and lizards and all. Since he'd dug one man out of the sand with his bare hands and poured the Light into him until his own arms cracked gold, and the man had opened his eyes and looked at those glowing hands and said the word.

The cracks from that healing were still fading on his forearms, thin lines of dull honey under the skin. Every so often he'd look down at them and then look away fast.

"You can talk to me, you know," I said. "I'm told I'm an excellent listener."

"Who told you that?"

"No one. I was hoping you'd be the first."

The corner of his mouth moved. It wasn't a smile, but I've worked with less.

His water skin had been empty since the afternoon before. His lips had split. His feet dragged. He kept his hand pressed to the feather mark, as though it were the only thing holding him upright.

Then he stopped.

"Do you hear that?" he said.

I did. I'd been hearing it for some time and hoping he wouldn't.

It was a hum. Low and steady and very large, as if the whole desert were humming to itself with its mouth closed. When Zayn stood still you could feel it coming up through the soles of your feet and into your teeth.

"Water," Zayn whispered.

"Unfortunately," I said.

---

You'll want to know why a fox would be unhappy about water in the middle of the Destiny Desert.

The Singing Wells are older than Veimore, older than the glass city, older than most of the names on the map. Seven stone well-mouths sit in a ring among date palms, and the water in them hums. The water-finders will tell you it's the voice of the Weaver, or the ghosts of everyone who ever drowned in them. Water-finders will tell you anything if you buy them a drink.

What I know is this. The water is sweet and cold, and it remembers. Drink from it, and it will hand you back something you had forgotten so completely that you didn't know there was a hole where it used to be.

People think that sounds like a gift.

People have never had their own past drink them back.

"Zayn," I said. "Before you go running in there with your tongue out—"

But he'd stopped again, and this time it wasn't the hum.

From somewhere ahead, beyond the dark shapes of the palms, came a snarl. Then a clang of steel. Then a sound I had not expected to hear anywhere within three days' walk of the Hollow.

Someone was laughing.

---

We came over the last low dune and looked down into the grove.

The palms stood black against the Embers. The seven well-mouths glowed faintly between them, a soft silver light rising off the water like breath on a cold morning. And in the middle of all that quiet beauty, a young man was fighting five Hollow Hounds by himself.

I've described the Husks to you. Let me tell you about the Hounds.

They are wolves, or the memory of wolves, made of shadow the way smoke is made of fire: solid in the middle and fraying at the edges. Their eyes are embers. Not the gentle Embers in the sky; the angry red coals at the bottom of a fire that's burned too long. They make one sound only, a low, wet snarl like tearing cloth.

They hunt the Light. That's what they are for.

The young man didn't seem to care.

He was tall, a few years older than Zayn, with dark hair tied back and a jaw that had been badly cut once and healed into a pale seam. He wore a plain, dusty desert robe. But he held his sword the way I've only ever seen kings' guards hold them, high and easy, the point never wandering. Or the half of the point he had left. The sword was broken. Snapped clean a hand's width above the guard and a little more, so that what remained was barely longer than a long knife, with a jagged end like a broken tooth.

He moved like water poured from a jug.

"No, not you," he was saying, sidestepping a lunge. "You, the ugly one. Yes. You know who you are."

He spun. The broken blade went through the nearest Hound's throat. It didn't bleed. It just burst, the way a dropped sack of flour bursts, into a cloud of fine black ash that drifted down onto the sand.

"Four," said the young man cheerfully. "Who's next? Don't all volunteer at once."

---

Zayn had dropped flat on his belly behind the crest of the dune. His hand was on his knife.

"Not our business," I said.

"He's alone."

"He seems to be enjoying it."

That was true. My tail glowed with it. But the young man was also bleeding from a long scratch on his left shoulder, and he was breathing harder than his voice let on, and one of the four remaining Hounds had begun, very quietly, to circle around behind him while the others kept him busy.

"They're not here for him, you know," I said. I said it before I could stop myself. "Hounds hunt the Light. They smelled you coming a day ago. They were waiting at the water for you. He just got in the way."

Zayn turned and looked at me.

I wish I hadn't said it. But it was true, and my tail was glowing so bright it lit the sand between us.

He got up.

---

Here's the thing you need to understand about Zayn and the Light by this point in the story. He didn't trust it.

It had saved Bram. It had pulled Bram's little brother out of the grey. It had saved a man from the sand after the beast. And every time, the people it saved had looked at him afterwards with the same face, and that face had a word in it, and the word had driven him out of his village with stones at his back.

So when he ran down that dune toward the Hounds, the first thing he felt wasn't courage. It was a cold, sick little voice in his head saying: *He'll see. He'll see what you are, and then he'll look at you like they all do.*

The light came, and it came grey.

It flickered out of his fists, thin and dim as dirty water, and the Hound that was circling behind the young man stopped. It turned its ember eyes toward Zayn. It lowered its head and grinned the way only shadows can grin.

Fear does that. I've told you. It turns the Light into something the dark can chew.

The Hound sprang at him. Zayn threw himself sideways into the sand and felt its teeth snap shut on the air beside his ear. He rolled. He came up on his knees. And over the Hound's smoking back he saw the young man stagger as a second Hound caught his wounded shoulder and dragged him half off his feet.

The young man didn't cry out. He set his teeth and swung the broken sword one-handed. He kept standing up.

And Zayn thought, with no words at all, *Not him too.*

The light went gold.

---

It didn't blaze the way it had in Harrow's End. He was too worn through for that. But it came up out of his chest warm and steady, like the Fire climbing over a dune, and it ran down his arm and into the rusted blade of his scavenged knife until the metal shone like it was made of honey.

The Hound in front of him flinched.

Zayn lunged, and for the first time in his life his knife went *into* a creature of the Hollow instead of sliding off. The Hound burst into ash. Zayn ran through the falling cloud of it, and slammed his shoulder into the Hound that had the young man's arm, and the light went through it like lamp-oil going up.

Then there was a back against his back.

A warm, solid weight, someone else's shoulder blades pressed against his own. The last two Hounds paced in a slow circle around them, snarling their tearing-cloth snarl, and the young man shifted his feet so the two of them could turn together, like the hands of a water clock.

The young man glanced back over his shoulder. He looked at Zayn's face, and Zayn's arms, and the gold pouring out of Zayn's skin and lighting up the palms, the wells and the falling ash.

"You glow," he said.

A pause, while a Hound feinted and drew back.

"That's weird."

"I know," said Zayn.

"I didn't say it was *bad*." The young man turned forward again. "Duck."

Zayn ducked. The broken sword whistled through the air where his head had been and took the leaping Hound clean across the chest. Ash rained down on both of them. The last Hound looked at the young man, then at the boy made of light, and seemed to do some quick arithmetic. It turned to run.

Zayn's light reached it first.

---

The grove went quiet. Only the wells were still humming, low and patient, the way they'd been humming for a thousand years.

The gold drained back into Zayn's chest. It left him swaying on his feet with new cracks running up both forearms, brighter than the old ones, and that was when it came: the thing that always came.

The full Ash, white and enormous.

The Crown Dune, so high its top was lost in the sky.

Fire, rising up through him. His own arms, opening.

And this time, something new. A sound, somewhere below him on the dune. Someone shouting. Someone shouting a *name*, over and over, in a voice cracked down the middle, and he couldn't hear what the name was. He leaned toward it with everything in him, and he couldn't hear it.

"Hey. *Hey.*"

A hand on his shoulder. Zayn blinked, and the desert came back: palms, wells, Embers, the thin scratch of Ash in the west. He was sitting in the sand. He didn't remember sitting down. The young man was crouched in front of him, his broken sword laid across his knees, looking at him with frank, dark-eyed interest.

"You went somewhere," the young man said. "Your eyes did a thing."

Zayn looked up at the sky, at the Ash. It was thin. It was so very thin. He let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

I didn't tell him how fast an Ash fills up. It seemed unkind.

"Does it hurt?" the young man asked.

Zayn stared at him.

He nodded at the golden cracks on Zayn's arms. As if it were a perfectly normal question to ask someone who had just turned into a lamp in front of you.

In fifteen years of being Zayn, no one had ever asked him that.

"Yes," said Zayn.

My tail glowed. The young man glanced at it, then back at Zayn, and nodded slowly.

"Thought so," he said. "Your face looked like mine does when I've been punched." He stood up and held out a hand, and when Zayn took it he pulled him to his feet in one easy motion. "Right. Excuse me a moment. I need to collect my wages."

---

He took a clay jar from a pack lying by the nearest palm, knelt in the sand, and began to scoop up Hound-ash with a flat wooden spoon.

Zayn watched him do it for a long time.

"What," he said finally, "are you doing?"

"Making a living." The young man didn't look up. "Glassblowers in the free towns pay a water token a pinch for this. It makes black glass that won't break." He tapped the jar. "A good Hound's worth a week of water. You just doubled my income, glowing stranger. I'd thank you, but I'm going to have to give you half, and I'm still sad about it."

"You can keep it."

"Absolutely not. I have principles." He considered that. "One. It's that one."

He sealed the jar and sat back on his heels. In the silver light from the wells, I saw the ring on his right hand for the first time. It was heavy and gold, too heavy and too gold for a dusty young man who scraped up monster-ash for a living. It was carved with a crest I had seen before, on banners and on great red city gates.

I kept that to myself. I keep a lot of things to myself. You may have noticed.

"Soren," said the young man, and pointed at his own chest.

He said it out loud. Right into the open night, with the wind moving in the palm fronds over his head.

Zayn flinched as if he'd dropped a lamp. "You shouldn't— The wind—"

"The wind and I have an understanding," said Soren. "It's never done me a single favour, and I've never asked it for one." He raised his eyebrows. "You've got one too, I assume. A name. Or do I keep calling you glowing stranger? I don't mind. It's got a ring to it."

Zayn's hand went to his chest. He pressed the mark under his shirt. He looked at the wind moving in the palms.

Then he said, so quietly that I barely heard it, "Zayn."

"Zayn," Soren repeated, at a perfectly normal volume. "Good. Easy to shout when you're about to be eaten." He looked down. "And who's this?"

That was me. I had trotted down from the dune and was sitting by the nearest well, washing ash off my front paw with what I felt was considerable dignity.

"Kiri," I said. "Yes, I talk. No, it isn't a trick. Yes, I've heard every joke."

Soren looked at me, then at Zayn.

"Your fox talks," he said to Zayn.

"He's not my fox," said Zayn.

"I'm not his fox," I said, at exactly the same time.

Soren took this in. Then he shrugged. "Fine," he said. "I've had a strange year."

He didn't scream. He didn't make the sign against evil, which is what a man two hundred years ago did right before he fell into his own well. He just went back to tying up his shoulder with a strip of cloth, one end held in his teeth.

I decided I liked him. I didn't say so. I have standards.

---

Zayn's knees chose that moment to remember that he hadn't had any water for a day and a half. He sat down rather suddenly beside the nearest well.

The water inside was black and still, and the hum was so loud this close that the stone lip shivered under his hand. His throat clenched.

"Careful," said Soren. He'd stopped tying his bandage. "You know what these are?"

"The Singing Wells," said Zayn. "Kiri says they remember."

"Kiri's right." Soren came and crouched beside him. For the first time his voice wasn't joking. "First drink is the worst. It'll hand you something back. Something you didn't know you'd lost." He looked down into the black water. "I drank once. My first night out here. I saw my mother's face. She died when I was three. I didn't know I remembered her at all." He was quiet a moment. "Now I strain it through cloth and boil it, and it still hums at me a bit. But it doesn't sing."

Zayn looked at the water.

I knew what he was thinking. A boy found alone in the sand had nothing back there to remember: no face, no voice, nothing but a fox sitting a little way off, watching. Whatever the well handed him, it couldn't be worse than the emptiness he already had.

"Zayn," I said.

He looked at me.

I could have stopped him. One word would have done it.

I didn't say it. I've thought about why, many times, and I'll let you decide whether it was kindness.

He cupped his hands, dipped them into the Singing Well, and drank.

---

It was cold enough to ache in his teeth, and sweeter than any water he'd ever bought with a clay token. He swallowed, and the humming came inside him, filling his chest and his skull until it wasn't humming anymore.

It was singing.

And the desert went away.

He was small. Smaller than he had ever been, wrapped tight in something soft, warm all the way through for the first time in his life. It was dark, and he wasn't afraid of the dark. He couldn't see faces, only the orange flicker of a fire somewhere close.

But he could feel a hand.

It was a child's hand. Small, a little sticky, very warm, and it had hold of his entire fist. It wasn't letting go. It squeezed, gently and then tighter, the way you'd squeeze something to make sure it was real, the way he himself pressed the feather mark on his chest when he was afraid. Someone was holding on to him. Someone small had decided he was worth holding on to.

And above him, somewhere in the warm dark, a man was singing.

His voice was low and rough and a little out of tune. It was a song about fire, and about wings, and about something that falls into the ashes and rises up out of them again. Zayn didn't know the words. He couldn't hold on to them. They slid away like water through his fingers. But the tune went into him and stayed. It went into him like a hook.

Because he knew it.

He'd heard it before. Not long ago. He'd heard it hummed, out in the dunes, behind a smooth white mask, by a man with twin grey blades who had cornered him in the dark and then, for no reason at all, let him go.

---

Zayn came up out of the water gasping.

He was on his knees by the well, his face wet, and not all of it was well-water. He pressed both hands to his chest as if he could keep something in there.

Soren didn't say anything.

That was the second thing he did that night that no one had ever done for Zayn. He didn't ask questions. He didn't laugh, or stare, or tell him to stop. He just sat down in the sand beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the palms, and waited.

After a long time he said, "Good one or bad one?"

Zayn wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"I don't know," he said.

My tail glowed. It was the truest thing he could have said.

Later, when Soren had built a fire between two of the wells, Zayn came and sat by me. He didn't look at me. He kept his eyes on the flames.

"Kiri," he said softly. "That song. Do you know it?"

The hum of the wells filled up the silence.

"Lots of people sing about fire," I said at last. "It's the desert. There isn't much else to sing about."

My tail glowed, because every word of that was true.

That's the trouble with the truth. You can hide a whole lie in the spaces between it, and no tail in the world will ever give you away.

---

Soren shared his flatbread, hard as roof tiles, and the boiled, strained water from the tin pot. It only hummed a little.

"You can stay, if you like," Soren said, poking the fire with the end of his broken sword. He said it lightly, as though it didn't matter. It mattered a great deal. I could hear it in the spaces between the words. "Fire's big enough for two." He glanced over at me. "Three."

"I eat scorpions," I said. "I won't be touching your bread."

"Then you're cheap company. Even better."

Zayn lay down on the far side of the fire with his patched cloak pulled over him. I watched him do something I had never once seen him do in fifteen years. He fell asleep with his knife lying in the sand beside him, instead of held in his hand.

Soren sat up a while longer, turning the heavy gold ring on his finger. Then he lay down too.

I stayed awake. Someone has to.

The wells hummed. The Embers turned. And low over the western dunes, the thin Ash hung like a white thread, like the first stitch in something that would take exactly one turn of the sky to finish.

I didn't count the nights that first night. I should have. If I'd started counting from the beginning, I'd have known exactly how many they had, and I could have told you every one.

I started the next night instead.

I've never been able to stop.

---

*End of Chapter Eight*

# Chapter Nine
## A Prince with a Broken Sword

> *A broken blade still cuts, if the hand is true.*
> — words cut above the door of an old swordsmith's forge

---

Night one. I counted it at dawn, which is cheating, but I'm the one telling this story and I'll count how I like.

Zayn woke to the sound of someone fighting nobody.

He came up out of his cloak with his knife already in his hand, the way he'd woken every morning of his life, and found Soren in the open sand between the wells, moving through the grey light before the Fire. He was barefoot. His broken sword flashed and turned and cut at the air in long, looping patterns. He stepped and spun and stepped again, with his breath coming slow and even, as if the whole thing were a dance he'd been taught before he could walk.

Which, as it turned out, it was.

Zayn sat and watched him for a long time. He'd learned to fight from bullies and falling ceilings. He'd never seen fighting that was *beautiful* before. He hadn't known it could be.

"You're staring," Soren said, without stopping.

"Sorry."

"Don't be. I'm magnificent." He finished the pattern with the broken point an inch from a palm trunk, held it there a moment, then lowered it. "Taught by the finest swordmaster in the city, and then by every guard who wanted to knock a prince on his backside and call it a lesson. I've never lost a fight."

From somewhere inside Zayn's pack, muffled by the cloth around it, came a small, clear *ting*. He'd kept the bell wrapped up in there since the caravan, where nobody could see it glint and ask.

Soren stopped. He looked at the pack. He looked at Zayn.

"It's a truth bell," said Zayn. "It rings when someone lies to me."

I glanced down at my tail. It had gone as dim as an old wick.

"Ah," said Soren. He sheathed his sword with great dignity. "Twice. I've lost twice. Once to a palace guard when I was nine, and once to a camel."

No bell. My tail glowed. Zayn's mouth twitched at one corner.

"A *camel*?"

"It was a very large camel, and I don't want to talk about it."

---

Something was wrong with that sword, and Zayn noticed it while Soren was cleaning sand out of the guard.

The blade, of course, was snapped. That was obvious. But the hilt was strange too. It was wrapped in old dark leather, and the pommel at the end was a round cap of bronze that didn't quite match the rest, as though it had been made for a different sword.

"Why don't you fix it?" he asked.

Soren didn't look up. "Some things you don't fix."

My tail stayed bright. It was true; it just wasn't an answer.

"The old smiths of my city made their hilts hollow," Soren said after a moment, as if he'd decided to give Zayn a different answer instead. He twisted the bronze pommel, and it turned and came off in his hand. Inside the grip was a narrow empty space, about the length of a finger. "You're supposed to keep a prayer in here, written on a scrap of paper. So the Weaver rides with you into battle."

"What's in yours?"

Soren tipped the hilt toward him. Empty.

"I was never much good at praying," he said, and screwed the pommel back on.

Zayn nodded slowly. "Me neither."

It was a small thing. Two boys admitting a small thing to each other beside a well. But I've learned to watch small things. They're usually where the big ones are hiding.

---

He told his story that night, by the fire, with the wells humming around them like a crowd that had come to listen.

Zayn didn't ask. That's important. Zayn had never in his life asked anyone to tell him who they were, because asking meant they'd ask him back. But somewhere in the long hot day, between Soren showing him how to hold a blade properly and Zayn showing Soren which palm fronds had grubs in them, something had loosened between them. When the Embers came out, Soren just started talking.

"I'm from Veimore," he said.

Even Zayn, who had never been farther than the Old Ruins until a few weeks ago, knew that name. Everyone in the desert knows it.

"The City of the Dead and Darkness," Soren said, and smiled crookedly at the fire. "That's what the free towns call it. They're not wrong. The walls are red. Not sunset red. Blood red, and very high. There are lanterns on every wall, thousands of them, and they burn day and night, and they must never all go out. Not ever. There are men whose whole lives are just walking the walls with oil jars, filling lanterns." He poked the fire. "And under the city there are catacombs, deeper than wells. And on the walls of every street, someone's painted the names of the dead. Every name. Back to the beginning. You walk to the market and you're walking past your great-great-grandmother."

"It sounds…" Zayn searched for a word.

"Grim. Yes. It's lovely, though, in its own way. A city that refuses to forget anybody." He was quiet. "Mostly."

He turned the heavy gold ring on his finger.

"My father is King Absalon," he said. "I'm his eldest son. Or I was. It's complicated."

My tail glowed like a lamp. Zayn looked at it, and then at the young man who had spent last night scraping Hound-ash into a jar with a wooden spoon, and his mouth fell slightly open.

"You're a *prince*."

"Was. Probably still am, technically. Nobody's quite sure. I think it depends who you ask." Soren shrugged. "Close your mouth. You'll get sand in it."

---

There was a boy called Nim.

He worked in the palace stables, Soren said, looking after the riding lizards and the king's white camels. He was maybe twelve. He had ears that stuck out like jug handles, and he laughed like a donkey with hiccups, and he knew the name of every animal in the stables and told them good morning each one, every day.

"I used to hide out there," Soren said. "When lessons got too much. When my father got too much. Nim never told anyone. He'd just hand me a brush and point at a lizard."

One night Nim was sent outside the walls on an errand, past the last lantern. He came back grey.

"Not all the way," Soren said. "Not a Husk. But half of him was gone. He didn't know the lizards' names anymore. He didn't laugh. He'd just stand there, looking at his hands." He was staring into the fire now, and his voice had gone flat and careful, like someone walking on a roof. "The law says the emptied are to be taken outside the walls and given to the dunes. Before they spread it. So I hid him."

"Where?"

"An old storeroom over the stables. I brought him food. I sat with him every night. I talked to him about the lizards. I read him the names off the walls, all the old names, thinking maybe if he heard enough names he'd find his own again." Soren swallowed. "Once, after about a month, he almost smiled. I swear he did. *Almost*."

The fire cracked. Nobody said anything.

"The Vizier found out," Soren said. "Orrin. My father's advisor. He's—" He stopped. "He's very soft-spoken. He's never once raised his voice in my hearing. He has grey eyes, pale grey, like someone left them out in the Fire too long. And he stood in front of my father's throne and said, very gently, that the crown prince of Veimore had been hiding a creature of the Hollow inside the palace walls. Practising dark magic on it. *Treason*, he called it. He said it so kindly, Zayn. Like he was sorry he had to."

"Was it true?"

"I was hiding him," said Soren. "The rest was a lie."

My tail glowed for both halves of that.

"They sent guards that night. Six of them. I stood in the storeroom door." He touched the hilt of the broken sword lying beside him. "One of them had an iron shield, and I hit it wrong. I was trying to get past it to the one who'd grabbed Nim's arm. The blade went in half. I kept fighting with what was left." He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I'm very good with what's left. I've had practice."

"And Nim?"

The wells hummed.

"Orrin had him taken away," Soren said. "That's all anyone would tell me. *Taken away.* I asked everyone. I asked the guards and the gatekeepers and my father. Nobody knows where. I've been looking for most of a year. I sell ash, and I ask in every free town, and nobody's ever seen a boy with jug-handle ears."

"And your father—"

"Wouldn't look at me." Soren's voice didn't change. That was the worst part. "Orrin did all the talking. My father just sat there. And then I was outside the gates with a broken sword and a bag of flatbread, and that was that."

He picked up a stick and drew in the sand with it, absently. A small figure. A stick-boy.

"My brother Idan ran after me," he said. "He's ten. He got past two guards and all the way to the outer gate in his bare feet. He was crying so hard he couldn't talk. He just grabbed my robe and wouldn't let go." His mouth twisted. "They had to carry him back. I can still hear him."

Zayn looked at the stick-boy in the sand for a long time.

Then, very quietly, because a story was owed for a story and Zayn always paid what he owed, he told Soren about Harrow's End.

He told him about the stones. He told him about the scar under his shoulder blade, and Bram, and the lane full of Husks, and the light, and Bram's little brother, and the faces afterwards. He told him about the word. He told him every time somebody had used it.

When he finished, Soren didn't say anything for a while.

"They'll call you a monster, Zayn," he said at last. "They called me one too." He looked up from the fire, and the crooked smile came back, a little shaky at the edges. "Let's be monsters together, then."

My tail lit up the whole grove.

---

Night four. That was the night of the scorpions.

I want to say first that it was not my fault. I want that understood.

I had caught a fine fat one under a flat stone at the edge of the grove, a golden desert scorpion about the length of Soren's hand. I brought it back to the fire to eat it, as is my right. I crunched it slowly and with great enthusiasm, because I've found over the centuries that nothing ruins a bearer's appetite faster. Zayn turned away and made a small sick noise, which was what I'd been going for.

Soren, however, leaned in.

"What does it taste like?"

"Like victory," I said. "And a little like old boots."

"Can I try one?"

Zayn turned back round. "*No.*"

"Why not? He eats them every day. He looks fine."

"I am older than your city, and half of me is—" I stopped. "I have a very strong stomach."

Soren had already got up. He came back a short while later with his own scorpion pinned under the flat of his broken sword, looking deeply pleased with himself. He pulled the sting off with two fingers, the way he'd watched me do it, and held the thing up to the firelight.

"Soren," said Zayn.

"Don't be a coward, Zayn. Princes eat all sorts of things. I once ate a whole roasted lizard eye at a feast because the ambassador from the salt towns was watching."

He put the scorpion in his mouth, and crunched.

His face did several interesting things in a row. Then he swallowed, nodded slowly, and said, "Huh. Actually, that's not so—"

He was sick for a full day.

---

I've watched a great many people be ill in my time. Plague, fever, the bitter cough that comes off the salt flats. Soren being sick from a scorpion was not the worst of it. But it was by far the loudest.

He lay in the shade of the palms with his arms flung out like a man who'd been dropped from a great height. He groaned. He turned interesting shades of green. Every so often he rolled over and was sick into the sand. Then he rolled back and announced, to the sky in general, that he was dying.

"Tell Idan," he croaked, "that I died bravely. In battle. Against a beast of terrible size."

"It was the length of your hand," I said.

"Tell him it was *this big*." He tried to spread his arms and gave up halfway. "Zayn. Zayn. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"I'm leaving you my sword. It's broken. Look after it. Don't let the fox near it. He'll eat it."

And Zayn laughed.

It wasn't a big laugh. It came out of him in a short, surprised burst, like a bird startled out of a bush. Then another one, bigger, and then he had his hand pressed over his mouth and his shoulders were shaking. He tried to stop, and couldn't, and that only made it worse.

Soren lifted his head off the sand. His face was green and his hair was full of grit.

"Is that—" he said. "Kiri. Is he *laughing*? I'm dying and he's *laughing*."

"It would appear so," I said.

I'll tell you something now, and you can do what you like with it.

I had not heard Zayn laugh in eight years. Not since he was seven, and gave himself a name, and sat on the edge of the well in Harrow's End saying it over and over under his breath until it made him giggle. After that, the village had taken the laughing out of him, the way they took everything else. One stone at a time.

And here was this green, groaning, ridiculous prince, flat on his back in the sand at the Singing Wells, and he had given it back without even trying.

Zayn offered to help him. He held out his hands, with the faint gold already stirring under the skin.

"Absolutely not," Soren moaned. "Every Hound for three days would come running, and I'd have to fight them lying down. I'll be sick the honest way, thank you."

So Zayn sat with him instead. He brought him water, strained and boiled, and wet a cloth for his forehead. All through the long hot afternoon, every so often, he'd look over at Soren groaning on the sand and start laughing again.

I didn't eat any scorpions in front of either of them for three days after that. I'd like that noted. It was a sacrifice.

---

The lessons started on the sixth night.

It was Soren's idea. He'd been sitting by the fire watching Zayn try to make sense of some marks someone had scratched into the stone lip of the oldest well, centuries ago, and finally he said, "You can't read, can you?"

Zayn's whole body went stiff. His hand went to his chest.

"No."

He said it to the fire. In Harrow's End, not being able to read was nothing. Hardly anyone could. Only the rich could read, the rich and the priests and the people in cities, people like Soren. And somewhere under his ribs Zayn had already started to dread the moment Soren would realise exactly how far below him he was.

But Soren just smoothed a patch of sand flat with his palm and picked up a stick.

"Right," he said. "Come here."

He drew a shape. A sharp line, a slant, another line, like a lightning flash lying on its side.

"That's a *zed*," he said. "It makes a *zzz* sound. Like a fly. Or like the start of you." Beside it he drew three more shapes. "*Ah*. *Yuh*. *Nnn*."

He sat back.

"That's your name," he said.

Zayn stared at it.

Four shapes in the sand, lit by the fire, wobbly and slightly crooked, because Soren wrote like a prince who'd always had somebody else to write for him. *ZAYN*. The only thing in the world that had ever been his, sitting there outside of him, where anybody could see it.

"Is it—" Zayn's voice cracked. "Is that like saying it? To the wind?"

"The wind can't read," said Soren.

"You'd be surprised," I said.

They both looked at me. I looked at the fire. My tail glowed, steady and calm.

Zayn copied the four shapes, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. Then he did it again. Then again. He was still doing it long after Soren had fallen asleep, by the light of the fire and then by the light of the Embers, until the patch of sand in front of him was covered edge to edge with his own name.

In the morning, the wind had smoothed it all away.

He wrote it again.

---

It went both ways. That's what I want you to understand about the two of them. Nobody was rescuing anybody. Every night Soren taught Zayn a few more letters, and every day Zayn taught Soren how not to die.

He showed him how to stretch a cloth over a hole in the sand before dawn so the dew would gather on it and drip into a cup. He showed him which grey-green plants had water in their roots and which had poison. He showed him that if you dig on the outside bend of a dry stream bed, not the inside, you'll sometimes find damp. He taught him to smell a sandstorm coming a whole day off, a dry metal smell like an old coin. He taught him to listen for the change in the sand's hiss that meant the wind was turning.

"And when the dunes move at night," Zayn said, "you don't follow the sand. The sand lies. You follow the Embers. They never move." He pointed up. "That bright one. And those three in a line. Keep them on your left and you're walking east."

Soren looked up at the sky for a long time.

"Nobody ever taught me that," he said. "I had six tutors. Not one of them ever taught me anything that would keep me alive."

"Nobody ever taught me either," said Zayn. "I just didn't die."

They looked at each other over the fire. Then Soren started laughing, and after a moment, so did Zayn.

---

On the tenth night, Soren reached into his pack and pulled out a small clay pot with a wax seal, and two handfuls of dates he'd shaken down from the palms.

"Honey," he said, cracking the wax. "From a beekeeper in the salt towns. Cost me two whole Hounds. Don't tell anyone, it ruins my reputation as a dangerous exile."

Zayn went very still.

He told Soren then about the cloth bundles outside his tent, and the dates rolled in honey, and how he always saved them. He'd keep them for days, sometimes weeks, for the hardest days. As if sweetness was something you could store up like water.

Soren listened. Then he dipped a date in the pot and held it out.

"Then let's have it on an easy day," he said. "For once. See what that's like."

Zayn took it.

They ate the whole pot. Every date. They licked their fingers and argued about which of the Embers was brightest, and I sat between them in the warm, and my tail glowed so steadily all night that I gave up trying to hide it.

I counted that night. I counted all of them. The thin Ash had gone and left the sky dark, and now it was coming back, a little fuller every evening, like a cup being slowly filled.

When Zayn told him that Old Hesset had sent him to find the City of Glass, Soren didn't even pretend to think about it.

"I'll come," he said. "I know roughly where it is. And frankly, you need someone with you who can read the signs."

"I can read *some* letters now."

"You can read your own name, Kiri's name and the word for *camel*." Soren grinned. "I'm coming."

My tail stayed bright. He meant it entirely.

I looked up at the Ash, filling. I didn't say anything.

---

*End of Chapter Nine*


---

# Chapter Ten
## The City of Glass

> *Here the first one said no, and the sand wept glass.*
> — scratched into a glass wall, in a hand no one living can read

---

Night fourteen. We saw the City of Glass at sunset, which is the only proper way to see it, and the worst.

It rose out of the dunes like something the desert had coughed up and couldn't swallow back. Towers of it, and walls and broken arches, and whole streets of glass running away into the sand. Not clear glass, like a window. Glass the colour of everything: green as a bottle, black as oil, amber as honey, and here and there a strange cloudy red. When the Fire went down behind it, the light went *into* the glass and stayed there, and for a few moments the whole city burned from the inside, every tower lit up like a lantern.

Soren stopped walking.

"Weaver's teeth," he said softly.

Zayn didn't say anything at all. His hand was on his chest. The mark under his shirt had been warm all day, and now that the city was in front of him it was hot, throbbing in time with his heartbeat.

I sat down in the sand and did not look at it.

---

It had taken us four days to walk there from the Singing Wells. Zayn led us by the Embers at night, and Soren read us the stones by day: old waymarkers, half-buried, with letters carved into them that had been worn almost smooth. They were good days. I want you to know that. Soren complained about his feet, and Zayn found water twice in places Soren swore there couldn't be any, and every evening they sat by a small fire while Soren drew letters in the sand and Zayn copied them.

He'd learned a good many by then. He could write his own name, and Soren's, and mine. He could write *water* and *fire*, and *camel*, which Soren insisted was the most useful word in any language. And on the twelfth night, sitting under the Embers, he'd asked Soren to show him the sky-words.

"Fire," Soren had said, drawing. "Embers. And Ash. That one's easy. Three letters. *Ah. Sss. Huh.*"

Zayn had copied it ten times. He didn't know yet why it mattered. Neither did Soren.

I did. I could have stopped the lesson. I didn't.

---

"Here the first one said no," I said, when they'd both stared at the city long enough. "That's what the old stories say. The first bearer came here, a long time ago. There was a city then, an ordinary one, stone and clay. He stood on that hill over there," I nodded toward a glassy rise at the heart of it, "and the Weaver asked him to give the Light away, and he said *no*. And the Light went the wrong way. It went *inward*. It burned so hot the sand around him melted. The whole city." I watched a cloud of glass-dust blow across the dunes. "The sand wept glass."

My tail was glowing. Every word of it was true.

"How do you know that?" Zayn asked.

"Stories," I said.

My tail stayed bright, because I'd *been* the story. But Zayn didn't know that, and I didn't tell him.

I'd stood on that hill. I'd watched a small, poor, furious boy with a shepherd's crook close his hands around the Light instead of opening them. I'd felt the heat of it singe my whiskers. I'd called his name over and over, and he'd looked straight at me and hadn't known who I was.

I hadn't been back in a very long time. I'd have been happy never to come back at all.

"Kiri?" said Zayn.

"I'm fine," I said. "Glass makes my paws cold. Let's get inside before the Hounds smell us."

My tail flickered. That one was only half true, and Zayn saw it, and for once he was kind enough not to say so.

---

The murals were in the great hall at the centre of the city.

It might have been a temple once. It was hard to tell. The roof was gone, and the walls were glass all the way up, smooth and dark, and the evening came down through the open top in long violet bars. And in the walls, *inside* the walls, deep in the glass like insects in amber, there were pictures.

Nobody had painted them. Nobody had carved them. They were simply *there*, floating in the glass, the way your reflection floats in water. The glass had remembered them.

There were eleven.

They ran in a long curve around the hall, each one taller than a man. Under each one, set into the glass in pale gold, ran a line of letters.

Soren had gone off to find the old cistern. He said a city that size must have had one, and we were down to half a skin of water. "Don't touch anything," he'd said as he left. "Especially you, fox."

So it was only Zayn and me in the great hall when he started to read.

---

He went slowly. You have to understand how slowly. Two weeks before, he hadn't known a single letter. Now he stood in front of each mural with his lips moving, sounding out the gold shapes underneath one by one, the way a child sounds them out. *Thuh. Eh.* A long pause. His finger traced the letters on the glass without quite touching them.

He couldn't read most of it. The titles were too long, the old words too strange. But he could read *some*. He could pick out letters he knew, and short words, and every so often a whole word came clear for him like a coin turning up in the sand.

"*Smith*," he said, in front of the third mural. It showed a broad man with a hammer. "The… Smith."

In the fifth he found *king*. In the seventh, *salt*.

He stopped in front of the first one for a long time.

It showed a small boy holding a crook. The boy was turning away, out of the picture, his face hidden. The glass all around him was black and cracked, as if something had struck it from the inside. I didn't look at it. I looked at the floor. I am very good at looking at the floor when I need to.

"Is that—" Zayn said.

"Keep reading," I said.

He did.

The ninth mural showed an old man walking away from a dune with his hands pressed over his eyes. Zayn looked at it for a long while, and something moved across his face that he didn't put into words. I didn't help him. I'll tell you about that one later. I promise.

The eleventh was clouded, as if the glass had breathed on itself. You could just make out a figure running. Nothing else.

And then he went back to the beginning and looked at them all again, together. That was when he saw it.

---

It wasn't the words that told him first. It was the pictures.

Not all of them. In the first, the boy with the crook was turning away. In the ninth, the old man was walking away. In the eleventh, whoever it was was running. But in the other eight, the picture was the same.

A dune. A very high dune, so high that its top went up out of the picture into the dark. A full white Ash hanging above it, enormous, filling half the sky. And on the top of that dune, a figure with its arms open, and fire rising up out of it like wings.

Eight times. Eight different people. A woman with long hair. The broad man with his hammer. A boy who looked like he was made of white crystals. A man with a falcon lifting from his wrist. Each one of them on that same dune, under that same Ash, with their arms open, and burning.

Zayn had seen that picture before.

He'd seen it every time he used the Light. He'd seen it in Harrow's End with Bram's little brother in his arms. He'd seen it at the Singing Wells, with a stranger's back pressed against his own. Every time, a little longer and a little clearer. He'd told himself it was a dream, or a warning, or the Light showing off. He'd told himself it was a picture of something that *might* happen.

He went back to the first of the eight, the woman with long hair. His finger found the line of gold letters underneath, and moved along it, letter by letter, past all the words he didn't know. Right to the very end, where there was one short word on its own.

Three letters.

*Ah. Sss. Huh.*

"*Ash*," Zayn whispered.

He went to the next mural, the Smith. The last word was *ash*.

The next. *Ash*.

The next. *Ash*. *Ash*. *Ash*. Eight times, eight murals, the same word at the end of each line. He'd written it ten times in the sand two nights ago by the light of a small fire, and Soren had told him it was an easy one.

He stood in the middle of the great hall of the City of Glass with the night coming down on him through the broken roof, and he understood.

It wasn't a picture of something that *might* happen.

The Phoenix-Born didn't save everyone and go home. They didn't grow old. They weren't given feasts. They climbed the Crown Dune under the full Ash and they opened their arms and they burned, every one of them who'd been brave enough, and the grey went away, and they were *ash*.

*They are remembered*, Hesset had said, in his tent full of herbs and bells, when Zayn asked what happened after.

No bell had rung. It was true, after all.

---

He didn't cry. I'd half expected him to. He'd cried at the Singing Wells, and that was only for a song.

He just sat down on the glass floor, slowly, the way a very old man sits, and pulled his knees up to his chest. His hand was pressed flat over the feather mark. He was very quiet for a long time.

Then he said, without looking at me, "You knew."

"Yes."

My tail glowed. I couldn't have lied about that if I'd wanted to. I'm not even sure I wanted to.

"Hesset knew."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly. He looked up at the eight figures in the glass, burning, burning, burning. In the dark the gold in them shone faintly, like coals under ash.

And then he asked me.

He asked it in a very small voice. It wasn't the voice of a boy who'd fought Husks with a rusty knife, or run into a pack of Hounds to save a stranger. It was the voice of a seven-year-old sitting on the edge of a well, saying his own name over and over so he wouldn't forget it.

"Kiri," he said. "Is there a way I don't have to die?"

---

I want to tell you about that moment properly. I've told you I can't stand liars. That's true. I've spent more years than I can count sitting beside people who lie, to me and to each other and to themselves. I have watched my own tail go dark for them over and over, and every time, I've thought a little less of them.

I have walked beside eleven bearers. I'd never lied to one. Not once.

And I looked at that boy on the glass floor, with his knees pulled up and his hand on his chest, and the gold-lettered word *ash* written eight times around him in the dark. I thought: *He's fifteen. He's only just learned to laugh. He has a friend for the first time in his life, and that friend is somewhere in this city right now looking for water, and he'll come back soon, and they'll eat together and argue about the Embers.*

I thought: *Let him have that. Just for a little while. Let him have a little longer.*

So I said, "Yes."

---

My tail went dark.

Not dim. *Dark*. It went out like a lamp in a gust of wind, all at once, so that the only light left in the hall was the faint gold glow inside the glass.

And somewhere in Zayn's pack, muffled in its cloth, Hesset's little bronze bell gave one small, clear note.

*Ting.*

Zayn was looking at my tail. He'd looked at it before he'd even finished asking. Of course he had. He'd been looking at my tail every time anyone spoke, ever since Harrow's End.

He didn't say anything.

That was the worst part. I'd been ready for anything else. I'd been ready for him to shout at me, or call me a liar, or turn his back. I'd have deserved all of it. But he just looked at my dark tail for a long moment, and then he looked away. He looked up at the eight burning figures in the glass, and he nodded slightly, as if I'd confirmed something.

Which, of course, I had.

"Okay," he said quietly.

That was all.

I've told a great many hard truths in my life. I've told mothers what happened to their sons. I've told kings their cities were going to fall. None of it ever hurt as much as that one small lie, and that one small word he gave me back.

I'll be honest with you now, because I owe somebody the truth about it. I don't know whether I lied to protect him, or to protect myself from watching his face when I said *no*.

Both, probably. People are rarely only one thing. Not even foxes.

---

Soren came back with a full water skin and glass-dust in his hair, talking before he was even through the doorway.

"There's a cistern under the east side, it's full, it's disgusting, there's something living in it but I think it's only a frog. Zayn, you have to see the—"

He stopped.

He looked at Zayn on the floor. He looked at me, with my tail still dark as an old coal. He looked up, slowly, at the eight figures burning in the glass.

"What happened?"

Zayn told him.

He couldn't have done anything else. Lying wasn't open to him. And I think, even if it had been, he wouldn't have lied to Soren. He told him all of it: the dune, the Ash, the word *ash* eight times over, and the vision he'd been having since Harrow's End. He told it in a flat, quiet voice, staring at the floor.

He didn't tell him what I'd said. He left that out. I've never forgotten that he left that out.

When he'd finished, Soren set down the water skin very carefully. Then he walked to the first of the eight murals and began to read it. Properly, out loud, every word. He read it like a prince who'd had six tutors.

"*The Weaver's Daughter*," he read, "*who climbed the Crown Dune under the full Ash and opened her hands, and the grey went away. And of her there remained only*—" His voice caught. "*Ash.*"

He went to the next one. And the next. His voice got quieter and harder with each one. He read all eight. Then he read the first, the ninth and the eleventh. *Refused.* *Refused.* *Refused.*

Then he stood in the middle of the hall with his fists clenched at his sides.

"No," he said.

"Soren—"

"*No*." He swung round. His eyes were too bright in the dark. "Look. Look at them. Three of them said no. Three of them *lived*."

"The first one turned into the Hollow," Zayn said quietly. "Kiri told me. This whole city is what happened when he said no."

"Then he said no *wrong*." Soren's voice rang off the glass. "There's eleven of them, Zayn. Eleven. Eight burned and three ran. That's two ways. Who says there are only two? Who says there isn't a third, or a tenth? Who *decided* that? Some sand? A wall?"

"The Weaver."

"Then the Weaver can explain itself to me in person." He jabbed his broken sword at the open roof, at the dark sky and the swelling Ash. "I mean it. I've already had one person taken away from me because someone decided there was only one way things could go. I'm not standing here and letting it happen again. I'm *not*."

My tail glowed. He meant every word of it. He couldn't have meant it more.

Zayn looked at him for a long time.

And there, on the floor of the City of Glass, something happened that I'll remember until the Embers go out. Zayn smiled. It was small and tired and terribly sad. But it was a real smile.

"Okay," he said. "A third way."

He didn't believe it. I could see that. But he'd seen how much Soren needed him to say it, and he'd said the only true thing he could find. He *would* look for one. That was true. My tail glowed faintly gold for him, the first light it had shown since my lie.

---

They slept in the great hall that night, because it was the only place in the city out of the wind.

Soren lay down with his back to the wall and his broken sword across his chest, facing the doorway. He didn't say much else. Once, in the dark, I heard him say, "Idan would like this place," to nobody. Then he was quiet.

Zayn didn't sleep for a long time.

He sat cross-legged on the glass floor with his pack open in front of him. He took out the broken golden feather Hesset had given him, the one that had been found with him in the sand. He turned it over in his fingers. In the dim light of the murals, it seemed to me that the break across its middle had closed. Only a little. Only by the width of a hair. As if facing one hard truth had mended something in it.

He didn't notice. He was too busy looking at the twelfth place on the wall.

There was one. I'd known there would be. At the end of the long curve, after the clouded figure of the Runner, there was one more stretch of smooth, dark glass. It was empty. It had nothing in it at all.

Not yet.

Zayn looked at it for a long time. Then he reached out with one finger and, in the thin film of glass-dust that lay over everything, he wrote three letters on the floor.

*A. S. H.*

He looked at them. Then he wiped them away with the flat of his hand, lay down beside Soren, and closed his eyes.

I sat in the doorway and kept watch, with my tail glowing faintly in the dark. Above the broken roof, the Ash hung half full now. It was halfway to the dune.

I counted the night. Night fourteen. I wished I'd never learned to count.

---

*End of Chapter Ten*

# Chapter Eleven
## Riders of the Storm

> *Ride with the storm, never against it.*
> — the first thing every lizard-rider's father tells him, and usually the last thing he listens to

---

Night eighteen. The Ash was fat and white and getting fatter, and I'd stopped looking at it.

We'd left the City of Glass three days before, walking east toward the free towns. Soren had decided that if the walls didn't know a third way, then somebody alive might. Some old scholar, some water-finder, some half-mad hermit in a cave who'd heard a different version of the story. "Everyone in this desert has a different version of every story," he said. "One of them has to be the right one."

Zayn didn't argue. He walked. He found water. He sat by the fire in the evenings and copied the letters Soren drew, and when Soren made jokes, he laughed at them, a little too quickly, like someone paying a debt.

I didn't say much. I'd told one lie in that glass hall and it had used up all my talking. Zayn hadn't mentioned it, not once. He still let me sleep against his side at night. Somehow that was worse.

On the morning of the eighteenth day, Zayn stopped on top of a dune and sniffed the air.

"Storm," he said.

Soren sniffed too. "I don't smell anything."

"Like an old coin." Zayn was looking south, where the sky along the edge of the world had gone a strange, bruised yellow. "It's coming fast. Faster than the last one." He frowned. "And something else."

I'd already smelled it. I'd smelled it a long way back and kept hoping I was wrong.

Smoke without fire. Ash without heat. The smell of the Hollow.

From the dunes behind us came a snarl like tearing cloth.

---

They came over the ridge to the west in a long grey line.

Hounds first: eight, ten, more, running low, with darkness streaming off their backs like torn banners. And behind the Hounds, on sand lizards, came men.

I'll describe the lizards for you, since you've never seen one. They're long and low and the colour of the dunes, with a frilled crest along their backs that flares red when they're angry, which is always. They run with their bellies almost touching the sand and their tails held out stiff behind them. They're faster than any camel ever born, and they're bad-tempered, and they bite.

The men riding them wore grey robes. Their faces were smooth white masks with nothing on them at all: no eyes, no mouth, no nose, just a blank white oval like an egg. Each one carried a blade at his hip that didn't shine in the Fire. It was grey, the dull flat grey of a sky before rain.

The Unnamed.

Twelve of them. Zayn counted, and I watched him go pale as he did it.

"Friends of yours?" said Soren.

"They hunt me."

"Of course they do." Soren drew his broken sword. "Fine. Twelve of them, ten Hounds, two of us, and one small rude fox. I like those odds."

From somewhere in Zayn's pack, muffled, came a small *ting*.

"I don't like those odds," Soren admitted. "Run."

---

They didn't get far. Nobody outruns a sand lizard on foot. By the time they'd stumbled down into the next hollow between the dunes, the first rider was coming over the crest behind them, his lizard sliding down the slope in a hiss of sand with its crest blazing red.

Soren didn't run any farther. He turned, planted his feet, and waited.

The rider came at him with the grey blade swinging low. Soren stepped *toward* it, which is not what anyone sensible does. He ducked under the swing, caught the rider's sleeve with his free hand and simply *pulled*. The masked man went off the side of the lizard and into the sand, and Soren went up onto its back in the same movement, as smoothly as if he'd practised it a thousand times. He probably had.

"Zayn!" He wrenched the lizard round. "Come on!"

A second lizard had come down the slope behind the first. Its rider was turning to slash at Soren's back. Zayn didn't stop to think. He ran at it, ducked under its snapping jaws and grabbed the rider's ankle with both hands, and hauled. The masked man fell. Zayn scrambled up onto the warm, scaly back in his place.

The lizard went mad underneath him.

It bucked and spun and hissed, its crest flaring scarlet, and Zayn grabbed two fistfuls of the frill and held on with his knees. I leapt. I'd like to say it was graceful. I landed half on his pack and half on his head, and clawed my way down into his lap, and he didn't even yell at me.

Soren was already racing away. Zayn's lizard saw him go and decided, as lizards do, that wherever its friend was going looked more interesting than here.

It ran.

---

I've been many places in my long life. I've ridden on camels and in carts and once, unwillingly, in the beak of a very large bird. Nothing has ever been as fast as a sand lizard running for its life.

The dunes went past in a blur. The wind tore at Zayn's hair and filled his mouth with grit. Behind them came the Unnamed, a grey wave pouring over every ridge, with the Hounds running ahead of the lizards like smoke blown before a fire. In front of them, the yellow sky had turned brown, and then dark, and the storm was coming up to meet them like a wall.

"*Left!*" Soren shouted over his shoulder. "We go left, around it—"

"*No!*" Zayn's voice cracked. "*With it! Ride with it!*"

"*What?*"

"*The wind's coming from the south! If you turn across it, it'll knock you flat! Go where it's going!*"

I'll give Soren this. He'd spent most of a month learning from Zayn how not to die, and he didn't waste a breath arguing. He hauled his lizard's head round, away from the edge of the storm and straight in, where the wind was blowing north. Zayn followed.

And the storm swallowed them.

---

The world went brown. Then it went dark.

The sand was everywhere. It was in the air and in their eyes and their teeth, roaring like the sea, so thick Zayn couldn't see his own hands on the lizard's frill. He pulled his cloak over his face and rode blind. I pressed myself flat against his stomach. The wind came behind them like a great hand at their backs, pushing, and the lizards ran with it, faster than they'd ever run.

Behind them, the Unnamed tried to cut across.

I heard it happen. I couldn't see it, but I heard it. A lizard screamed as a rider tried to turn it sideways into the wind. The storm caught it like a sail and threw it, lizard and rider and all, tumbling away into the brown. Then another. Then a Hound, howling for the first time I'd ever heard one howl, as the wind tore the shadow off it in strips.

*Ride with the storm, never against it.*

Zayn had learned that from no one. He'd learned it from a lifetime of sandstorms at the edge of Harrow's End, crouched alone inside a patched tent, listening to which way the seams were straining. It had never seemed like a useful thing to know.

It saved their lives.

Not all of them followed blind. One rider was still with us, close behind, his lizard running in the shelter of our own. Zayn didn't see him until the grey blade came swinging out of the brown, low and fast toward his leg.

He twisted. The blade missed his leg and caught his forearm instead, a shallow cut along the old golden cracks.

It didn't bleed much. But for one terrible heartbeat, Zayn couldn't remember what honey tasted like.

He knew he'd eaten it. He knew there had been a pot of it, and dates, and someone laughing. But the sweetness itself, the *taste* of it, was gone, sliced out of him like a page cut from a book. There was only a cold grey blank where it had been.

Then it came back, slowly, like water seeping into a hole. He gasped.

That's what the grey blades do. They don't cut flesh so much as they cut *you*.

Soren's lizard came round out of the storm, and the broken sword came down, and the rider behind us was gone.

---

They came out of the worst of it into a place of stone.

Ruins. Old ones, older than Harrow's End or the Old Ruins where Zayn used to dig. A long road ran between two rows of great carved pillars, half buried in the dunes, half fallen, like the ribs of some enormous dead animal. The storm still howled overhead, but down here between the pillars the wind broke and swirled, and for a moment they could see.

The lizards didn't like it.

Soren's stopped so suddenly that he went over its head into the sand. Zayn's reared up, hissing, and threw him off its back, and I went flying. By the time Zayn had rolled to his feet, both lizards were gone, vanishing back into the brown with their crests blazing.

"Well," said Soren, spitting out sand. "That's gratitude."

Then the ground shook.

One of the great pillars beside him had been leaning for a thousand years. The storm had been pushing at it for an hour. Now, slowly, with a long groan like the wall in the Old Ruins on the morning this story began, it gave up.

"*Soren!*"

He threw himself sideways. He almost made it.

The pillar came down across his legs with a sound like the end of the world. Soren didn't scream. He made a sound, a short, punched-out noise like all the air had been knocked out of him, and then he lay very still, pinned face-down in the sand from the knees down under a tower of carved stone.

"I'm all right," he said into the sand. His voice was thin. "I'm all right. Nothing's broken. I think nothing's broken. It's just *very heavy*."

Zayn was already there. He got his shoulder under the edge of the pillar and heaved, and it didn't move. He set his feet and heaved again, and his heels dug into the sand, and the stone didn't so much as tremble. It must have weighed as much as a house.

From the storm behind them, out of the dark between the pillars, came a snarl like tearing cloth.

---

Six Hounds. Four masked riders on foot, their lizards lost in the storm, their grey blades drawn. They came out of the swirling brown slowly, because they could see there was no hurry. A boy who couldn't move a stone. A prince who couldn't move at all. A fox.

Zayn turned to face them.

He reached for the Light. It came. But it came grey and thin and sputtering, like it had with the Hounds at the Wells, because he wasn't thinking about anyone else now. He was terrified, so terrified it filled him up. Soren was pinned behind him, and the grey blades were coming, and the Light knew it.

The Hounds grinned their shadow grins.

"Zayn," said Soren from the sand. "Zayn, go. *Go.* Run with the storm. You know how. Leave me—"

"*No.*"

The first Hound sprang.

And I stopped being a small fox.

---

I'll tell you how it feels, because Zayn never asked me and nobody else ever has.

It feels like drowning in reverse. Like something cold and enormous that has been folded up very small inside you for a very long time, suddenly *unfolding*. My silver eye goes first. It burns cold, and the cold spreads out from it, through my skull and down my spine, and the dark comes pouring up out of the place where half of me used to be. It isn't a part of me so much as a part of *it*. A piece of the Hollow I swallowed long ago, trying to drag someone out.

I grew.

I grew until my head was level with the tops of the pillars. My fur went black. Not black like a dune wolf's coat; black like the space between the Embers. It streamed off me the way darkness streams off the Hounds. My teeth were long. My eyes burned like coals.

The Hound that had sprung at Zayn hit my chest and simply came apart.

I put my front paws under the fallen pillar, the one Zayn couldn't move, the one that weighed as much as a house. I lifted it the way you'd lift a stick. Soren dragged himself out from under it on his elbows. And then, because I was angry, and because the Hollow part of me is always angry, I threw it.

It went end over end through the swirling sand and came down on the four masked men with a sound I won't describe to you.

The other Hounds turned to run. I caught two of them. The rest melted back into the storm.

Then it was quiet. Only the wind howled overhead.

And I turned round, a huge shadow-beast with ember eyes and darkness pouring off my back, and looked down at the boy I'd spent fifteen years protecting.

---

I've hidden that form from every bearer I ever walked with. All of them. All eleven. Not one of them ever saw it.

I'd always told myself it was to keep them from being afraid of me.

It was really to keep me from seeing their faces when they were.

Zayn's face, looking up at me in that storm, was the worst thing I've ever seen. And I have seen a great many things.

He didn't know it was me. Of course he didn't. All he saw was a monster made of the Hollow standing over Soren with ember eyes, the biggest creature of the grey he'd ever seen except for the Veilbeast. All he felt was fear, pure and total, filling him up like water filling a well.

"*Get away from him!*"

The Light came out of him like the end of the world.

It wasn't gold. It wasn't grey either. It was both, twisted together and pouring out of his hands and his chest and his eyes in a raging white torrent, too much and too wild, gold with grey running through it like smoke through fire. He didn't aim it. He couldn't have. It just *burst* out of him, and it hit me square in the chest.

Light and Hollow don't mix. Nothing in the world burns the dark the way the Light does.

I howled.

I'd never howled before. Not in all my long years. I heard it come out of me, a terrible, broken sound that rang off the pillars and up into the storm, and I felt the Light tearing into the Hollow half of me like fire into dry grass. It burned my silver eye. It burned the black streaming off my back. It burned through the cold place inside me where the darkness lived, and it *hurt*. Weaver help me, it hurt more than anything has ever hurt.

I fell.

And as I fell, I got smaller. The dark went out of me in a rush, the way water goes out of a broken jar. The black fur went gold. The long teeth shrank. By the time I hit the sand I was only a fox again: a small, sand-coloured fox lying on my side between the pillars, my fur scorched and smoking along one side, my silver eye squeezed shut.

The light went out of Zayn all at once. He stood there swaying. His arms were cracked gold from wrist to shoulder. He was staring at me.

"Kiri?" he whispered.

---

The vision came as it always did, as soon as the Light went out. It was longer this time. It was clearer.

The full Ash, so near he could see the grey shadows on its face. The Crown Dune beneath his feet. The fire climbing up his arms. Somebody below him on the dune, shouting. And for the very first time, he could feel the sand under his bare feet, and how cold it was.

Then it let him go, and he was on his knees in the ruins with his scorched fox in front of him.

"Kiri," he said. "*Kiri.* I didn't— I didn't know— Kiri, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry—"

I didn't look at him. I couldn't.

"Don't look at me," I said.

That was the last thing I said to him for a long time.

---

The storm blew itself out by nightfall.

They made camp right there among the pillars, because Soren couldn't walk far. His legs were bruised black from the knees down but, by some miracle, nothing was broken. Zayn built a fire out of an old dead thornbush. Then he sat down in the sand and gathered me into his lap, very carefully, scorched side up, and curled himself around me. And he stayed like that.

He didn't sleep for a long time. He kept saying he was sorry. Over and over, very quietly, into my fur. I didn't answer him. Eventually, near the middle of the night, he cried himself to sleep with his arms around me.

I lay in his arms with my good eye half open, and I watched Soren.

Soren didn't sleep either.

He waited until Zayn's breathing had gone slow. Then he got up, wincing, and limped away from the fire toward the far end of the ruins, where the pillar had come down. He went to look at the masked men. I think he wanted to see whether any of them were still alive. Soren was like that.

One of them was. Barely.

He was lying a little apart from the others, half under the edge of the stone. His mask had cracked down the middle. Under it was a face much younger than I'd expected, thin and grey and frightened, not much older than Soren. Soren knelt down beside him. I couldn't hear what they said. The man's lips moved once or twice. Then they stopped moving.

Soren stayed kneeling there a long while. Then he reached into the dead man's grey robe and drew something out.

A letter. Folded small and sealed with grey wax.

He held it to the light of the Ash, which was bright enough now to read by. I watched him look at the seal, and I watched him go still, the stillness of a man who has just recognised a face in a crowd. I learned later that he'd seen that seal before. He'd seen it pressed into the wax at the bottom of the order that sent him out of Veimore with a broken sword. It was the personal seal of the Vizier Orrin.

He broke it. He read the letter by the light of the Ash.

I'll tell you what it said, because you'll need to know, and because Zayn couldn't have read it then even if he'd seen it. It was signed *The Voice*. It gave orders, in a soft and careful hand. On the Night of Embers, when every lantern in Veimore was lit for the lost bearers, King Absalon was to die.

Soren read it twice. His hand was shaking.

Then he folded it up very small. He twisted the bronze pommel off the hilt of his broken sword, and slid the letter into the hollow space where the prayer was supposed to go. He screwed the pommel back on, tight.

He came back to the fire. He sat down beside the two of us with the sword across his knees. He looked at Zayn asleep, with golden cracks still glowing faintly up both his arms, and a scorched fox in his lap. For a long time he didn't move.

He didn't wake Zayn. He didn't tell him.

I think he meant to. I think he was waiting until he knew what to do with it. I think he wanted Zayn, for one more night, to be carrying only one terrible thing instead of two.

I watched all of it through one eye. I didn't say anything either.

We were a fine pair, the prince and I. Two keepers of secrets, sitting up in the dark, watching over the same sleeping boy. Above us the Ash hung nearly full.

---

*End of Chapter Eleven*

# Chapter Twelve
## The Night of the Full Ash

> *Under the full Ash, every promise is weighed.*
> — from the oath-sayings of the free towns, spoken over water at weddings and at graves

---

I didn't speak again before the full Ash.

I'll tell you about them anyway. That's the strange thing about being the one who tells a story: you can be silent all the way through it and still have to describe every minute.

I walked. That's mostly what I did. I limped along behind the two of them with my fur scorched brown down one side and my silver eye swollen shut. I didn't catch scorpions. I didn't eat much at all. At night I slept a little way off from the fire instead of against Zayn's side, and every night he looked over at me before he lay down, and every night I turned my face away.

He tried to say sorry. Again and again. Sometimes out loud, sometimes just by bringing me water in his cupped hands and setting it down beside me without a word. Once he left me the best piece of a roasted sand-hen, and sat with his back to me so I could eat it without being watched. I ate it after he'd fallen asleep.

I wasn't angry with him. You need to understand that, because he didn't. I wasn't angry with him at all.

I was ashamed. He had seen what I was. And there is no word for how that feels, except that it's the opposite of being remembered.

---

Soren was different too, after the storm.

He still joked. He still complained about his feet, and his bruised legs, and the general unfairness of sand. He still drew letters every evening, and Zayn learned fast now. He could read whole short lines. One night Soren wrote ZAYN AND SOREN, MONSTERS in big letters in the sand and made him read it out, and Zayn did, and they both laughed.

But Soren had started touching the hilt of his sword. Not the grip, the way a fighter does. The pommel. He'd rest his thumb on the bronze cap without seeming to know he was doing it, the way Zayn touched his feather mark. And when he thought no one was looking, he'd stand on top of a dune and stare west, toward Veimore, for a long time.

"We're changing direction," he announced on the fourth morning. "West. I need to go home."

Zayn stared at him. "You were exiled."

"I know. I was there."

"They'll kill you."

"They'll *try*." Soren's grin came and went too fast. "I need to see Idan. I need to make sure he's all right."

My tail glowed. That was true. Every word.

But Zayn, who had been watching my tail since Harrow's End, had also learned to watch the spaces between words. He looked at Soren a long moment. He knew there was something more. And because Zayn never in his life asked anyone for something they didn't want to give, he didn't ask.

I knew what it was. I'd watched it go into the hilt. I didn't say anything either.

Three of us walking west together, and every one of us carrying something we didn't say.

---

Every night, the Ash got fuller.

Zayn stopped looking at it. Then he started looking at it all the time. He'd lie on his back by the fire with his hand on his chest, staring up at that great white face as it swelled a little more each evening. I knew what he was seeing. It was the dune, and the fire, and the sound of somebody shouting a name at the bottom of the world.

He wasn't afraid it would happen *tonight*. He wasn't stupid; he knew the Crown Dune was nowhere near. But every full Ash would bring it one turn closer, and this was the first one since he'd learned what the word *ash* meant at the end of eight lines in the glass.

On the twenty-ninth night, it rose full.

It came up out of the eastern dunes huge and white and close, so close it seemed to fill half the sky, exactly as it did in his vision. The whole desert went silver under it. Every grain of sand had a shadow. It was the brightest night Zayn had ever seen, and it was beautiful, and he sat by the fire and couldn't stop shaking.

One turn of the Ash. Thin to full, and the dark between. That's how long they'd had.

I knew it. I'd counted. Neither of them knew.

---

It started, as the worst things do, with someone trying to help.

"Come to Veimore with me," Soren said.

He'd been quiet for a long time, sitting across the fire turning his ring. Now he leaned forward, and his face in the firelight was serious in a way Zayn had hardly ever seen it.

"I mean it. Listen. Under the city there are the catacombs, and in the catacombs there's the Archive. It's every book anyone in Veimore ever wrote. Every story. Every name. Thousands of years of them, Zayn, rooms and rooms, all the way down. If there's a third way written anywhere in this desert, it's there. You can read now. Not fast. But we could look together."

"Soren—"

"I can get you in. I know a way through the kitchens. The cook would hide an army if I asked her nicely and brought back her honey jars."

"I can't go to Veimore."

"Why not?"

"Because it's not *there*." Zayn's voice cracked. "It's not in a book. It's not in a library. You read them. In the glass. You read them out loud to me. Eight of them burned, and three of them ran, and the three who ran—" He pointed east, at nothing, at the empty silver desert. "Look what happened. One became the *Hollow*. That's the third way, Soren. That's the only other way there is."

"You don't *know* that."

"I've seen it! I see it every time! Every time I use the Light, I'm standing on that dune, and I'm—" He stopped. He pressed his hand flat over his chest. "It's not a story anymore. It's going to happen. It's *written*."

"Then we unwrite it."

"You can't unwrite the *sand*!"

The wells, the glass, the storm: none of it had been as loud as that. The words rang out across the silver dunes and the desert swallowed them, the way it swallows everything.

They sat there breathing hard on either side of the fire.

Soren looked away first. He looked down at his hands. When he spoke again his voice had gone quiet.

"Promise me something," he said.

Zayn didn't answer.

"Promise me you'll never give up." Soren looked up. His eyes were very bright. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see in your visions. Whatever the walls say. You keep going. You don't just *lie down* and let it take you. Promise me."

Zayn looked at him across the fire, under the full Ash.

He could not lie. You know that. He'd never been able to. So when he said it, he said it knowing that the feather on his chest would have burned like a coal if he hadn't meant it with everything he had.

"I promise," said Zayn.

It didn't burn.

My tail lit up the sand like a second fire.

---

If they'd stopped there, this would be a different story.

They didn't stop there.

"Good," said Soren. He let out a long breath, almost a laugh. "Good. Then come to Veimore."

"That's not—"

"You just promised."

"I promised not to give up. Going to the dune isn't giving up." Zayn's voice was shaking now. "Don't you see? It's the *opposite*. The ones who gave up are the ones who ran. If I keep going, Soren, *that's* where I'm going. That's what not giving up *is*, for me."

Soren stood up so fast he nearly kicked the fire.

"No," he said. "No. I'm not doing this again. I'm not standing by while someone I—" He bit it off. "I'm not letting them take you away. I won't. I'll drag you to Veimore by your ankles if I have to. I'll lock you in the catacombs. I'll find the third way myself and read it to you through the door. I am *not* losing anybody else."

"You can't save me."

"Watch me."

And then Zayn said it.

He didn't plan it. I know that, because I know him. He was frightened, so frightened, and he was tired, and the Ash was full and white above him like an eye that wouldn't close. Soren was standing over him promising things nobody could promise. And it hurt so much to be loved like that, when he knew exactly how it was going to end, that something in him reached for the sharpest true thing it could find and threw it.

"You can't save me," he said. "You couldn't even save Nim."

---

The fire cracked.

That was the only sound in the whole desert.

Soren didn't move. He didn't shout. His face didn't even change, at first. He just stood there with the full Ash behind him, looking down at Zayn, and something went out of his eyes. It was like a lantern going out on a high red wall, one of the ones that must never all go out.

My tail glowed. I wish it hadn't. But it was true, every word, and there's nothing in the world that cuts quite like the truth said at the wrong moment for the wrong reason.

Zayn saw his face. He saw what he'd done.

He got up. He didn't say sorry. He couldn't make his mouth do it. He turned round, and he walked away from the fire, out into the silver dark.

"Zayn," said Soren behind him.

Just his name. Once. Not loud.

Zayn didn't turn round.

I've thought about that moment more than any other in this story. More than the dune, even, and more than the fire. A boy walking away across moonlit sand, and another boy standing alone by a small fire, saying his name once to the wind.

He meant to come back. You should know that. He meant to walk until he'd stopped shaking, and then come back and say sorry, and make it right. He'd come back in the morning, he thought. There'd be time in the morning.

He walked away and left Soren alone.

It was the worst mistake he ever made. He'd tell you so himself.

---

And where was I?

I'll tell you that much. I'll tell you the first part.

I'd smelled Hounds that evening, before the argument even started. They were a long way off, but they were coming, drawn by the Light still glowing in the cracks on Zayn's arms. I'd been lying a little way from the fire with my scorched side to the cold sand, listening to the two of them talk. When Zayn walked off into the dark, I got up and went the other way.

I went hunting.

I let the Hollow half out. It hurt. Everything about it hurt that night. The burned part of me screamed every time it moved. But there were Hounds out in the dunes and the boy I loved was alone out there in the dark, and that was the only use I've ever found for the thing that I am.

I hunted them across the silver sand under the full Ash, for most of the night.

That's all I'm going to tell you about where I was. Don't ask me any more. Not yet.

---

Zayn didn't go far. He climbed a high dune a long way off and sat at the top, with his knees pulled up to his chest and the Ash pouring white light down on him. He could still see the glow of the fire, very small, down in the hollow where he'd left it.

He said sorry to it. Out loud, over and over, practising.

*I'm sorry. I didn't mean it. You were right to try. You were right to try to save him. You were right to try to save me.*

He was going to say all that in the morning. He went through it again and again, getting the words right, the way he'd copied his name in the sand until the letters were perfect.

At some point, near dawn, with the Ash sinking down into the western dunes and the sky going grey, he fell asleep sitting up.

He didn't dream. He'd tell you that if you asked him. He had no visions that night and no warnings, and he didn't feel anything at all. Not a whisper in the sand. Not a single bell.

The Weaver was very quiet that night. I've never forgiven it for that either.

---

He woke when the Fire touched his face. He scrambled up and ran down the dune, sliding and stumbling. He had the words ready in his mouth. *I'm sorry. You were right to try.*

He came over the last rise and stopped.

There were camels in the hollow. Five of them, kneeling in the sand, with packs of salt-blocks strapped to their sides. And there were men, five desert travellers in dusty white robes, standing in a ring around the cold remains of the fire.

They were looking down at something on the sand.

Zayn walked toward them. He didn't feel his legs moving. He didn't feel anything at all.

Soren lay on his back beside the dead fire, with one arm flung out and his broken sword in the sand by his hand.

He was grey.

Not Husk-grey. Not emptied. Something worse. His skin had gone the colour of old ash, every bit of it, and running all through the grey, across his face and his throat and down both his arms, were fine bright lines of *gold*. Thin glowing cracks, like the glaze on a broken pot.

Like the cracks the Light left on Zayn's own skin.

His eyes were open, looking up at the morning sky. They didn't see it.

---

One of the travellers saw Zayn first.

He was an older man with a beard gone yellow at the edges, and he backed away a step, then another. Then the others turned and saw him too, and they all went still.

They looked at Zayn. They looked at his arms, at the golden cracks still glowing faintly from wrist to shoulder, left by the storm.

They looked at Soren, cracked with the same gold.

"That's him," said one of them, a young man with a falcon hooded on his wrist. His voice was high and thin. "That's the prince. The exiled one. The *king's son*. Look at the jaw, the scar—they said in the free towns, they said he was out here selling ash—"

"And *that*," said the old man, staring at Zayn, "is the glowing boy."

Zayn didn't move.

"From the caravan," the old man said. "They talked about him at the last well. The one the beast didn't eat. The one who glows. A man came out of the sand alive, and he was telling everyone." He was backing toward his camel. "*Monster*, he said. And here he is."

"I didn't," said Zayn.

His voice came out of him like something broken. He looked at Soren, and back at them.

"I didn't do this."

The feather mark on his chest didn't burn. He felt it not burn. It was true. Every word. And it didn't matter at all, because these men had never heard of a boy who couldn't lie, and if they had, they wouldn't have believed it.

There were other true things, too. They came up in him one after another, like water rising in a well.

*I left him.*

*I said the worst thing I could think of, and then I left him alone in the dark.*

"I left him," said Zayn. "I walked away. I left him alone."

The feather didn't burn for that either.

The travellers stared at him. And then one of them, I never did learn which, said it: the word that would follow Zayn across the whole desert, from oasis to oasis, carried on falcons' legs and in traders' mouths, faster than any lizard could run.

"*Prince-Killer.*"

They ran.

They were on their camels and away in moments, with salt-blocks swinging and the young man's falcon screaming on his wrist. They went east, toward the free towns, toward the wells. Toward everyone. I could hear them shouting it to each other as they went, over and over, until the dunes swallowed the sound.

*Prince-Killer. Prince-Killer. The glowing boy killed the prince.*

By the next turn of the Ash, there wouldn't be a town in the desert that hadn't heard it.

---

Zayn knelt down in the sand beside Soren.

He didn't touch him at first. He just knelt there, with his hands open on his knees and the golden cracks glowing in his skin.

Then, very slowly, he reached out and closed Soren's eyes.

He wanted to heal him. Of course he did. His hands were already burning. He put them on Soren's chest and called the Light, and it came, gold and desperate. And it found nothing to hold on to. Nothing at all. There was nobody in there to bring back.

He'd never seen it fail before.

He took Soren's hand. It was cold. His fingers were grey and cracked with gold, and on the right hand, on the finger where the heavy ring had always been, there was nothing. Only a band of paler skin where the ring had always sat. The ring he never took off.

Zayn looked at that bare finger for a long time.

He looked down at his own arms, at the golden cracks. Then he looked at the cracks on Soren's face. The same gold. The same light. And a question came into his eyes that I could see as clearly as if it had been written in the sand. He'd been asleep. He'd been far away. He'd felt nothing. But the Light had burst out of him once already when he didn't mean it to, and nearly killed me.

*Did I do this?*

He would carry that question for a long time. I'm sorry to tell you that.

---

The broken sword was lying in the sand by Soren's open hand.

Zayn picked it up.

It was lighter than he'd expected. Half a blade, a hand's width and a little more of jagged steel, with dark leather on the grip and a bronze pommel that didn't quite match. It was still warm from the morning Fire. He held it across his knees the way Soren used to hold it by the fire at night.

He didn't know what was inside it. Not yet.

He sat there in the hollow with the sword in his lap and his friend beside him, as the Fire climbed up the sky and the shadows of the dunes went short and the wind came and began, gently, to move the sand.

He didn't cry. Not then. That came later.

He just sat, and held on.

---

I was already on the dune above the hollow when he came over the rise that morning. I'd been there before the travellers came. Don't ask me how long.

I sat at the top of the slope, a long way off, with my scorched fur and my one good eye, and I didn't go any closer. My tail lay in the sand behind me. It wasn't glowing. It wasn't dark. It just *lay* there, the way a tail does when no one's saying anything at all.

Zayn didn't look up. I'm not sure he knew I was there.

I could have gone down to him. I could have sat in his lap and let him hold on to me instead of a broken sword. I could have said a great many things.

I didn't say anything.

I'm very good at that. I've told you before. It's becoming a theme.

It's the one I'm least proud of.

---

*End of Chapter Twelve*

# PART FOUR: THE UNNAMED

# Chapter Thirteen
## Nameless Again

> *The loneliest grave is the one no one visits.*
> — a saying of the desert burial-keepers

---

For four days, he didn't say a word.

You'd think that would be a relief to me. Now I had all the silence I could ever want, and I would have traded every scorpion between here and the edge of the world to hear him tell me to shut up.

He walked. That was all he did. He walked while the sand was still cold, and at noon when the Fire hammered down so hard the air shook, and at night under an Ash that was already starting to thin. He didn't choose a direction. He let the dunes choose for him. When a slope rose in front of him he climbed it. When a valley opened he went down into it. If the Weaver was writing the sands that week, it was writing them for a boy who had stopped reading.

Soren's sword was tied across his back, wrapped in his scarf. Half a blade and a plain leather hilt. It knocked against his spine with every step, and every time it did, he flinched, and every time he flinched, he kept walking.

He hadn't given Soren to the dunes. He hadn't been able to make his hands do it. He'd sat beside him until the Fire was high and the wind had begun the work for him, and then he'd walked away and left the sand to finish it, with the word the travellers had shouted chasing him over the dunes the way stones had once chased him out of Harrow's End.

*Prince-Killer.*

He had been called a lot of things in his life. Nameless. Sandborn. Monster. Now he had a new one, and the worst part, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, was that he couldn't be sure it was wrong.

I followed him a dune behind. I'd have walked closer, but I wasn't walking well.

---

You remember the storm. The pillar, and what I turned into to lift it, and how his light caught me full in my Hollow half. It burned the way truth burns a lie, all the way down, looking for the bottom.

There was a patch on my left side, the silver side, where the fur had come away. What was underneath wasn't fox. It was dark and faintly smoking, like a coal dropped in water that hadn't decided yet whether to go out. It hadn't healed.

That night, the night of the full Ash, had been one of the worse ones. I'll say that much and no more.

So I limped. And he walked. And neither of us talked about it.

---

He still did the things that kept him alive. Fifteen years of scraping by don't leave you just because your heart has.

On the second day he found water. He licked his finger and held it to the wind, then walked straight down to a hollow where camel-thorn grew, and dug until the sand went dark and damp, and drank.

He had taught Soren how to do that. I watched him remember it. I watched him sit back on his heels with wet sand on his fingers and his face go completely still, the way a pool goes still after something has sunk to the bottom of it.

Then he got up and kept walking.

At night he didn't sleep much. He sat at the top of whatever dune he'd stopped on, and with one finger he wrote in the sand.

The same word, every night. Five letters. He wrote them slowly and carefully, the way you write a thing you've only just learned, with your tongue between your teeth. Soren had made him practise that word fifty times by the fire, laughing at the way he wrote the S backwards. *It's my name, Zayn. Have some respect.* He didn't write it backwards anymore.

S. O. R. E. N.

And every morning, when he woke, the dunes had moved in the night, and the word was gone.

That's the trouble with being given to the desert. There's no stone. There's no place. The sand takes you and keeps moving, and the people who loved you have to carry the grave around inside them, because there's nowhere else to put it.

---

On the third night he tried to lie.

I watched him do it. He was sitting with his knees pulled up and the sword across his lap, and he said, so quietly I nearly didn't hear it, "It wasn't my fault."

His face twisted. His hand flew to his chest. The feather mark flared under his shirt, hot and sharp, and he doubled over and pressed his palm against it until the light went out.

He couldn't even lie to himself. Not even for comfort. Not even once.

Most people get to tell themselves small kind lies in the dark. *There was nothing I could have done.* Lies like that are how people get up in the morning. Zayn never got any.

And what was true was this: they had argued under the full Ash, and he had walked away, and he had left Soren alone in the dark.

"The sand remembers," he whispered afterwards. The old words. The ones he'd said every bad night in Harrow's End.

The mark didn't burn. It wasn't a lie.

It just wasn't something he believed anymore, either. There's a difference, and I watched him fall into it.

I limped up the slope and sat down a little way off. He didn't look at me.

"It wasn't your fault," I said.

My tail lit up, bright and steady as a lamp. I wanted him to see it more than I've wanted most things.

He didn't turn his head.

"Don't," he said.

It was the first word he'd spoken in three days, hoarse, as if his voice had rusted.

"It's true," I said. "Look at my tail. Look at it."

"It glowed at the City of Glass too," he said. "Right up until it didn't."

I had nothing to say to that. I had told him one lie in fifteen years, one small, merciful lie, and it had cost me every true thing I would ever say to him afterwards. Remember that, if you ever think a lie is cheap.

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, without looking at me, "Where were you?"

The wind moved over the dune.

"That night," he said. "After I walked away. You weren't with him. You weren't with me. Where were you?"

I opened my mouth.

I'm not going to tell you what I nearly said. I'm not going to tell you where I was, either. Not yet. There's a night in this story I haven't told you about, and I'm not ready, and it's my story, so I get to choose when.

I closed my mouth again.

He nodded slowly, as though I'd answered. Then he lay down with his back to me and the broken sword in his arms, and I sat beside him until the Embers went pale, and neither of us said anything at all.

---

There was a twist of waxed paper at the bottom of his bag. You'll know what was in it. Three dates rolled in honey, the last of the four, gone sticky and dark, carried all the way from Harrow's End.

He had carried them through the beast and the wells and the glass city and the storm, saving them for the hardest day.

On the fourth evening he took them out and held them in his palm for a long time. I think he understood that this was it. If he didn't eat them now, he never would.

Then he wrapped them up again and put them back in the bag. He couldn't do it. Eating them would have meant saying that this was as bad as it got, and some small, stubborn part of him still wasn't ready to promise that.

I'm not sure, even now, whether that was hope or the opposite of it.

---

The fourth night was the one I'd been afraid of.

He stopped at the bottom of a long slope instead of the top. In fifteen years he had always slept high, where he could see what was coming. Now he walked down into the cold trough between two dunes, where the wind poured sand down the slip-face in hissing curtains.

And then he lay down.

He lay on his back with the sword on his chest and his arms folded over it, and he looked up at the Embers, and he let the sand come.

It came softly. A trickle over his feet. A drift against his side. The dunes were moving, the way they moved every night, and he had lain down in the path of one.

"Zayn," I said.

He didn't answer.

"Zayn. Get up."

The sand was over his ankles now. His knees. It was spilling into the folds of his shirt.

The dead are given to the dunes. That's what desert people do. He hadn't stayed to see the sand close over Soren. So he was giving them the next best thing.

"*Get up.*" I was on my feet, if you can call it that. I got my teeth into his sleeve and pulled. I pulled with everything I had, which that night was less than a kitten has. My burned side split open and smoked and I yelped, and his arm didn't even move.

I could have changed. You're thinking it. I was thinking it. I could have let the big dark half out and dug him free in three strokes.

He'd seen that half once, and been so afraid of it that his light had nearly burned it out of the world. I didn't know what it would do to him to wake up in the mouth of that thing.

So I stood there in the sliding sand, shaking, a small burned fox shouting at a boy who wouldn't listen.

And the light came.

---

It wasn't his light. That's what I knew first.

His light starts at the mark and pours outward. This came from the slope beside him, from nowhere at all. It was soft and gold and it didn't burn. It just *glowed*, the way a lamp glows in a window when someone is waiting up for you.

I didn't see anyone in it. I want to be honest about that. I did not see him. I only saw the light on Zayn's face, and Zayn's eyes open wide, and Zayn's lips move.

He told me later what he saw. I'll tell it the way he did.

Soren was sitting on the slope beside him. Cross-legged, elbows on his knees, the way he used to sit by the fire when he was about to say something annoying. The scar along his jaw. His dark hair coming loose. He looked exactly as he had in life, except that light came through the edges of him, like a cloth hung in front of the Fire.

His hand was bare. No ring. Zayn noticed that. He couldn't stop noticing it.

"You look terrible," said Soren. "That's weird."

Zayn made a sound. It wasn't a word.

"You're lying in a hole," said Soren. "Letting a dune sit on you. Do you know how stupid that looks from up here? Very. It looks very stupid."

"I left you," Zayn whispered.

Soren stopped smiling.

"Yeah," he said. "You did."

There was no anger in it. That was the worst part, Zayn told me. Soren only said it the way you say *the sand is hot*. A plain fact, laid down between them.

"I left you," Zayn said again, "and you died."

"I know. I was there." Soren leaned forward. He put out his hand and laid it on Zayn's shoulder, and it was *warm*. Zayn swore to me it was warm, warm as a living hand, warm as the Fire on a cold morning. "Get up anyway."

"I can't."

"Yes, you can. You're just choosing not to. It's different." Soren's grip tightened. His eyes were very bright. "Never give up, Zayn. You promised."

Zayn shut his eyes.

He had. Under the full Ash, on the last night, Soren had held his arm and said *Promise me you'll never give up*, and he had said *I promise*. And he could not lie. Not even with his last breath. Not even lying in the sand with a dune on his chest.

He had promised. So it was true. So he had to make it true.

When he opened his eyes again, the slope was empty and dark, and there was only a fox standing knee-deep in the sand beside him, with a tail blazing so bright it hurt to look at.

I hadn't seen anything. I'll swear to that.

But my tail had been burning gold the whole time he was talking, and it doesn't do that for dreams.

---

He got up.

He dug himself out with his hands, the way he'd once dug himself out of collapsed rooms in the Old Ruins, and climbed the slope on his hands and knees, dragging the sword.

Then he looked at me. Actually looked at me, for the first time in four days.

"Did you see him?" he said.

"No," I said.

My tail stayed bright. He watched it.

"But you believe me."

"Yes," I said. It stayed bright.

He looked at it a long moment. Then he looked away, out over the moving dunes, and he didn't say anything more. But he didn't turn his back on me either. That night, for the first time since the full Ash, he slept with me curled against his side.

It's not nothing. In the desert, you learn to count the things that are not nothing.

---

This next part is where I went wrong.

In the morning he walked on, and I couldn't keep up.

The dune had cost me more than I'd thought. By the time the Fire was two hands above the horizon I'd folded up in the shade of a rock with my legs not listening to me. He was three dunes ahead. Then four. I told myself I'd catch up when the heat broke.

Which is why I was a long way off, lying on a ridge, when the lanterns came.

You don't usually see lanterns in daylight. That's what made me lift my head. A little line of them, swaying, coming over the eastern dunes in a string: camels, six or seven, with lanterns hung from their saddles still burning in the morning, the way they burn on the walls of Veimore day and night. Rich camels. Red tassels. Water skins so fat they sloshed.

They came straight to him. Not near him. *To* him, as if they'd known exactly where a tired boy would be standing.

Fox ears are very good. Fox legs, that morning, were not. I dragged myself to the crest of the ridge and lay flat and listened.

The lead camel knelt. A man climbed down from it.

He was old, his hair fully silver and combed long over a robe the deep blue of the sky just after the Fire sets. His face was soft and kind and lined, the face of someone's grandfather. His voice was low and gentle, the kind that made you lean in to hear it.

And his eyes were grey.

Faded grey, flat and pale, like cloth left out in the Fire for a hundred years. Like a Husk's eyes, before the white sets in.

The fur along my spine stood straight up, and I couldn't have told you why. That's not true. I could have. I'm not going to. Something in me went cold and silent the moment I saw that face, the way it always goes cold and silent when anyone speaks of the night of the full Ash. Let's leave it there.

Behind the old man, another figure climbed down from a camel. He was the biggest man I had ever seen. Shaved head. Arms like the trunks of date palms. He didn't look as though he had ever said a word.

"Take the boy's bag," the old man said to him softly. "Can't you see he can barely stand?"

And the giant stepped forward and lifted the bag gently from Zayn's shoulder and carried it back to the baggage camel and tied it there, among the water skins.

Remember that. I didn't, not then. I was watching faces. I should have been watching the bag.

---

"You are the boy who glows," the old man said.

Zayn didn't answer. His hand had gone to the hilt of the broken sword on his back.

"My name is Orrin," said the old man. "I am the vizier of Veimore. I serve King Absalon." He paused. "You will have heard my name before. From him."

Zayn's knuckles went white on the hilt.

He had. Soren had told him the whole story by the fire: the stable boy he'd hidden, the broken sword, and the soft old voice of the vizier calling it treason, calling it dark magic, until his father believed it.

"Yes," said Orrin. "I was the one who named him traitor." He bowed his silver head. "I was wrong. I have spent a turn of the Ash crossing this desert to tell him so. And I came too late."

He reached into his robe.

He held something out on his open palm, and I saw Zayn's whole body go still.

A ring. Heavy gold, worn smooth, with the crest of Veimore cut into its face.

Soren's ring. The one he never took off. The one that had not been on his hand when the travellers found him.

"He gave me this," Orrin said gently.

On the ridge, my tail flickered.

I *think* it dimmed. I'm nearly sure. But the Fire was bright across the sand by then, and a tail is a hard thing to read in daylight, and I was in pain, and afraid. All of that is true. None of it is an excuse.

"When?" said Zayn. His voice cracked on it.

"On the night of the full Ash," said Orrin. "I found him at last. We spoke. He forgave me, I think, or began to. And he gave me his ring, and he said, *If anything happens to me, find the boy who glows. Look after him.*" The old man's voice shook, very slightly, in exactly the right place. "I didn't understand what he meant. Not until morning."

There was no bell. Hesset's bell was tied to a camel twenty paces away, in a patched bag, among the water skins. I told you to watch the bag.

Zayn reached out. Slowly, the way you reach for something that might vanish. Orrin let him take the ring, and Zayn held it in his palm and closed his fingers over it, and his face crumpled.

"Did you kill him?" Orrin asked quietly. Not cruelly. Almost kindly. "They're saying you did."

Zayn's eyes were streaming. He could have said *no*. It would have been the truth. Zayn hadn't killed anyone.

But the boy could not lie, and in his heart, the truth was something else.

"I left him," he said.

Orrin nodded slowly. "I know," he said. "So did I, once, a long time ago. Someone I loved." His grey eyes were wet. "Everyone leaves someone, child. It's what we do afterwards that matters."

He held out his hand. After a moment, Zayn gave the ring back. Orrin tucked it into his robe again, carefully, close to his heart.

"It should go to his father," he said. "And to his brother. I will see that it does. But you need food, and shade, and sleep. There's an oasis town two days east of here. I have a house there. Come with me." He paused. "And when you're stronger, I'll tell you about your father."

Zayn's head came up.

"I knew him," said Orrin. "Long ago. He carried what you carry. I can tell you who he was. If you want to know."

---

By the time I got there, Zayn was already climbing onto a camel.

I came down the slope in a sort of controlled fall, my side smoking. Orrin's soft grey eyes found me, and he smiled.

"Ah," he said. "The fox. Of course."

There was no surprise in it at all.

I stood at the camel's feet with my legs shaking and my fur on end and looked up at the boy. He was holding the reins in one hand. His other hand was pressed flat to his chest, over the mark.

I could have said a great many things in that moment. I've had a long time to make a list of them.

I said, "I don't like him."

Four words. My tail glowed. They were perfectly true.

Zayn looked down at me. He looked tired beyond anything a boy of fifteen should be. "You don't like anyone," he said.

That was true too.

"And you told me *yes* once," he said, more quietly. "Remember?"

Then he turned the camel's head east and followed the old man's lanterns over the dune.

I stood there in the sand for a long moment. Then I followed. What else was I going to do? I'd been following him for fifteen years.

I didn't know then what it would cost, those four small, cowardly, true words, and all the words I didn't say after them. I know now. I'll tell you. Not yet.

But the Ash was thinning above us, night by night, and I had started counting again.

---

*End of Chapter Thirteen*

# Chapter Fourteen
## The Ringing of the Bell

> *The bell does not lie, but it can be too late.*
> — a saying of the healers at the desert's edge

---

The town was called Thornwell, and it was the kind of place you could love if nobody in it was trying to kill you.

It sat in a shallow bowl between three dunes, around a spring so old that the stones of its well had been worn into the shape of hands. Nine date palms stood over it. Somebody, long ago, had decided nine was lucky, so the people of Thornwell never cut one down and never planted a tenth. There was a market of striped awnings, a dyer's yard hung with red and saffron cloth, and a hundred flat-roofed mud-brick houses. A free town, like most oasis towns in the desert. No king. No walls. Just water, and people who'd decided to stay near it.

Orrin's house stood on the edge of the market. It had blue shutters and a courtyard with a fig tree and a fountain that actually worked, which in the desert is about as close to showing off as you can get.

"Animals sleep in the yard," said the steward at the gate, looking down at me.

"I'm not an animal," I said. "I'm a guest with fur."

The steward went pale, the way people always do the first time, and looked at Orrin.

Orrin smiled his soft smile. "The fox may go where he likes," he said. "Outside."

So I slept on the roof.

---

They gave Zayn the top room. It had a real bed with a real blanket, a basin of clean water, and a window that looked out over the nine palms. He stood in the doorway looking at it the way you'd look at a meal you suspected was a trap.

Then he took off the broken sword and laid it on the bed. He put his bag beside it. And for the first time since the full Ash, he slept through a whole night without the sword in his arms.

I watched him through the window from the roof's edge, lying in the cool dark with my burned side pressed against the warm bricks.

I didn't sleep.

---

You have to understand how good Orrin was at it.

I've known a great many liars, and most of them are bad at it. They talk too much. They hurry. Orrin never hurried.

On the first evening, there were dates on the table. Dates rolled in honey, on a blue clay plate.

Zayn stopped in the doorway and stared at them.

"Soren told me you liked them," said Orrin gently. "Sit. Eat."

The bell was upstairs, in the bag on the bed. I was on the roof, with my ear pressed to a crack in the shutter, and my tail went dim as a dying coal. Nobody saw.

Zayn sat. He ate one date, slowly. Then another. He didn't touch the little twist of waxed paper in his pocket, the one he'd been carrying all the way from Harrow's End. He ate Orrin's dates instead, and his eyes filled with tears, and Orrin pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in days.

That's how it's done, you see. Not with chains. With dates, and a real bed, and an old man who knows exactly when to look away.

---

Orrin wrote letters in the evenings.

He sat at a low table by the lamp with paper and ink and a little stone seal he wore on a chain around his neck. When each letter was done he folded it, dripped grey wax onto the fold, and pressed the seal into it. A falcon waited on a perch in the corner, and every night it flew out of the window with a letter tied to its leg.

On the second evening, Zayn sat across from him and watched the pen move.

"*Water,*" he said quietly. "That word. Upside down. It says *water*."

The pen stopped.

Orrin looked up. For a moment, only a moment, something moved behind those faded grey eyes, quick and cold as a fish in a dark pool.

"You can read," he said.

"A little," said Zayn. "Soren taught me."

"Did he." Orrin set the pen down. "He was a patient teacher?"

Zayn almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen anything like it since the full Ash. "No. He was terrible. He laughed every time I wrote an R. He said they looked like camels falling over."

"Then you must be a very good student," said Orrin, and went back to his letter, and folded it, and sealed it.

I remember the seal. The grey wax, and the way he pressed it down with his thumb, and the little curling mark it left behind. I didn't know why I was remembering it. Some part of me must have known. The part that always knows things a little too late.

---

On the third evening, he talked about his son.

The lamp had burned low, and the way conversations wander after dark, they had come round to families.

"I had a son once," said Orrin.

He said it to the lamp, not to Zayn. His voice was very quiet.

"And a brother. A long time ago, before I came to the palace. There was a sickness that year, in the town where I was born. It came through like a wind. My brother first. Then my boy." He turned his cup slowly in his long fingers. "He was six. He used to sit on my knee while I wrote, and try to catch the pen."

My tail glowed gold on the roof. Every word of it was true.

The truly dangerous liars don't lie very often. They tell you the truth, and the truth is sad and real, and it opens you up like a key in a lock. Then, once the door is open, they walk in.

"I'm sorry," said Zayn.

"So am I." Orrin smiled at him, tired and gentle. "I've spent a long time trying to forget them. It doesn't work, you know. Not the way you'd hope."

Zayn was quiet for a while. Then he said, "Tell me about my father."

Orrin set the cup down.

"He carried what you carry," he said. "The Light. Before you. The old murals have a name for him. You've seen them, I think. In the glass city."

Zayn's hand went to his chest.

"*The Runner*," he whispered.

"The Runner," said Orrin. "He was afraid of what the Light would ask of him. So he ran." He paused. "He's alive, Zayn."

Zayn's breath went out of him as if he'd been struck.

"Where?" His voice cracked. "Where is he?"

"When you're stronger," said Orrin, and reached across the table and laid a hand over Zayn's, very lightly. "I promise. When you're stronger, I'll take you to him."

It's a strange thing to sit on a roof and watch a boy's heart get caught on a hook. He leaned toward that old man the way a plant leans toward a window.

He trusted him.

For the first time since Soren, Zayn trusted someone.

---

That night he climbed up to the roof.

He didn't say anything at first. He just sat down beside me with his knees pulled up, looking out over the nine palms and the dark dunes beyond them. The Ash was thinner now. Down to a half, and still shrinking.

"He's alive," Zayn said at last. "My father."

"I heard."

"He said he'd take me to him."

"I heard that too."

He turned his head and looked at me. "You don't believe him."

My tail stayed exactly as it was. I didn't say anything.

"Kiri."

"Wear the bell," I said.

He frowned. "What?"

"Hesset's bell. The one in your bag. Take it out and wear it." I looked at the palms. "Humour an old fox."

"Why?"

I didn't answer.

"Why?" he said again, sharper. "If you know something, *tell* me. If he's lying, tell me what he's lying about."

There it was. The door, wide open, and all I had to do was walk through it.

*Don't trust him.* Three words. That's all it would have taken. And he would have asked, *Why not?* And I would have had to tell him where I'd been on the night of the full Ash, and what I'd been, and everything that came after. I wasn't ready to say that out loud. Not to him. Not even to you, yet. And I had no proof. I had nothing but my own word, and my own word had gone dark at the City of Glass.

I'm not going to make excuses. I've told you I won't. I'll only tell you what I did.

I said nothing.

Zayn looked at me for a long time. Then he made a small, tired sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Where were you?" he said. "That night."

I said nothing.

"That's what I thought," he said, and went back down the stairs.

---

In the morning, he wore the bell.

I think he did it out of spite, to prove me wrong, the way you'd taste a dish someone said was poisoned just to show them. Or maybe he did it for Hesset. He'd been angry at the old man ever since the murals in the City of Glass told him what Hesset hadn't. Maybe, in that soft blue house, he'd started to forgive him a little. You can't always tell why people do things. Sometimes they can't either.

But he tied it to his belt, on a short thread, under the fold of his scarf where it wouldn't show. A small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

It didn't ring all morning. Nobody lied to him all morning. Orrin was in his room, writing.

I went down into the market to find something to eat.

---

That's where I saw Bram.

I almost didn't recognise him. The boy I'd last seen in Harrow's End had been broad and loud and well-fed, almost a prince. This boy was thin as a stick. His clothes were rags. His feet were bound in strips of cloth, black with dirt, and his lips were split from thirst. He was standing at the old well with his hands cupped, begging for water, and nobody was giving him any.

There was a grey streak across one side of his face. Not the old one, from the Husk in the lane. A new one, running from his temple down into his hair, as if someone had dragged a dirty thumb across him.

I went very still in the shadow of an awning.

He didn't see me. He was watching the house with the blue shutters. He watched it the way a hungry dog watches a butcher's door. And after a while, when the market got busy around noon, he turned and slipped off down a narrow alley between the dyer's yard and the wall.

I followed him.

The alley turned, and turned again, and ended in a little dead square full of broken pots, where the red and saffron cloths from the dyer's yard hung over the wall like curtains.

Someone was waiting there.

Grey robe. Smooth white mask, with no face on it at all.

I flattened myself against the wall behind a cracked water jar and stopped breathing.

---

"Well?" said the mask. Its voice was flat and dry, like sand poured from a cup.

"He's here," said Bram. His voice shook. "You were right. He's in the house with the blue shutters. The top room, the one that looks over the palms. He sleeps by the window. He's got a sword, a broken one. He keeps it on the bed."

"You're certain it's him."

"I'd know him anywhere." Bram swallowed. "I've known him all my life."

"Good," said the mask. "Tonight, then."

"And Tobin?" Bram took a step forward. "You said. You *promised*. When you found us in the desert, you said you'd keep him safe from the grey. You said if I found the glowing boy for you, you'd give my brother back."

"When we have the bearer," said the mask, "the child goes free."

My tail went dark.

It went completely dark, like a lamp with the oil run out. I wanted to scream at Bram, *Look at me! Can't you see?* But he wasn't looking at me. He was looking at that blank white face as if it were the only door left in the world.

"Everyone's gone grey," he said. His voice cracked in half. "Do you understand? All of them. My father sits by the well all day and he doesn't know my name. Oszkar. Garrem. Everyone. I took Tobin and I ran, and we walked for days, and then you came, and—" He stopped. He pressed his hands over his face. "He's seven. He's only seven."

"Tonight," said the mask, and turned away.

Bram stood alone in the dead square for a long time, his hands over his face, his shoulders shaking. Then he went back up the alley without looking round.

---

I've thought about that conversation a great deal.

At first, it made no sense to me. Why would the Unnamed need a starving boy to tell them where Zayn slept? Their master was the one who'd *put* him there. Orrin had chosen the room. Orrin had chosen the bed.

I understood it later. A man like Orrin never lets his own hands be seen. If anything went wrong, if the bearer escaped, if he lived long enough to wonder how the masks had found him, it would be Bram's face he remembered. The boy from his village. The boy who'd thrown the first stone. Not the kind old man with the dates and the falcon, who'd tried so hard to keep him safe.

It was a clever plan. It would have worked, too.

Except for a small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

---

I ran.

I ran faster than I'd moved since the storm, my burned side tearing and smoking, across the market and under a camel and through a basket of figs.

The gate of the blue-shuttered house was shut.

I found a spot where the bricks were rough, and climbed. I'm not built for climbing. I'm built for running and digging and being rude. It took far too long. By the time I dragged myself over the top of the wall and dropped into the courtyard, the Fire was going down red behind the palms.

The giant was standing by the fountain.

He turned his head and looked at me. That was all. He simply stood between me and the door, and when I tried to go round him, he was there. And when I tried the other way, he was there too.

I could have changed. I've told you that before, and I'll tell you again before this story's done. I could have let the dark half out, and maybe I'd have got past him, and maybe I wouldn't.

I was still standing there, shaking, deciding, when I heard the sound upstairs.

---

They were sitting at supper. That was the strange thing. Zayn told me later. It was such an ordinary evening.

There was bread and lentils and a little bowl of dates. Orrin had been telling him why nobody in Thornwell planted a tenth palm, and for one evening Zayn had almost forgotten to be sad.

Then there were footsteps on the stairs. Many footsteps, soft and quick.

The door opened.

Grey robes. White masks. Five of them, then eight, filling the doorway and spilling into the room, and in their hands were blades. Long and thin, the colour of old ash, and they seemed to drink the lamplight instead of reflecting it.

Zayn was on his feet. The table went over. His hand went to his belt for the knife, and the knife wasn't there; it was upstairs, in his bag, with the broken sword, on the bed by the window, exactly where Bram had said it would be.

And Orrin rose.

He rose smoothly from his cushion, the old man in his blue robes, and stepped between Zayn and the masks. He put one arm out behind him, shielding the boy, the way a father shields a child.

"Stay behind me, child," he said. His voice was low and calm and full of love. "You're safe. No one will harm you while I live."

And the bell rang.

---

It wasn't loud. That's what I always remember. I heard it from the courtyard, through the shutters, a single clear note, high and sweet as a drop of water falling into a well. A small sound. A tiny sound.

It filled the whole room.

Zayn went completely still.

He looked down at his belt. At the bell, swinging on its thread under the fold of his scarf, still shivering with the note. Then he looked up at Orrin's back. At the silver hair. At the outstretched arm.

Orrin turned his head. The soft face didn't change at all. But the eyes did. Those faded grey eyes moved down to the bell and rested there, and something in them went flat and cold and very, very tired.

"Ah," he said quietly. "Hesset."

Zayn's lips were white. "The ring," he whispered. "Did he give you the ring?"

"Of course he did," said Orrin.

The bell rang again.

And then, like a lamp being lit in a dark room, all of it came at once. Zayn told me he could feel the pieces falling into place, one after another, each one a little stone hitting him in the back. The bag on the camel. The dates on the plate. *Soren told me you liked them.* The ring that had not been on Soren's hand.

*He gave me this.*

"No," said Zayn. It came out of him like something tearing. "*No.*"

Orrin sighed. He lowered his arm.

"I should have searched you myself," he said, and stepped aside.

---

The masks came in.

Zayn fought. I want you to know that. He had no knife and no sword and no friend, and he fought anyway: with his fists, with the overturned table, with a lamp he swung in a burning arc that sent two of the masks reeling back.

And the light came.

But it came *grey*.

Thin and dim and sputtering, the colour of the Hollow, the colour of fear and fury. It crawled out of his fists like smoke and did nothing at all. A mask stepped in close and laid a grey blade flat against his forearm, almost gently.

Zayn screamed.

It didn't cut him. There was no blood. But where the blade touched his skin, the grey light went *into* it, sucked out of him like water into dry sand, and he felt something leave him. A memory. A small one. He'd tell me later he never knew which. Only that it had been warm, and now there was a cold place where it used to be.

That was when I came through the shutters.

I don't remember how I got past the giant. Then I was in the room in a spray of broken wood, and I went for the nearest mask with every tooth I had.

I got one ankle. That's all. Then a hand like the side of a house closed around my middle, right over the burn, and the giant lifted me into the air, and I made a sound I'm not proud of, and he threw me.

I hit the wall. The world went white and then grey and then very far away.

---

The last thing Zayn saw, before the sack came down over his head, was the doorway.

There was someone standing in it. Behind the masks, in the shadow at the top of the stairs. A thin boy in rags, with his feet wrapped in cloth and a grey streak across his face.

Bram.

They looked at each other across the wrecked room. The boy who'd thrown the first stone, and the boy it had hit.

"They've got Tobin," Bram said. His voice was barely there at all. "I'm sorry. Zayn, I'm sorry."

He had never once, in fifteen years, called him by his name. I don't think he'd even known it, until the masks told him.

The bell on Zayn's belt hung silent. Not a whisper. Every word was true.

Then the sack came down, and the grey blades came with it, and the room went dark.

---

I woke alone on the floor of a room that smelled of lamp oil and spilled lentils.

The masks were gone. The old man was gone. The broken sword was gone from the bed upstairs, and the bag with it. Out in the courtyard, the fountain was still running, as if nothing had happened at all.

And on the floor, near the overturned table, there was a scrap of thread. Snapped. A little bronze bell lay beside it on the tiles, on its side, silent.

I picked it up in my teeth.

I didn't know then where they'd taken him. I didn't know whether there'd be anything left of him when I found him. I only knew that the bell had rung, and that it had rung too late, and that I had been standing in a courtyard when it did, with the truth in my mouth, saying nothing.

Bram told me, many years later, that he never forgave himself for that night.

Neither did I.

---

*End of Chapter Fourteen*

# Chapter Fifteen
## The Buried Ship

> *A ship in the desert is a coffin waiting for the sea.*
> — a riddle the caravan children tell, which has no answer

---

I'm going to tell you this part the way he told it to me.

He told it once, in the mountain caves, by a fire that kept going out, in a flat, quiet voice I never heard him use again. Most of that time I was lying in the dark under a dead ship with sand in my mouth, listening.

But I heard everything.

---

It took me a night and a day to find it.

I followed the camels, and under the camel-smell, something cold and dry and empty, like a room where no one has spoken in a hundred years. The smell of grey blades.

I carried Hesset's bell in my teeth the whole way. Don't ask me why. It seemed important not to put it down.

At dusk on the second day, under an Ash thinned almost to a sliver, I came over a long ridge and saw it.

A ship.

There's no sea in the Destiny Desert. There never has been, not in all my centuries. And yet there it lay in the trough between two great dunes: a vast wooden hull, longer than the whole of Harrow's End, sunk on its side into the sand like a drowned animal. Its prow reared out of the dune, carved like some long-necked beast with its mouth open. Its ribs showed through the broken planks, pale as bones.

And inside it, through every crack and gap in the hull, lanterns burned.

Grey lanterns. Their light was the colour of ash.

Then I crept down the slope in the dark, and found a place where the sand had drifted up against the hull, and dug.

---

He woke up chained.

A great beam ran down the middle of the hold, black with age, and from an iron ring in it ran a grey chain to a cuff around each of his wrists. The chain wasn't iron. It was cold in a way iron isn't, and when the light in his chest tried to rise, the chain drank it.

The hold smelled of old salt, which made no sense, and of lamp smoke, which did. The curve of the hull rose around him like the inside of a ribcage.

And in the far corner, tossed in a heap like rubbish, were his bag and Soren's broken sword.

He stared at that sword for a long time.

---

They brought him up on the first night, for the burning.

The upper deck lay tilted under the sky, one edge running down into the sand like a ramp. The Unnamed stood in a great circle on it. Fifty of them, perhaps more, grey robes and smooth white masks, silent as Husks. In the middle stood an iron bowl, and in the bowl a fire burned.

The flames were grey.

To one side, in a line, stood the children.

There were eleven of them. The youngest was perhaps five. The oldest might have been twelve. They wore little grey robes, and their heads had been shaved, and they stood very straight and still, the way children stand when they've learned that moving gets you hurt. None of them wore masks. Not yet. The masks came later, when there was nothing left behind their faces to hide.

The Unnamed brought him into the circle and forced him to his knees.

Then three young men stepped forward from the ranks. They wore grey robes, but no masks. Each held a strip of white cloth, and on each strip, in charcoal, a word was written.

A name.

"*Names are chains,*" said the circle, all together, in one flat voice. "*Memory is pain. Forget, and be free.*"

One by one, the young men held their names over the bowl and let them fall.

The cloth caught, and curled, and was gone. Each young man, as his name burned, gave a small gasp, as though something had been pulled out of his chest on a string. Then their faces went slack and empty, and the brothers fitted three white masks over three blank faces, and that was all.

Three men had walked into the circle. Three nobodies walked out.

Zayn told me that was the worst thing he ever saw. Worse than the Husks, because the Husks hadn't chosen it.

---

Then a figure stepped into the firelight, and the circle bowed.

It wore a grey robe and a white mask like all the others. But when it spoke, Zayn knew the voice before the first word was finished.

"Hello again, child."

The Voice. That's what they called him there. Not Orrin. Not vizier. Just *the Voice*, because a voice was all he had left after he burned his name.

He crouched in front of Zayn and held out a strip of white cloth and a stick of charcoal.

"Write it," he said.

Zayn looked at the cloth.

"You can, now, can't you?" said the Voice softly. "He taught you. The prince. I imagine your name was one of the first things he taught you. Write it, and give it to the fire, and all of this stops. The grief. The guilt. That walk under the full Ash, over and over, every time you close your eyes. You'll never have to remember it again."

The charcoal hung in front of Zayn's face.

He thought about Soren drawing letters in the sand with a stick. *Z. Like a lightning bolt. Then A, like a tent. Then Y, like a man waving for help.* Laughing. Always laughing.

Zayn spat on the cloth.

The circle was very quiet.

The Voice looked down at the wet spot for a moment. Then he folded the cloth carefully and tucked it into his robe.

"I have to go back to Veimore," he said, rising. "The king grows restless without me, and the Night of Embers is close. But I'll leave you in good hands." He turned to the circle. "Drain him slowly. Make him remember. When he is empty enough to forget his own name, he will give it to us himself. They always do."

He climbed onto a camel at the edge of the sand and rode off into the dark toward the red city. I'd like to tell you it was the last time we ever saw him. I can't.

---

They put him back on his chain in the hold, and the draining began.

I'm not going to tell you all of it. But somebody should know what he survived.

They came in pairs, every few hours. One held the lantern. The other held a grey blade.

They didn't cut him. A grey blade doesn't cut skin. It lies flat against you, cold as the bottom of a well, and it pulls. And the Light comes out of you in thin gold threads, the way a thread pulls out of a hem, and runs into the blade, and turns grey, and is gone.

And with each thread, a memory came.

Never a good one. Every time the cold metal touched his arm, Zayn was six years old again, running from a crowd of children with a stone in his back. Or he was kneeling with Tobin's small hand in his, and Bram was screaming *Monster!* and the first rock was already in the air.

Or he was on the dune under the full Ash.

Over and over. *Promise me you'll never give up.* And Zayn turning away. Walking. Not looking back. Again. And again. And every time, the last thing he saw was Soren's face the next morning. Grey. Cracked with gold.

Under the hull, with the sand pressing in around me and my burned side screaming, I heard every sound he made.

I didn't make any. I couldn't do anything. Not while he was chained to that beam with fifty masks around him. I had one chance, and I had to choose the moment exactly right.

That's what I told myself. It was even true.

---

On the second night, they let the Husks in.

Dozens of them were penned in the deep lower hold. The Unnamed didn't fight Husks. They *kept* them, the way you'd keep dogs. And Husks are drawn to light.

They came shuffling out of the dark and gathered around the boy on the chain, so close he could feel the cold coming off them. They didn't touch him. They just stared, the way they'd stared at the lamp in Bram's window.

And then, all at once, they spoke.

Every mouth. Together. One voice, coming out of thirty grey throats at the same moment, low and soft and terrible.

"*Little light.*"

Zayn went rigid on his chain.

He knew that voice. He'd heard it in a dream, in the Valley of Echoes, leaning over him in the dark.

"*Little nameless light,*" said the Husks. "*They threw stones at you. They called you monster. They drove you into the desert to die. And you'd burn for them? Why? No one remembers the bearers. Ask the fox. Ask him how many he's buried. Ask him if anyone sings their names.*"

Zayn shut his eyes.

"*Let it go,*" said thirty voices. "*Let me hold it for you. It's so heavy, little light. I know. I carried it too.*"

Then the voice changed. It went softer. Almost shy.

"*Hello, Kiri,*" it said. "*I know you're down there. You always did listen at doors.*"

Under the hull, in the dark, I stopped breathing.

I'm not going to tell you what his voice sounded like, all those centuries ago, when it was just a shepherd boy calling me in from the hills at dusk. That's mine.

I'll only tell you that I didn't answer. And that it was the hardest thing I have ever not done.

---

The children came at dawn, with water.

It was a lesson, I think. The Unnamed wanted them to see what happens to people who hold on.

One of them stopped in front of Zayn and stared at him.

He was seven. His head was shaved. His grey robe was too big for him. And his eyes were brown, clear brown, not yet faded at all.

"You're the monster," said Tobin.

Zayn looked at him. He'd last seen that face grey and empty in Harrow's End, coming slowly back to life under his hands.

"Bram said you weren't," Tobin whispered. "After. He said he was wrong." His lip shook. "Is Bram coming?"

Zayn opened his mouth. And closed it again.

He wanted to say *yes. I promise. I'll take you home.* It's what you say to a frightened child in the dark.

But Zayn couldn't lie, and he didn't know.

"I'll try," he said instead. It was all he had. "I'll try. I swear I will."

Tobin nodded, as if *I'll try* from someone who couldn't lie was worth more than *I promise* from anyone else.

Maybe it was.

---

That night, alone on the chain, Zayn reached for his name and couldn't find it.

The blades had been at him all day. He was so cold he couldn't feel his hands. And he thought, the way you think of something just to make sure it's still there, *My name is*—

And there was nothing.

A blank, smooth place, like the face of a mask. He could feel the shape of where his name should be. But when he reached for it, his hand closed on air. He tried to remember who had named him, and the village, and the patched tent, and they were going grey at the edges, like cloth left in the Fire.

*Write it,* the Voice had said. *They always do.*

His finger moved in the dust on the floor of the hold. Searching. He didn't know what for.

"That's not even a letter," said someone.

The hold was full of gold.

---

Soren was sitting on the beam above him, swinging his legs.

He looked the way he had on the dune. Light coming through the edges of him. His hair falling loose. That bare finger, where the ring should have been.

"I spend a whole turn of the Ash teaching you," he said, "and you give up on the first word. I'm insulted. I'm deeply insulted."

Zayn stared up at him.

"Z," said Soren. He leaned down and pointed at the dust. "Like a lightning bolt. Go on."

Zayn's finger moved. A line. A slant. A line.

"A. Like a tent." Soren was smiling now. "Y. Like a man waving for help, which is you, right now, honestly. And N. Two posts and a fence. Go on. Say it."

"*Zayn,*" whispered Zayn.

It came back all at once, like water into a dry well. A boy of seven at the edge of a village well, deciding he would be called a word that sounded like wind over a dune.

"There you are," said Soren softly.

His hand came down onto Zayn's shoulder. Warm. Warm as the Fire.

"You promised," he said. "Remember? Never give up." And then, quieter, with a grin that was almost the old grin, "Besides. There are eleven kids up there who don't know their names either. Somebody's going to have to teach them. I'd do it, but I'm a bit busy being dead."

Then the gold went out of the hold, and Zayn was alone in the dark, with four letters drawn in the dust beside his hand.

He didn't wipe them away.

---

The guard that came at midnight was alone, and he was humming.

Zayn knew the tune before he knew the man. Fire and rising wings. It was the song from the Singing Wells, the one he'd heard in the water: a man's voice, low and warm, singing over a small boy's hand.

The masked figure came down the ladder with a lantern and two grey blades crossed on his back. He sat down a little way off and went on humming, very softly, as if he didn't know he was doing it.

Brother Seven. Always hunting him. Always, somehow, missing.

Zayn started to hum too.

It just came out of him: the next line, the one the masked man hadn't reached yet.

The humming stopped.

Brother Seven sat completely still. The white mask stared at him across the lantern-light.

"Where did you learn it?" Zayn whispered. "That song. Who taught it to you?"

No answer. Brother Seven didn't speak. I never once, in all that time, heard him speak.

He stood and drew one of the grey blades. Drain him slowly. He knelt beside the chain and laid the blade flat against Zayn's arm.

And Zayn's hand closed around his wrist.

He wasn't thinking about himself. That's the only reason it worked. He was thinking about eleven children in grey robes upstairs, standing straight and still. About a boy with clear brown eyes asking whether his brother was coming.

The light came up out of his chest like the Fire coming up over the dunes.

*Gold.*

It ran down his arm and into the grey blade and *through* it, faster than the blade could drink. It went up the masked man's wrist. Up his arm. Into him.

And they both saw it.

---

A tent, at night. Lamplight. A storm howling outside, the walls of the tent bowing in and out like something breathing.

A small boy, five years old, sitting on a rug, very serious. In his arms, held carefully, the way he'd been shown, was a newborn baby wrapped in a grey cloth covered in strange woven symbols. The baby had one tiny fist wrapped around the boy's finger and wouldn't let go.

And somewhere above them, a man's voice was singing. Low and warm. Fire and rising wings.

The boy looked down at the baby and smiled. A gap-toothed smile, so proud he could burst.

Then the tent wall tore open, and there were white masks in the storm.

---

The vision broke.

So did the mask.

I heard it from under the hull, a sharp, clean crack like ice splitting. A line ran straight down the middle of the smooth white face, from brow to chin, and golden light shone through it. Through the gap, for a single heartbeat, he saw an eye. Wide and wet and pale.

Too pale for a desert child.

Zayn saw something else, too, in the moment before the light faded. He saw what he always saw. The full Ash. The Crown Dune. The fire. But longer this time, clearer, and there were people in it now. A man on his knees in the sand, begging. A figure in a cracked white mask, standing very still.

Then it was gone.

Brother Seven tore free and scrambled backwards, one hand clamped over the crack in his mask, making a small, broken, wordless sound, like a child who's woken from a nightmare and can't find the door.

He stared at Zayn.

Then his blade flashed once in the lantern-light. I can't tell you whether he meant to do it. I don't think he knew himself. But the grey chain parted between Zayn's wrists like a cut thread.

And Brother Seven fled. Up the ladder, through the hatch, out into the night, and was gone.

---

The cuffs were still on his wrists. It didn't matter. He was up and across the hold before the hatch had stopped swinging, and he had the broken sword in his hand and his bag over his shoulder.

He went up the ladder to the children.

They were in a narrow room near the prow, awake. Children in places like that are always awake. Eleven small, frightened faces turned toward the boy in the doorway with golden cracks shining on his arms.

"Up," he said. "All of you. Now. Hold hands. Don't let go."

They stared at him. They had been taught not to move.

Tobin moved first. He got up and took the smallest girl's hand. Then the next child got up. And the next.

That's when the shouting started.

Running feet. Grey lanterns swinging. Masks poured toward the prow from both ends of the ship. Zayn backed the children into the corner and stood in front of them with half a sword and no way out, and the light in his chest flickered grey, because he was very, very afraid.

And the ship moved.

---

That was me.

I'd been waiting under the hull for three nights. Three nights in the sand with my burned side splitting and smoking and Malek's voice in my ears. I had one chance. And when I felt the light go gold above me, I knew.

I let it out.

The other half. The big half. The one I hide from every bearer. The one he'd burned in the storm.

It hurt. The burn hadn't healed, and the dark part of me came up through it like smoke through a crack, screaming. I became the thing I hate, a great shadow-beast with ember eyes and one silver eye burning cold, my back pressed up against the belly of a dead ship, sand pouring off me in rivers.

And I *lifted*.

The ship groaned, like the whole desert sighing. Timbers that hadn't moved in a thousand years tore loose from the dune. Masks went sliding and tumbling down the deck. Grey lanterns smashed. And the old hull split along its belly with a crack like thunder, and the night came pouring in.

I held it. Sand and wood and fifty masked men and a thousand years of silence, all on my back.

Through the gap in the broken hull, Zayn looked down at me.

He'd seen this shape once before, in the storm, and been so afraid of it that his light had burned it.

He wasn't afraid now. I saw his face. I'll never forget his face.

"*Kiri,*" he said.

That was all. Just my name. Not *monster*. My name.

"*Hold it,*" he said.

"*What do you think I'm doing?*" I said. My voice came out like a rockslide.

He laughed. I swear to you, in the middle of all that, he laughed. Then he was through the gap with eleven children holding hands behind him, sliding down the sand under the great dark arch of me, one after another, Tobin last, the smallest girl on Zayn's back.

When the last bare foot touched the sand outside, I let go.

The ship came down. It fell back into its dune with a roar of splitting timber, and the sand rushed in over it like water closing over a stone, and the grey lights inside it went out one by one.

---

I don't remember shrinking. I remember lying in the sand, small again, a fox again, with my side on fire and my legs not working.

I remember hands. His hands, picking me up. Holding me against his chest, right over the mark, where it was warm.

"I've got you," he said.

I had something in my mouth. I'd snatched it from the sand where I'd hidden it. I let it drop into his palm.

A small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

"You dropped this," I said.

He looked at it. He looked at me. And behind us, over the ridge, came the sound I'd been dreading. Hissing. Claws on sand. The long, low howl of Hollow Hounds, and the drumming feet of sand lizards, and the flat voices of the Unnamed calling to one another in the dark.

Zayn tied the bell to his belt. He tucked me into the front of his shirt. He took Tobin's hand.

And he ran.

---

*End of Chapter Fifteen*

# Chapter Sixteen
## The Rider Unveiled

> *Every rider in the dark is someone's father.*
> — from a lullaby of the mountain herders

---

Have you ever tried to run across the desert at night with eleven children?

Don't.

They don't run in a line. They run in a sort of frightened cloud, and every few steps one of them trips, and the whole cloud stops to pick him up. The little ones can't climb the steep slopes, so the bigger ones have to drag them. Nobody can see. Everybody is crying, or trying not to, which is louder.

And Zayn ran in front of them with a broken sword in one hand and Tobin's hand in the other and a half-dead fox stuffed down the front of his shirt, and he didn't stop once.

I could feel his heart through his ribs. It was going like a drum.

Behind us, the Hounds were getting closer.

---

You'll remember the Hollow Hounds. Shadow wolves with ember eyes. They track the bearer's light the way a real wolf tracks blood, and they never get tired, and they never give up.

Zayn's light was still leaking out of him. He couldn't help it. It came through the golden cracks on his arms, faint and flickering, and on that dark night with the Ash down to a sliver, he must have shone like a lantern on a hill.

The Hounds were howling to each other now. I counted six voices. Then nine. And under the howling, the drumming of sand lizards, and the flat, empty voices of the Unnamed, calling out directions.

We came over a ridge, and the dune on the far side was too steep. The children stopped at the top in a huddle, staring down at the long fall of sand.

Zayn turned around.

Below us, in the trough we'd just crossed, eyes were opening in the dark. Pairs of them, red as coals, one after another, spreading out in a ring.

"Behind me," said Zayn to the children. His voice was very calm. "All of you. Stay together. Don't look at their eyes."

He set Tobin's hand into the hand of the oldest boy. He lifted the broken sword.

"You can't," I said, from inside his shirt. "Not with half a blade and no strength left. Zayn, there are too many."

"I know," he said.

My tail glowed. It was true. He knew.

He stood at the top of the ridge anyway, between the dark and the children, and the light came up in him. Not grey. Not this time. It came up gold, because he wasn't thinking about himself at all.

The first Hound leapt.

---

He cut it out of the air.

Half a blade, the way Soren had taught him. Soren had been the best sword in Veimore, even broken. Zayn wasn't. But he was quick, and the gold ran down his arm into the steel, and when the sword went through the Hound it came apart into ash and sparks.

The second one he missed. It hit him in the chest and knocked him flat, and I went flying out of his shirt into the sand. Its jaws were an inch from his face. He got the hilt between them and shoved, and light burst from his palm, and the Hound shrieked and was gone.

Then there were four of them at once.

He got up. He fought. He was bleeding from a scratch on his cheek and the golden cracks were spreading up his arms, and he was getting slower. I could see it. I was lying in the sand trying to make my legs work, and I could see him getting slower.

Then the fourth Hound came over his shoulder, straight at the children.

And a spear came out of the dark.

---

It took the Hound in mid-leap and pinned it to the sand, and the Hound came apart in a spray of ash.

Hoofbeats. The long, rolling stride of a camel at full gallop, coming down the dune from the north.

A pale camel, almost white in the dark. And on its back a tall figure in grey wrappings, his face hidden behind a veil, leaning low from the saddle to pull his spear free as he passed.

The Veiled Rider.

You'll have heard about him. He'd come before. Out of the dark with a spear the night the village drove Zayn out. Out of the storm on the day the Veilbeast came, a hand reaching down to drag him from the sand. Twice, and never a word. Never a face.

This was the third time.

He rode through the Hounds like a scythe through grass. I'd forgotten how good he was. That's a lie. I hadn't forgotten. I'd just hoped I would. The spear went left and right, and the pale camel turned on the spot, and Hounds burst into ash wherever he went. Zayn fought beside him, gold flaring from the broken sword. The lizard riders came over the far ridge, took one look, and wheeled away into the night.

Then it was quiet.

The children were crying at the top of the ridge. Zayn was on his knees in the sand, gasping. Ash drifted down around us like grey snow.

The Rider slid down from his camel.

He stood there for a long moment, looking at the boy. Then, slowly, as though his hands didn't quite belong to him, he reached up and pulled the veil away from his face.

---

He was a man of perhaps forty. Weathered and lean, his skin burned dark by the desert. His beard was cropped short and shot through with grey. There was a hollow look about him, the look of someone who's slept alone in cold places for a very long time.

And his eyes were light. Too light for a desert man. Grey-gold, like sand at dawn.

Zayn's eyes.

Zayn stared up at him. I watched the understanding come into his face, one piece at a time, the way the Fire comes up over a dune.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

The man's mouth moved. Nothing came out. He tried again.

"I'm your father," he said.

The little bronze bell on Zayn's belt hung perfectly still.

Not a whisper. Not a shiver. And beside Zayn in the sand, my tail lit up so bright that the man flinched from it.

---

Zayn didn't move for a long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "Three times."

"More than that," said the man. His voice was rough, like a door that hadn't been opened in years. "Three times you saw me. There were others."

"You were *there*." Zayn's voice cracked. "All this time. You were right there, and you never—" He stopped. His chest was heaving. "Why did you leave me? In the sand? Why did you *leave* me there?"

The man went down on his knees in front of him.

"To protect you," he said. "They were hunting us both."

The bell stayed silent.

And that was when Zayn broke.

I'd seen him cry before. In the dunes after the full Ash. At Orrin's table, over the dates. But not like this. This came up out of him from somewhere so deep that I don't think he'd known it was there. Fifteen years of it. Every stone. Every *Nameless*. Every night in the patched tent listening to the wind and wondering who had thrown him away, and why, and whether they ever thought of him.

He hit the man once in the chest with his fist. It wasn't hard. There wasn't any strength left in him.

Then he fell forward, and his father caught him.

They knelt there in the ash together, the boy sobbing into the grey wrappings and the man holding him so tightly I thought they'd both break. The man was crying too, silently, his face pressed into his son's pale hair, his eyes squeezed shut.

I lay in the sand and looked away. Some things aren't for watching. Even for me.

---

His name was Daruun.

I knew it already. Of course I did. I'd walked beside him once, a long time ago, when he was young and had a feather on his chest and was so afraid of the Crown Dune that he couldn't say its name aloud. He was the eleventh. The one the murals call *the Runner*.

When he finally looked up from his son, his eyes found me.

"Fox," he said.

"Runner," I said.

Neither of us said anything else. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't have taken all night. I'm still angry at the ones who ran. I've told you that. I'm angry at him most of all, except one. But I'll tell you something I haven't admitted to anyone before.

I'd known who the Rider was from the first spear in the dark, back on the night of the stones. And I'd said nothing to the boy about that either.

I'm noticing a pattern. I expect you are too.

---

The mountain caves were a night's walk north, where the desert ended against a wall of red rock that rose straight up out of the sand like the edge of the world.

The dunes can't reach the mountains. That's why he'd gone there. The sand rolls up to the foot of the cliffs every night and falls back, the way the sea must fall back from a rock, and it can't write anything on stone. For a man who couldn't bear to read what the dunes were saying, it was the only safe place in the world.

His caves were high up, reached by a narrow path that switched back and forth across the cliff. Inside, they were dry and cool and lit by a small fire. Hides on the floor. Spears along the walls. Leather sacks of grey ash hung from pegs. The ash of Hollow beasts, the creatures he'd spent fifteen years hunting, trying to make up for what his running had let into the world.

The children fell asleep almost the moment they were inside, in a heap on the hides by the fire like a litter of puppies. Tobin was in the middle, his shaved head on the oldest boy's arm.

Daruun sat beside them and, very softly, without seeming to notice he was doing it, began to sing.

A low, warm voice. Fire and rising wings.

Zayn went completely still.

It was the voice from the Singing Wells. The man in the water, singing over a small boy's hand. He'd heard it before he could remember anything. He'd been hearing it, somewhere deep down, his whole life.

---

Daruun told him everything that night. I'll keep it short. It was the longest night of both their lives, and they've earned their privacy.

He had carried the Light before Zayn. He'd been afraid. Not of pain, he said, but of being gone. Of the world going on without him. So when the time came, he ran. He hid in these mountains, where the dunes couldn't follow, and he told himself the Light would choose someone else.

And because he ran, the Hollow grew.

He didn't stay hidden. Not then. Years later he came down out of the mountains, and he had a family, and he was happy for a little while. Later, sitting by that fire, Zayn asked him about his mother.

"Another night," said Daruun, and his face closed like a door. "I'll tell you about her. I promise. Not tonight."

The bell didn't ring. He meant it.

On the night Zayn was born, the Unnamed came out of a storm. They'd been hunting the Runner for years. They wanted the Light, and they knew that it had passed into the newborn child. Daruun fought. He got the baby out. But his elder son, his first son, a boy of five, was dragged away screaming into the storm.

He searched for weeks. He found nothing. He believed the boy was dead.

And he knew that as long as the baby was with him, the Unnamed would find them both. So he carried his son across the desert to the edge of a poor village called Harrow's End, and in the middle of a sandstorm, he laid him down in the sand, wrapped in a grey cloth, and walked away.

"The storm hid my tracks," he said. "That was the desert, protecting you. I believed that. I had to believe it." He looked at the fire. "Every night since, I've wondered if I was just telling myself what I needed to hear."

Zayn was quiet for a long time.

Then he said, "Your son. The one they took. What was his name?"

Daruun closed his eyes.

"Tamir," he said.

And Zayn told him.

---

He told him about the guard in the hold. The phoenix song, hummed in the dark. The light running up the grey blade. A five-year-old boy in a tent, holding a newborn baby wrapped in grey, a fist around his finger. The mask cracking down the middle. One pale eye, wide and wet, staring.

"He's alive," Zayn said. "He's one of them. They call him Brother Seven. He's been hunting me since the start." He swallowed. "And he keeps letting me go."

Daruun didn't move.

He sat by the fire with his hands open on his knees. I watched the words go into him. I watched them hit the bottom.

Then he stood up, and walked to the mouth of the cave, and went down on his knees on the cold stone ledge, high above the dark desert.

I've heard a great many terrible sounds in my life. The Veilbeast's roar. Husks speaking with one voice. A boy screaming as a grey blade drank his light. The sound Daruun made on that ledge was worse than any of them. It was the sound of a man finding out that the worst thing he ever did had been even worse than he thought. Fifteen years. His son, alive, all that time, being emptied in the dark. And he'd been hiding in a cave.

Zayn didn't go to him. Not yet. Forgiveness doesn't work like that.

He turned to me instead.

"Did you know?" he said.

I could have said nothing. I'd had a lot of practice.

"Yes," I said.

My tail glowed. He looked at it, and then at me, and something went across his face that I'll be thinking about for a long time.

"You knew he was my *brother*."

"Yes."

He turned away. He didn't say anything else that night.

I told him the truth about that one, at least. There was another truth I was still sitting on, a heavier one. One out of two. You'll have to decide for yourself whether that counts.

---

In the morning, we gave the children away.

That sounds worse than it was. At the foot of the cliffs there was a valley with a spring in it, and a clan of nomad herders was camped there, as they were every year when the Ash went thin. They called themselves the Tamarisk clan, after the trees that grew around their spring. Daruun traded with them. Meat and hides and Hollow ash, for flour and salt and the occasional word with another human being.

Their headman was a broad, grey-bearded man with a voice like gravel. He looked at the eleven shaved heads for a long moment, and then at the golden cracks on Zayn's arms, and then at Daruun.

"Unnamed?" he said.

"Not anymore," said Zayn.

The headman nodded slowly. "Then they're ours," he said, "until someone comes for them, or until the grey lifts. Whichever's first."

Tobin held on to Zayn's waist for a long time before he'd let go.

"Will you find Bram?" he said into Zayn's shirt.

Zayn couldn't lie. He didn't know.

"If I see him," he said, "I'll tell him where you are. I'll tell him you were brave."

That was true. Tobin let go.

The smallest child, a boy of five, tugged at Zayn's sleeve as he turned to leave. "I don't know my name," he whispered. "They took it."

Zayn knelt down in front of him.

"Then you'll find it," he said. "Or you'll make a new one. I did."

My tail glowed all the way back up the cliff.

---

That evening, Daruun gave him two things.

The first was a pendant on a leather cord. A knot, cast in old bronze, a loop that turned back on itself and never ended. The infinity knot.

"Every bearer has worn one," said Daruun. "I took it off the night I ran. It was never mine to keep after that." He held it out. "It's yours."

Zayn put it on. It lay against his chest, just below the feather mark, and it was warm.

The second was a compass. Small and battered, in a brass case dented on one side.

"It doesn't point north," said Daruun. "It points toward whatever you're running from. For fifteen years, it pointed at the Crown Dune." He looked at the fire. "I stopped opening it."

Zayn opened it.

The needle spun. It wobbled. And then it settled, and it didn't point out of the cave at all. It didn't point at the desert, or the dunes, or the far-off place where the highest sand in the world waited under the full Ash.

It pointed at the broken sword, lying on the hides beside the fire.

---

He sat with it across his knees for a long time.

He'd carried that sword every step since the full Ash. He'd slept with it in his arms. He'd fought with it. And he had not once really looked at it, because looking at it meant looking at Soren, and he'd been running from that harder than he'd ever run from anything.

He turned it over in the firelight. Half a blade, notched and dull. A plain leather grip. A round bronze pommel at the end.

The pommel was loose.

He'd never noticed. It turned a little in his fingers, gritty, as if it had been unscrewed and screwed back on in a hurry. He twisted it. It turned again. And again. And then it came away in his hand, and the hilt was hollow, and there was something inside it.

A roll of paper, tied with thread.

Zayn's hands were shaking. He drew it out and unrolled it in the firelight.

There was a seal on it, broken. Grey wax. And pressed into the wax was a little curling mark that I knew, that *he* knew, because we had both watched an old man press that same seal into that same grey wax every evening in Thornwell, by the lamp, with the falcon waiting.

Orrin's seal.

And beneath it, writing. Fine and slanting.

Zayn read it.

He read it himself. Slowly. Out loud, the way Soren had taught him, his finger moving under each word, his lips shaping the letters one at a time.

"*To the Brothers,*" he read. "*On the Night of Embers, when every lantern in Veimore is lit, the king will die. Be ready on the palace stairs. When he falls, put out the lanterns. Then the city. The eldest son is gone. The youngest will come to me. Speak no names.*"

His finger stopped at the bottom.

"*The Voice.*"

---

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The fire cracked. Out on the ledge, the wind moved over the stone.

"He knew," Zayn said at last. "Soren. He had this. He was carrying it. He never told me." His voice was very small. "Why didn't he tell me?"

"Perhaps he meant to," said Daruun quietly. "Perhaps he ran out of time."

My tail stayed exactly as it was. I kept my mouth shut and looked at the fire. You'll know why by now, or you'll guess. I'm not going to say.

Zayn looked down at the paper in his hands. At the letters. At the words he could read, now, every one of them.

"Soren taught me to read," he said.

It came out of him like something breaking open. Not grief, or not only grief. Something else. As if every evening by the fire at the Wells, every backwards S and every falling-over R, every time Soren had laughed at him and made him try again, had been leading to this moment, in this cave, with this paper in his hands.

As if it had all been for something.

Later, by the fire, I saw him take Hesset's broken golden feather out of his bag, out of its grey cloth. The break across its middle had nearly closed. Only a few bent barbs were left. I looked at it and hated it, the way I told you I would.

He rolled the letter up again, carefully, and slid it back into the hollow hilt, and screwed the pommel tight. Then he stood up.

"I'm going to Veimore," he said.

Daruun was on his feet too. "They'll kill you. Every gate in that city is looking for the Prince-Killer. You'll be walking straight into Orrin's hands."

"Probably," said Zayn.

The bell on his belt didn't ring. Of course it didn't. It was his own voice, and he couldn't lie.

"The king has to see this," he said. "Soren's little brother is in that palace. And Soren died carrying it." He picked up the sword. "I promised him I'd never give up."

Daruun looked at him for a long moment. Then he put a hand on his son's shoulder.

Zayn let it stay there. He didn't lean into it. He didn't call him father. But he didn't step away.

It's not nothing. I've told you that before.

In the desert, you learn to count the things that are not nothing.

---

*End of Chapter Sixteen*

# PART FIVE: THE END IS THE BEGINNING

# Chapter Seventeen
## The Walls of Veimore

> *Behind every wall burns a lantern; behind every lantern, a fear.*
> — a saying of the lantern-keepers of Veimore

---

Veimore was red.

Not the warm red of a sunset. The dark, wet red of something that has been bleeding for a long time. The whole city was built from the same blood-coloured stone, and when the Fire sank behind it, the walls seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it.

People call it the City of the Dead and Darkness. They aren't being poetic. For as long as anyone can remember, the dead of the whole desert have been carried to Veimore on the backs of slow camels, wrapped in white, and laid in the catacombs beneath its streets. There are more dead under that city than there are grains of sand in your paw. Above them, on every street, the living paint the names of the dead on the walls, so that no one is forgotten. Some streets have more names on them than people.

And the lanterns. There were thousands of them. On the walls, on the rooftops, in every window, hanging from every arch, burning day and night, because the people of Veimore believed that the moment the last lantern went out, the darkness would walk in. The smoke of them hung over the city in a brown haze, so that even at noon the sky above it was never quite clear.

We lay on our bellies at the top of the last dune and watched the grey press against the walls as the night came on. It didn't come in. It sat there in the dark beyond the lantern-light, patient as a dog outside a butcher's door.

"It's waiting," said Zayn.

"Everything out here is waiting," I said. "That's what the desert is for."

I want you to see him properly, because there isn't much time left to look. He was thinner than he'd been in Harrow's End, his hair bleached nearly white. Golden cracks ran along both his forearms and up the side of his neck, fading so slowly now that the old ones hadn't gone before the new ones came. Soren's broken sword was strapped across his back in grey cloth, with the letter inside the hilt. He touched it about every hundred breaths, the way he touched his mark.

Behind us, at the bottom of the dune, the pale camel shifted and groaned. Daruun stood beside it with his veil down around his neck, and I could feel him not saying things. He was very good at that. It ran in the family.

"I'll stay out here," he said at last. "The Unnamed won't come through the gates like honest men. They'll come out of the desert. When they do, I'll see them first."

Zayn nodded without turning round.

"And Zayn." His father's voice caught on the name, the way it still did. "Come back out to me. After. Promise me."

The boy was quiet for a long time.

You know why. You've been listening. He *couldn't* promise it. The feather on his chest knew the future better than any of us. If he'd tried to say *I promise*, it would have burned like a coal.

"I'll try," he said instead.

My tail glowed. It was completely true, and it wasn't anything like enough.

Daruun looked down at the sand. Then he climbed onto the camel and rode away along the hollow between the dunes, and the wind came behind him and smoothed out his tracks.

---

There were guards on every gate, and every guard knew what to look for. *A pale-haired boy, a fox with odd eyes, a broken blade. The Prince-Killer.* The story had run ahead of us across the whole desert the way stories do, faster than camels and twice as hungry.

So we didn't use the gates.

"He told me about it," Zayn said. We were crouched in the reeds where the oasis water slid out under the city wall through a low arch black with moss. "By the fire, the second night. An old water channel. It runs under the city and comes up beneath the palace kitchens. He used it to sneak out. And to sneak back in."

"What did he sneak back in *for*?"

Zayn smiled then, a real one, small and crooked and full of grief. "Honey cakes."

That was Soren. A prince of the last great city in the desert, and the most daring thing he ever did as a child was crawl through a drain for cake.

The channel was cold and waist-deep on Zayn, which meant it was considerably more than waist-deep on me. I rode on his shoulder with my tail held out of the water, complaining. His mark glowed through his wet shirt and gave us just enough light to see by.

Which was a pity, really, because then we could see the walls.

Halfway along, the channel ran beside the catacombs. On either side of us the stone was cut into shelves, row after row after row, and on every shelf lay a long white shape, wrapped and still. Above each one, painted in faded red, was a name. Thousands of them. They went on into the dark further than the light could reach, and the water made small sounds against the stone that were almost, but not quite, like whispering.

Zayn walked very slowly. He read some of the names as we passed. He couldn't help it. A boy who's only just learned to read reads everything.

"*Hamid*," he whispered. "*Little Dara. Joss the potter. Sefa, beloved.*" He swallowed. "Kiri. Who paints the names of the bearers?"

I didn't answer that. There was nothing true I could have said that he needed to hear.

After a very long time the channel ended in a row of slippery steps and a wooden hatch. Zayn pushed it up an inch. Warm air came through the gap, and with it a smell so wonderful I nearly fell off his shoulder: bread, onions, cinnamon, and honey. Lots of honey.

He climbed out into a storeroom full of sacks and jars and hanging garlic, and found himself face to face with a cleaver.

---

The woman holding it was the largest person I had seen in some centuries.

She was nearly as tall as Daruun and twice as wide, with forearms like a blacksmith's and a face as round and red as one of the city's own bricks. She held the cleaver the way other people hold a sword.

"Thief," she said. Her voice could have stripped paint. "Out of my drain like a rat. You've got a count of three to tell me why I shouldn't—"

Then she saw the sword.

The grey wrapping had come loose in the channel. The hilt stuck up over Zayn's shoulder, and the lamplight fell right across it.

The cleaver dropped an inch.

"Where," she said, in a completely different voice, "did you get that?"

Zayn stood there, dripping on her clean floor, and told her the truth, because that was the only thing he had ever known how to do.

"It was Soren's," he said. "He was my friend. I didn't kill him."

I sat on a sack of lentils and let my tail do the rest. It lit up gold, so bright the jars on the shelves threw shadows.

"His tail does that when someone's telling the truth," Zayn said, a little apologetically.

"It also does it when it's warm and dry," I said. "Which it isn't. Thank you for asking."

The woman shrieked. Then she clapped a hand over her mouth, and stood there with her eyes going wide and then wet. The cleaver clattered onto the stone.

"I knew it," she whispered through her fingers. "I *knew* it. I told them, and nobody listened to the cook."

She was Mother Yusa. She had run the palace kitchens for thirty years and half-raised the king's two sons, because the king was busy being a king and somebody had to make sure the boys ate. She told us this very fast, while she sat Zayn on a crate and threw a sack over his shoulders and put lentils and a plate of honey cakes in front of him, all without seeming to breathe.

"That boy," she said, pointing a floury finger at the hilt as if Soren himself were inside it, "stole honey cakes from my kitchen for ten years. Ten years! Came up out of that drain smelling like a dead fish, every single time, and thought I didn't know." Her chin wobbled. She steadied it. "A boy like that doesn't kill anyone. And neither do you. Eat."

Zayn ate.

He ate the way he'd eaten in Harrow's End when Auntie Sabba's bundles came, slowly and carefully, like a boy who'd learned that good things get taken away if you look too pleased about them. When he got to the honey cakes, he stopped, and held one in his palm for a long time.

"He told me about these," he said quietly. "He said nobody in the world made them like you."

Mother Yusa turned round to stir a pot that didn't need stirring, and her big shoulders shook for a while.

---

She hid us behind the oil jars in the back storeroom, and she told us about the palace.

The king did not eat. That was the first thing she said, as if it were the worst. Since the news of Soren's death, King Absalon had sent back every plate, and the only person he would listen to was his vizier.

"Orrin," said Zayn.

She spat into the fire. I liked her very much at that moment.

"Grey eyes. Soft voice. You never hear him coming, that one. He's brought the king Soren's ring. Stood up in the throne hall and said he'd taken it back from the Prince-Killer's own hand, in some oasis town, before the boy slipped away from him. Proof, he called it. Proof the glowing boy robbed our Soren's body." She looked at Zayn sideways. "The king wears it on a chain round his neck now. Over his heart."

Zayn's hand had gone to the hilt.

"They've painted Soren's name on the shrine wall in the palace court," she said, more quietly. "Under the traitors. Orrin's idea. And the little one, Idan, he goes and sits in front of it every night at sunset, when the whole city's barring its doors, and he doesn't say a word."

Then she asked the question I'd been dreading since she picked up the cleaver.

"How did he die? Our Soren. Did it… did it hurt him?"

Zayn opened his mouth and closed it again. He didn't know. That was the truth, and it had been cutting him every night for a turn of the Ash.

"I wasn't there," he said. "I should have been."

My tail glowed. I looked at the floor.

Mother Yusa looked at me. I don't know what she saw. Something in the way I sat, perhaps. She was a woman who had spent thirty years knowing which kitchen boy had broken a jar just by the set of his shoulders. She didn't ask me anything.

I was grateful. I was ashamed of being grateful.

I'll tell you about that night soon, little one. Not yet. Let me be a coward a little longer.

---

Idan came down after midnight.

We heard bare feet on the back stairs, and then the sound of someone very carefully lifting the lid off the honey-cake jar, in exactly the way someone else had probably taught him to.

Zayn stood up behind the oil jars. The mark on his chest glowed.

The boy froze with a cake halfway to his mouth. He was ten, small for it, with his brother's dark eyes and none of his brother's swagger. He stared at the glow, and at the hilt over Zayn's shoulder, and the cake fell out of his hand.

"That's Soren's," he whispered.

"Yes."

"They said you killed him."

"I didn't."

Gold, steady and warm, from the tip of my tail. Idan looked at it.

"Your brother tried to eat a scorpion once," I told him. "Was sick for a whole day. Turned the colour of a lizard. I've never been prouder of anyone."

I don't know why I said it. I'm not good with children. But Idan made a small, cracked, astonished sound, and it took me a moment to understand it was a laugh, probably the first in a turn of the Ash.

Then he sat down on the floor and cried, and Zayn sat down beside him. Zayn didn't hug him. He didn't know how, yet. He just sat close enough that their shoulders touched, the way you'd sit beside someone at a fire to share the warmth.

It was the right thing. It's almost always the right thing.

Later, Zayn twisted the pommel of the sword and took out the letter. It was creased soft from handling, and the broken grey wax still held the little curling mark of Orrin's seal.

"You can read?" Idan asked.

"Your brother taught me. In the sand. With a stick." Zayn touched the letters one by one, as if they were still warm. "He was a bad teacher. He kept laughing."

Idan read the letter by the light of Zayn's mark. I watched his face change halfway down. *On the Night of Embers, when every lantern in Veimore is lit, the king will die.* And again a line later. *When he falls, put out the lanterns. Then the city.*

"The Night of Embers is tomorrow," Idan said. "And the Ash will be full."

Zayn touched his mark. I watched his fingers press it, once, the way you press a bruise.

"Father hears petitions in the morning," Idan said. He swallowed. "I could take you in."

---

The palace of Veimore was a fortress of black stone and red, and the throne hall at its heart had no windows at all. It was lit only by lanterns, hundreds of them, hanging on long chains from a ceiling so high and so dark that they looked like Embers in a starless sky. The air was thick with their smoke. Every sound in that hall went up into the shadows and came back softer, as though the dark were listening and repeating it to itself.

At the far end, seven steps rose to a chair of black rock, and in it sat the saddest man I have ever seen.

King Absalon had been tall and broad once. You could see the shape of it, the way you can see the shape of a house after the roof has fallen in. On a thin gold chain around his neck hung a heavy ring, and every few moments his hand drifted up to touch it, the same way Zayn touched his mark.

One step below the throne stood Orrin.

Silver hair, smooth face, soft robes the colour of sand. His faded grey eyes found Zayn the instant the boy stepped out from behind Idan, and something happened in his face that I didn't understand until later.

He smiled.

Not the wide smile of a man who's won. A small one. The smile of a man who sets a trap for a fox and comes back in the morning to find the fox exactly where he expected.

"The Prince-Killer!" someone shouted, and then everyone was shouting. Spears came down. A courtier shrieked "*Monster!*" in a voice like a goose, and I saw Zayn's jaw tighten, and the gold on his skin flicker grey for a single breath.

"*Stop!*" Idan had planted himself in front of Zayn with his arms out. He was shaking and he didn't move. "Father, he has something for you. You have to *listen*."

The king looked at his son for a long moment. Then he lifted one tired hand, and the spears went up.

So Zayn walked the long red floor with the whole court staring, climbed to the bottom step of the throne, and read the letter aloud. Every word. His voice didn't shake. Soren had made him practise until it didn't.

When he finished, the hall was so quiet I could hear the lanterns hissing.

"That is Vizier Orrin's seal," said Zayn. "Soren took this from one of the Unnamed and hid it in his sword. They killed him before he could bring it home."

The king took the letter and looked at the seal for a long time. Then he looked at Orrin.

And Orrin sighed, very gently, like a teacher whose favourite pupil has made a small mistake. When he spoke, his voice filled the shadows of that hall the way smoke fills a room.

"Your Majesty will remember that I reported my seal stolen on the night of the prince's exile, and had a new one cut. It is written in the palace records." He spread his soft hands. "I did not wish to believe Soren had taken it. But here it is. A forgery in a broken sword, carried by the boy who killed him, on the very eve of the Night of Embers. It is clever. I'll grant them that."

You see? Long before Soren ever found that letter, Orrin had made sure that if it was found, it would look like a lie.

"Did you kill Soren?" said Zayn.

It came out of him the way the Light did, without permission.

Orrin looked down at him, soft and patient and sad. "I loved that boy," he said, "as if he were my own son."

From the cord at Zayn's belt, Hesset's little bronze truth bell rang.

It wasn't loud. In that hall it sounded like a stone dropped down a well. Every head turned.

"A desert trinket," said Orrin, without looking at it. "Bells ring when boys shake them, Majesty."

"He didn't shake it," said Idan.

"I didn't kill your son," Zayn said to the king, very low. "I left him alone that night. We argued, and I walked away, and when I came back he was gone. That's the worst thing I've ever done. But I didn't kill him."

The mark on his chest didn't burn. My tail blazed gold, so bright that the courtiers nearest us shaded their eyes.

I watched the king waver. I watched it happen in his face like a dune shifting in the night. Believing us meant believing he had exiled his son for a lie, and mourned him for a lie, and worn his ring over his heart because the man who killed him had handed it to him. No father can look straight at a thing like that. Not all at once.

"Hold the boy," King Absalon said at last. "In the east tower, under guard. After the Night of Embers, I will hear all of this again. From both of you." He looked at Orrin. "Both."

"Of course, Majesty," said Orrin, and bowed. "Shall we cancel the festival? For safety?"

The king closed his eyes. "No. The people need their lanterns."

As the guards took Zayn's arms, I saw the vizier's face, just for an instant, when he thought no one was watching.

He was still smiling.

---

The tower room was small and round and had a window that looked east. They had taken the sword and the bell. They hadn't taken me, because nobody had quite worked out how you arrest a fox, and I wasn't going to help them.

The Fire went down. All over the city, doors were being barred. I could hear them, hundreds of wooden bolts sliding home one after another, like a great slow clock. Then the whispering began, rising from every rooftop and courtyard: the people of Veimore reading the names of their dead off the walls, as they did every night, so the darkness wouldn't take them.

The Ash came up over the dunes. One more night and it would be perfectly round.

There was a soft sound at the door. Not a guard. Guards stamp.

Orrin's face appeared at the little barred window in the wood. In the dark, his eyes looked almost white.

"Thank you," he said gently, "for coming. I've wondered for a turn of the Ash how to bring you inside these walls on the Night of Embers, under the full Ash. And you came by yourself, through the kitchen drain, with a letter in your hand." He sounded almost fond. "The desert really does write the sands, doesn't it?"

Then, even more softly: "Tomorrow night, the lanterns go out."

My tail glowed bright gold in that dark little room, and Zayn saw it, and I saw him understand. Everything Orrin had said was true. That was what made it so terrible.

The vizier's footsteps went away down the stair.

Zayn went to the window, and I hopped up onto the sill beside him. Below us, the city lay under its brown haze of smoke, ten thousand small flames holding back the dark. And on the roof opposite, just at the edge of the lantern-glow, a figure in a grey robe crouched with two grey blades across his knees. His face was a smooth white mask with a crack running down the middle, from brow to chin.

He was looking up at our window.

And very faintly, under the whispering of all those names, so faint I might have been the only one in the world who could hear it, he was humming.

---

*End of Chapter Seventeen*


---

# Chapter Eighteen
## The Night of Embers

> *On the Night of Embers, every lantern is a name.*
> — painted above the shrine gate of the palace court, Veimore

---

Once a year, on the night the Ash comes up perfectly round, the whole desert lights lanterns for the lost bearers.

You'll see it soon enough. In the small villages it's a few clay lamps on doorsteps. Children are told the old story, old men cry a little, and everyone goes to bed feeling vaguely holy.

In Veimore, which already had more lanterns than any city in the world, they did it the Veimore way. Every family carried one extra lantern through the streets at dusk to the shrine walls where the names of the dead were painted, and hung it there, so that for one night the names glowed. And on the great black stairs of the palace, eleven bronze lanterns of coloured glass were set out in a row. One for each bearer who had come before.

I sat on the sill of the tower window and watched them being lit, one after another, by a priest with a long taper.

The Shepherd. The Weaver's Daughter. The Smith. The Twin Who Stayed. The Last King.

I knew every one of them. I knew which ones had been afraid of the dark, and which ones had laughed too loud, and which ones had cried for their mothers on the last night. Nobody in that city knew a single one of their names. That's the joke, you see. A festival of lanterns for the remembered, and not one person down there could have told you who they were remembering.

The Stone Singer. The Boy of Salt. The Falconer. The Blind One. The Dreamer. The Runner.

Eleven.

Behind me, Zayn was sitting against the wall with his eyes closed. He wasn't asleep. The mark on his chest was glowing steadily, the brightest I had ever seen it, as though it knew what night it was.

"How many?" he asked.

"Eleven," I said.

He was quiet for a moment.

"There should be another one," he said. "Tomorrow."

I didn't say anything. I didn't have to. My tail said it for me.

---

The first lantern went out just after the Ash cleared the walls.

I saw it happen. It was on the far western wall, one tiny flame among thousands, and it simply wasn't there anymore. Then the one beside it. Then the next. A gap of darkness opened along the top of the wall, small at first, then spreading, like a black thread being pulled through a golden cloth.

Down in the city, the whispering stopped.

You must understand how frightening that was. Every night of their lives, the people of Veimore had read the names of their dead into the dark to keep it away. Now, all at once, they stopped. Every one of them, on every rooftop, turned west to look at the black gap growing in the wall. Ten thousand people holding their breath.

Then somebody screamed, and the lanterns on the southern wall went out all at once.

"Kiri," said Zayn. He was on his feet.

"I see it."

There were shapes on the walls. Grey robes, white masks. The Unnamed were walking along the battlements in a line, and as they passed each lantern they cupped a hand over it, almost gently, and it died. They weren't hurrying. They didn't have to.

And behind them, where the light died, the grey came in.

It poured over the top of the walls like fog over a cliff. It slid down into the streets. Where it touched the shrine walls, the painted names of the dead faded and vanished, one after another, as though someone were wiping them off with a wet cloth. Some of those names had been there for a hundred years. They were gone in a breath.

Then the grates in the streets began to lift.

The iron grates that led down into the catacombs. All over the city, they rose, and out of them came figures. Grey figures, white-eyed, silent. They'd come up through the old water channels in the dark, under the walls, through the endless rows of the dead.

The people of Veimore saw them climbing out of the catacombs and thought their dead were rising.

That was when the real screaming started.

---

Someone was at the door.

It wasn't a guard. There had been two guards on the stair, and I'd heard nothing from them for some time, which was not a good sign. The bolt slid back. The door swung open.

Brother Seven stood in the doorway.

Up close the crack in his mask was wider than I'd thought. It ran from his brow down to his jaw, and through it I could see a thin line of his real face, the edge of one pale eye. He had Soren's sword in one hand and Hesset's bell in the other.

He held them out.

Zayn didn't move. Neither of them did. They stood a pace apart, two brothers who'd been torn from each other on the night one of them was born, and the only sound was the screaming far below.

"Tamir," said Zayn.

The figure flinched as if he'd been struck. The sword and the bell clattered to the floor. He took a step back, then another, and his hands came up toward his mask as if he meant to tear it off, and then stopped, shaking.

He hummed two notes. Just two. The beginning of the phoenix song.

Then he turned and ran, and his footsteps went away down the dark stair.

Zayn picked up the sword. His hands were trembling.

"He's still in there," he said.

"Yes," I said. My tail glowed. I had known who Brother Seven was since the first night he'd cornered Zayn in the dunes and let him go. I had said nothing for all that time. I'll put that on the pile with the rest.

---

The streets were full of grey.

We ran through them, down from the palace walls and into the lanes where the families had gone to hang their lanterns. Most of the lanterns were out. The ones still burning lay smashed on the stones, or swung from broken chains, throwing wild shadows. People ran past us in every direction. Doors were barred, and people pounded on them, and the doors stayed barred.

And everywhere, Husks.

They moved through the crowds slowly, reaching out, touching. Every touch, someone stopped running. Someone stood in the street with their hands at their sides and their eyes going blank and lost. A man dropped the lantern he was carrying and stared at it, as if he couldn't remember what it was for.

At a shrine wall halfway down the lane, a little girl was standing alone. She couldn't have been more than six. A Husk had touched her cheek, and the grey was spreading out from it across her face, and she was looking up at the wall where her family's names had been, where there was nothing now but bare red stone.

"Whose names were those?" she said to no one. "I can't… whose were they?"

Zayn was on his knees in front of her before I could say anything.

"You'll bring every Hound in the city down on us," I said.

"Yes," said Zayn, and put his hands on her face.

The light came gold. It always did, by then, when it was for someone else. It poured out of his palms and over her cheek, and the grey shrank back from it like frost from the Fire. Her eyes cleared. Behind her, on the wall, a single name glowed faint and gold for a moment where the paint had been. *Mira.* Her grandmother. Then it faded again. But the girl remembered it. I could see her remember.

And Zayn saw.

He always saw, when he healed. Her past: a woman's wrinkled hands braiding her hair. Her future: a grown girl lighting a lantern on this same wall, many years from now, and reading the names aloud. And then his own death, the way it always came, crashing in after the rest.

It was longer now. Much longer. The full Ash, so close he could touch it. The Crown Dune. A small boy's hand slipping out of his. His father on his knees in the sand. Grey hands reaching into the gold. And shapes in the sand far below, letters, a word written across the dunes. He could almost read it. Almost.

He came back gasping. The golden cracks had spread along his jaw.

The little girl's mother found her and pulled her away into the dark without a word. Zayn didn't notice. He was staring at nothing.

"I saw a word," he said. "In the sand."

"Later," I said, because across the lane, three Hounds had come out of the grey with their ember eyes fixed on him, and their lips were peeling back.

---

They never reached us.

A spear came out of the dark and took the first one through the chest, and it burst into black sand. The pale camel came down the lane behind it like a ghost, and on its back was Daruun, his veil torn half away, his long hunting spear already swinging for the second Hound. He'd come in through a gate the Unnamed had left open behind them. Of course he had. He was always going to come.

"*The palace!*" he shouted. "They're inside! Go!"

The third Hound sprang at Zayn's throat.

It met me instead.

I don't like to talk about the other shape. You know that by now. But I'll tell you that it happened on that street, in front of the little girl's mother and a dozen of the people of Veimore, and I didn't care who saw. I broke out of my small fox body like a coal bursting out of a fire. I was huge and black and burning, and my eyes were embers, and the silver half of me that belongs to the Hollow howled with joy. The Hound came apart in my jaws like smoke.

The old burn on my side, where Zayn's light had caught me in the storm, tore open again. It hurt more than I'll tell you.

Behind us, the grey was rolling down the lane like a wave, full of Husks.

"Go," I said to Zayn. My voice was enormous. It shook the shutters. "I'll hold them. *Go.*"

He looked at me. He looked at the monster I was, the thing I'd hidden from him and from every bearer before him.

He wasn't afraid of me. He hadn't been for a long time. I hadn't noticed when it changed.

"Don't die," he said.

"I'm a great deal harder to kill than you," I said. "That's sort of the whole problem."

He ran.

---

The palace stairs were black stone, wide enough for twenty men to walk abreast, and they rose in one long climb from the court to the great doors. The eleven bronze lanterns stood along them. Seven had gone out. The other four were guttering, throwing coloured light across the steps: red, then green, then gold.

Brother One was waiting at the top.

He was the largest man I've ever seen, and I had recently met Mother Yusa. He stood in front of the doors with his feet planted wide and a grey blade in his hands as long as Zayn was tall. His mask was smooth and white and perfect. He had never once, in all the time he'd guarded Orrin, been heard to speak.

He didn't speak now. He simply came down the stairs.

Zayn met him halfway.

The first blow nearly ended it. The grey blade came round in a long, flat sweep that would have taken Zayn's head off if he hadn't dropped flat onto the steps. It hit the stone where he'd been and threw sparks. The second came straight down. Zayn rolled, and the edge of it skimmed across his shoulder.

It didn't cut skin. Grey blades don't need to. But the light under his skin dimmed where it touched him, and for a horrible moment he couldn't remember Soren's face.

He knew he'd had a friend. He knew the friend had been important. But when he reached for the face, there was only a blank grey space where it should have been.

He lay on the stairs with the giant above him, and he couldn't remember.

And then, out of the grey, a voice. Not the face. Just the voice, laughing at him by a campfire somewhere a long way back.

*A short blade means you have to get close. Most people are afraid to get close. So be the one who isn't.*

Soren had told him that on the third night, while he waved half a sword around and showed off. Zayn had rolled his eyes. He remembered rolling his eyes. And with that, the face came back, all at once: the scarred jaw, the crooked grin, the dark eyes always laughing at something. *There* you are.

Zayn got up.

Brother One swung again, a great flat sweep meant to keep him away. And Zayn didn't go away. He went *in*. He ducked under the swing and stepped right up inside it, so close to the giant that the long grey blade was useless, so close he could hear the man breathing behind his mask.

He raised Soren's broken sword.

And the light came.

It went down his arm and into the hilt and along the broken blade, and where the steel ended, it kept going. For one heartbeat Soren's sword was whole again. The missing half was made of gold, bright as the Fire, and it met the grey blade where the hilt joined the steel.

The grey blade shattered.

The pieces fell down the black stairs, ringing like bells. Brother One stumbled back. His heel caught on the edge of a step, and he fell, a long way, all the way down to the bottom, where he lay still beside the last of the guttering lanterns.

His mask had cracked in half. Under it was the face of a tired grey-bearded man with his eyes closed. He was breathing.

Zayn stood on the stairs with the golden half-blade fading in his hand, and his chest heaving.

He hadn't killed him. He hadn't even thought about it. I don't think it had occurred to him that he could.

Then, from inside the throne hall, a child screamed.

---

The great doors stood open, and inside, the dark had won.

All those hundreds of lanterns on their long chains had gone out but one. It hung high above the throne, a single small flame swinging slowly, and by its light I could see the shapes of grey robes and white masks standing in rows along the walls. Unnamed. Dozens of them. And at the top of the seven steps, beside the black stone throne, Orrin.

He had Idan.

He held the boy against his chest with one arm, quite gently, the way you'd hold a sleepy child. In his other hand was a grey blade, small and thin as a reed.

And on the steps below him lay the king.

Zayn ran to him. Nobody stopped him. That was the worst part. The masks along the walls just watched, and Orrin watched, and let him go.

King Absalon lay on his back on the black steps with one hand still stretched out toward his son. There was no blood. Grey blades don't do blood. But the grey was spreading out from his chest like spilled ink. His eyes were open. They found Zayn's face.

"He stepped in front," said Orrin softly, from above. "The blade was meant for the boy, to make him easier to carry. His Majesty stepped in front of it. I confess I didn't expect that. He hasn't moved quickly in years."

Zayn put his hands on the king's chest.

The light came, gold, and the grey drank it. That's what a grey blade does. It cuts the Light. It was like pouring water into sand. He pushed harder, and harder, until the cracks on his own skin blazed and spread down his arms, and the king's eyes cleared for a moment.

And Zayn saw. The king, young, laughing, swinging a small dark-haired boy up onto his shoulders. The same boy, older, being dragged out of this hall in chains while the king sat on his throne and didn't look up. And then no future at all. Just dark.

And then his own death again. The Ash. The gold. The word in the sand.

"Soren," the king whispered. "I didn't listen to him. I should have… listened."

"I know," said Zayn.

"Tell him." The king's hand fumbled at the chain around his neck and found the ring. He pulled, and the old chain broke, and he pressed the ring into Zayn's palm. "Tell him I'm sorry. And give this to Idan. When he's king."

And Zayn said, "I'll tell him."

My tail glowed.

I was in the doorway by then, small again, limping, bleeding from the old burn. I saw my tail light up bright gold in that dark hall, and I understood what it meant. The boy couldn't lie. He believed, with his whole heart, that before the night was over he would see Soren and be able to tell him.

The king's eyes went past Zayn to his younger son, held in Orrin's arm at the top of the steps. He smiled at Idan, a small, broken, loving smile.

Then he was gone.

---

"How touching," said Orrin.

High above the throne, the last lantern in the hall went out.

In the dark, Zayn's light was the only light. It came off him in waves, and it wasn't gold anymore. It had gone grey, dim and flickering, because he was angrier than I had ever seen him, angry enough to tear that hall down stone by stone.

That was what Orrin wanted. I knew it the moment I saw it. The light always goes grey when the bearer hates.

"*Zayn,*" I said. "*Don't.*"

He heard me. I watched him hear me. He shut his eyes and breathed, and slowly the grey went out of him. Not all the way. Enough.

When he opened his eyes, Orrin was already at the back of the hall, by the open door to the eastern balconies, with Idan under one arm. Beyond him I could hear the hiss and stamp of sand lizards in the court below, and the howling of Hounds.

Idan was crying. He wasn't screaming anymore. He'd stopped screaming when his father died.

"The Crown Dune, bearer," said Orrin. His soft voice filled the dark the way it always had. "Under the full Ash. You know the way. You've seen it every time you've closed your eyes." He looked down at Idan, almost kindly. "Come and give me your name. Or the boy will give me his."

Then he was gone, and the masks along the walls went with him like smoke.

By the time we reached the balcony, the lizards were already out through the eastern gate and running into the desert, a dark knot of riders with the Hounds loping around them. Orrin rode at the front with Idan in front of him. And at the very back, a lone rider in grey with twin blades on his back turned in his saddle and looked up at the palace.

He looked for a long time. Then he turned and rode after the others.

---

We stood on the eastern balcony of the palace of Veimore, the boy and the fox, and a moment later Daruun came up the stairs behind us with his spear in his hand and grey dust on his clothes. Below us the city was dark. Every lantern was out. In the streets, the Husks had stopped moving. They stood in the lanes like statues, all of them, every white face turned the same way.

East.

We turned to look where they were looking.

Yesterday, from this balcony, you would have seen nothing but a sea of low gold dunes going away to the edge of the world. That's what the desert out there had always been.

The desert had moved in the night.

It stood a little way out from the walls of the city, so close you could have walked there before the Ash went down. A dune, higher than any dune in the world. Higher than the mountains where Daruun had hidden for fifteen years. It rose out of the flat sand in one long, impossible curve, and at its very top, white and enormous and perfectly round, sat the full Ash.

The Crown Dune.

It had come to fetch him.

Daruun made a sound beside me that I have heard only a few times in all my long life. It's the sound a father makes when he understands that he can't stop what is coming.

Zayn looked down at the ring in his hand. Soren's ring. He closed his fingers round it, very gently, and put it on the cord at his neck, beside the infinity knot.

"It's time," he said.

Neither of us answered him. There was nothing left that was true and kind at the same time.

---

*End of Chapter Eighteen*

# Chapter Nineteen
## The Crown Dune

> *He'll walk where the dunes write the words of the sky,*
> *And the light will be born on the day he will die.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born

---

Mother Yusa found us on the palace stairs as we were leaving.

She'd come up out of the kitchens with a ladle in one hand like a club, flour and grey dust in her hair, and she saw the king's ring on the cord at Zayn's neck and understood everything at once. She didn't cry. She didn't ask. She pushed a small cloth bundle into his hands, and then she took his face between her two enormous palms and kissed his forehead, hard, the way you'd stamp a seal into wax.

Then she went back down into the dark to look for Idan's nurse, and I never saw her again.

Inside the bundle were dates. Rolled in honey.

---

We rode out of Veimore through the eastern gate, the three of us on the pale camel, Daruun in front and Zayn behind him and me in the boy's lap, bleeding quietly into his shirt.

The streets were silent behind us. The Husks still stood where they had stopped, all of them facing east. We rode right past them. They didn't reach for us. They didn't even turn their heads. They were waiting, the way the whole desert was waiting.

Zayn unwrapped the bundle and held the dates in his palm for a long time.

He'd always saved them. You remember. Ever since he was a small boy in a patched tent, he'd saved dates and honey for the hardest days, as if you could store sweetness the way you store water.

He ate one. Then he held one out over Daruun's shoulder, and after a moment his father took it. Then he gave one to me, and I ate it, though I prefer scorpions and always will.

"This is the hardest day," Zayn said. "I think. I don't think there's a harder one."

My tail glowed.

They rode in silence a while. The Crown Dune rose ahead of us, higher with every step, until it filled half the sky.

"Father," said Zayn.

It was the first time he'd called him that. I felt Daruun go rigid in the saddle.

"I forgive you."

Daruun didn't turn round. His shoulders shook. "You can't. I ran. If I hadn't run, none of this—your brother, the Hollow, all of it. You can't forgive that."

"I just did," said Zayn. "And I can't lie. So."

My tail lit up so brightly that it threw our shadow out across the sand ahead of us, long and gold.

Daruun made a sound I won't describe. He reached back with one hand without looking, and Zayn took it, and they rode like that, holding on, all the way to the foot of the dune.

---

The Husks were waiting there.

Not just the ones from Veimore. All of them. They had come from the whole desert in the night, from the grey wastes and the emptied villages, from Harrow's End and the caravan roads and the oasis towns, and they stood in a ring around the foot of the Crown Dune a hundred deep. Herders. Traders. Old women. Children. Thousands of white, blank faces, all turned up toward the top of the dune.

They parted for us like water.

We climbed. The camel couldn't, so we left it at the bottom. The sand slid under our feet with every step, and Zayn went first, and Daruun came behind with his spear, and I came last, limping. Halfway up, Zayn turned and picked me up without a word and carried me the rest of the way, the way he had carried me away from the buried ship. I let him. I was too tired to be proud.

And then we were at the top, and I looked up, and it was the vision.

I had seen it in his eyes every time he healed. Twelve times. Twenty. I had stopped counting. And here it was, every piece of it falling into place like the last stones of a wall.

The Ash hung full and white above the Crown Dune, so close it looked like you could reach up and smudge it with your thumb. The top of the dune was a long, flat crest of pale sand, cold under our feet. Below us, on every side, the desert went away silver and grey to the edge of the world.

Orrin was waiting at the far end of the crest, with Idan.

Around him stood the Unnamed in their grey robes, perhaps twenty of them, their white masks shining like small moons. And a little apart from the others, his twin blades in his hands, stood Brother Seven, with the crack in his mask grown so wide now that half his real face showed through it.

And between us and them, the grey was gathering.

---

It rose out of the sand like smoke from a fire that had been put out with water. It thickened. It took a shape.

A boy.

A small boy, smaller than Zayn, barefoot and thin, in a shepherd's tunic. He had a shepherd's crook in one hand. Everything about him was grey: his skin, his hair, his ragged clothes. Only his eyes were different. They were black, completely black, like two holes cut into the night.

I knew that face. I had known it before there were cities in this desert. I had sat beside that face at a thousand fires, when it was brown and laughing and smelled of sheep.

"Hello, Malek," I said.

"Hello, little fox." His voice was a child's voice, and under it, a thousand others. "You've come to watch another one burn."

"I've come to watch one *choose*. It's different. You'd know, if you'd done it."

The grey boy smiled at me. It was the worst thing I have ever seen.

Then he turned his black eyes on Zayn.

"Look at them," Malek said softly. He pointed his crook down the dune at the ring of Husks. "Look. Those are the people you're going to die for. Do you see that one? He threw stones at you when you were six. Those ones drove you out of your village. Those ones called you monster, and spat on you, and watched you starve. And you'd burn for them?"

Zayn's light flickered. It went grey at the edges.

"And after," said Malek, "they'll light a lantern. One more lantern, in a row of eleven. And in a hundred years, no one will remember what it was for. No one remembers the bearers. They didn't remember mine. You'll be forgotten." His voice dropped to a whisper, gentle as sand. "You'll be forgotten, Zayn. Just like you always were."

My tail went dark. Pitch dark, cold as a dead coal.

But I could see the boy's face. And I could see that it didn't matter what my tail said, because it was the oldest fear he had. It was the fear he'd been carrying since he was a baby crying in the sand. *Nobody will remember me.*

His light went grey all the way through.

"There's a way out," said Orrin.

He stepped forward with one hand on Idan's shoulder and the thin grey blade in the other. His soft voice carried along the crest the way it had always filled the throne hall.

"Give me your name," he said. "That's all. Say it to the wind. Your true name, the one in your blood. Malek will take it, and the Light with it, and then it's over. No burning. No pain. You'll simply forget. And then everyone will forget, everyone in the whole desert, every loss, every grief, every dead son." Something moved in his faded eyes. "I promise you, child, it doesn't hurt. I've done it. I burned my own name to stop remembering. Forgetting is the only mercy there is."

My tail flickered. Not dark. Not bright. Somewhere in between.

He believed it. That's the worst kind of lie, little one: the kind the liar has told himself so long it sounds like truth.

"Give me your name," said Orrin.

And Zayn, grey and shaking, opened his mouth.

---

Bells.

Small bronze bells, a great many of them, ringing very faintly somewhere below us on the slope.

Every head turned.

Someone was climbing the Crown Dune. Slowly. On hands and knees, through the sliding sand. He had a stick, but he wasn't using it any more. He was simply crawling, one hand and then the other, and around his waist hung a belt of tiny truth bells that chimed with every movement.

It was Old Hesset.

He should not have been there. You know that. He was bound to the edge of the desert, to Harrow's End, and had been for three hundred years, the price of the night he ran. To leave was to break the bond. To break the bond was to die.

He had walked across the whole Destiny Desert anyway.

He was dying as he climbed. I could see it. Every step had taken years off what little he had left. His beard was thin as smoke. His hands were like dry leaves. His milky blind eyes were fixed on nothing, and he climbed toward the sound of our voices as if they were a fire on a cold night.

He reached the crest and knelt there, gasping. Malek turned to look at him.

"Hesset," said the grey boy, in a warm voice. "My old friend."

Every bell on Hesset's belt rang at once.

"I was never your friend," said Hesset. "And you were never mine. You only told me what I wanted to hear, so I'd run. I've had three hundred years in the dark to work that out." He turned his blind face toward Zayn. "Boy. Are you there?"

"I'm here," said Zayn.

"I asked you a question once, in my tent. *Would you die for them?* You couldn't answer." The old man swallowed. "I never told you that someone asked me the same question, on this sand, three hundred years ago. I never told you what I answered. I said no. I said no, and I ran, and the last thing I ever saw was the top of this dune. I've been in the dark ever since." His voice broke. "I hid it from you. I hid that bearers die, because I was ashamed. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, child."

Not a single bell rang.

"Don't answer for me," whispered Hesset. "Don't answer for them. Answer for you."

Zayn stood very still on the top of the Crown Dune.

He looked at Malek. He looked down at the thousands of Husks, and somewhere among them, maybe, was a boy who'd thrown a stone at him once. He looked at Idan in Orrin's grip. He looked at his father. He looked at me.

Then he untied Hesset's little truth bell from his belt and held it up in front of him, so everyone could see.

"Yes," he said.

The bell didn't ring.

And the grey went out of his light like a shadow when the Fire comes up. It turned gold. Not the gold I'd seen in Harrow's End, or in the City of Glass, or even on the stairs of Veimore. A deeper gold, steady and warm, the gold of honey held up to the light.

Malek took a step backward.

---

"Brother Seven," said Orrin sharply. "Cut him."

Tamir raised his twin grey blades.

They were the only things in the world that could cut the Light. He stood between Zayn and Orrin, and his blades were shaking, and through the great crack in his mask I could see one pale eye, wet and wide.

He was humming. He didn't know he was doing it.

Zayn walked toward him.

He didn't raise his sword. He didn't hurry. He walked straight up to his brother until the grey blades were a finger's width from his chest, and he looked into that one pale eye through the crack.

"You held me," he said. "When I was born. I saw it. You were five, and you held me, and you were scared you'd drop me."

The blades trembled.

Then, slowly, Tamir lowered them.

Zayn walked past him to Orrin. The vizier backed away, dragging Idan with him, and raised the thin grey blade to the boy's throat. Zayn didn't stop. He put his hand over Orrin's hand, over the grip of the knife, and the gold went into it.

Orrin cried out and let go. His hand was grey to the wrist where the Light had touched it, the grey of something that had stolen too much for too long. The blade fell into the sand.

Idan ran.

He ran straight past Zayn to Daruun, and Daruun caught him, and held him, and the boy buried his face in the grey wrappings and shook. After a moment Zayn came and knelt in front of him. He took the cord from his own neck, untied Soren's ring from beside the infinity knot, and pressed it into Idan's hand and closed the small fingers over it.

"Your father wanted you to have this. And so would Soren."

Idan looked at him. He was ten. He wasn't stupid.

"Are you going to die?" he said.

"Yes."

"Like Soren?"

Zayn thought about that. "No," he said. "Soren didn't get to choose. I do."

---

Daruun let go of Idan, and took his son's hands.

"Let me," he said.

His voice was not the voice of a warrior or a hunter, or of the Veiled Rider who had saved us three times out of the dark. It was the voice of a man begging.

"Let me do it. I was meant to. It was mine, before it was yours, and I ran. Let me pay for it. *Please.* I ran so you could live. Don't make that for nothing. Please, son. Not you. Not you."

Zayn shook his head. He couldn't speak for a moment.

"It wasn't for nothing," he said finally. "You ran, and you left me in the sand, and the desert kept me. And I grew up alone, so I know what it's like to be the one nobody wants. That's why I can do this. That's why it's me." He tightened his grip. "It all meant something. Every bit of it. Even the bad parts. *Especially* the bad parts."

My tail was so bright I couldn't look at it.

Daruun bowed his head over their joined hands. His whole body shook. I have seen men face Hounds and Veilbeasts and armies, and I have never seen anything as brave as what that man did next.

He let go.

He didn't do it all at once. He couldn't. He did it one finger at a time, slowly, like a man lowering something precious into deep water. And when the last finger slid away, he stepped back, and stood beside Idan and Hesset, and didn't move again.

The hardest choice of his life. He made it.

---

Zayn turned to me last.

I was sitting in the sand, too tired to stand, my fur dark with my own blood, my silver eye aching. He knelt down so our faces were level.

"You lied to me once," he said.

"I know."

"Tell me the truth now. Will it hurt?"

"Yes," I said. My tail glowed.

"Will it matter?"

"Yes."

The glow of it lit his whole face. He looked at it for a long moment, and then he smiled, the small, crooked, rusty smile that he never wasted, and he put his forehead against mine.

"Look after them," he said.

I couldn't answer. For the first time in all my long life, I couldn't think of a single word.

He stood up. He walked out to the very end of the crest, where the dune fell away on every side into the silver dark, and stood there under the full Ash, with his back to all of us.

"I always wanted to be a hero," he said quietly. Not to us. To himself, maybe, or to the boy in the patched tent. "Like in the stories."

He took a breath.

"I am the hero."

It wasn't a boast. It was simply true. My tail had never burned so bright.

Then he lifted his face to the Ash, and to whatever was beyond it, and he said the last words he would ever say.

"I'm coming, Soren."

---

And here I have to tell it the way I told it at the very beginning, because I have seen this moment so many times since that it has never stopped happening. It's happening now. It's always happening, somewhere behind my eyes.

He is burning.

He stands at the top of the Crown Dune with his arms open, as if he's about to catch something falling from the sky. The fire doesn't start at his hands. It starts in his chest, right where the mark is, the small golden feather that has glowed in the dark his whole life. It cracks open like a seed. Light pours through the cracks in his skin, through every golden line that every healing left on him. It runs down his arms and up his throat and out of his eyes.

And Orrin lunges.

I see him come. Grey robes, grey hand, grey eyes wide and hungry. He's spent his whole life waiting for this light, and he throws himself into it with both hands outstretched, grabbing, as if he could tear it out of the boy and keep it for himself.

"*Mine,*" he says.

It isn't. It never could be. The Light can't be taken. It can only be given.

It turns grey in his hands. I watch it happen. The gold he's grasping goes dull, then ashen, and the grey runs up his fingers and his wrists and his arms like frost climbing a window. He looks down at himself. For a single moment, his face changes. I think he sees something in the stolen light: a small boy, perhaps, and a brother, and a name he burned a very long time ago. His mouth opens.

Then he crumbles. Not like flesh. Like an old grey statue that has stood in the wind too long. He comes apart into grey dust, and the wind lifts the dust off the top of the Crown Dune and carries it away into the dark, the way it carries away a name that has been spoken carelessly.

There's nothing left of him at all.

And the boy doesn't scream.

That's the part I always remember. He doesn't scream.

He looks down. Below him, all across the desert, the dunes are moving. Under the full Ash, in the silver dark, they're shifting and folding and rising, writing something across the sand in letters miles high. The same shapes they wrote for him on the first nights after he left Harrow's End, when he couldn't read a word and I wouldn't tell him what they said.

He can read them now.

A boy with a broken sword taught him, by a fire, with a stick in the sand, laughing the whole time.

*A*, the dunes write. *E. R. O. N.*

*Aeron.* In the old tongue, it means *the one who rises.*

It's his name. His true name, the one the Weaver wrote for him before he was born, the one the wind could never find, because the desert kept it safe for him all this time. And he reads it, the last thing he will ever read, and I see him smile.

Then he isn't looking at the dunes anymore. He's looking at something only he can see. But I see it too, just for a moment, in the light pouring out of him.

The Singing Wells, under a thin Ash. The water humming. And a young man sitting on the edge of the well with half a sword across his knees, a scarred jaw and a crooked grin, laughing. Laughing at him, the way he always did. Holding out his hand.

*You took your time,* says Soren.

And Zayn laughs. The way he laughed the first time, by a fire, when a prince tried to eat a scorpion. And he reaches out and takes the hand.

The light keeps growing until it isn't a boy anymore. It's a shape, two great wings of gold folding out across the dark, wide enough to cover the dunes from one edge of the sky to the other.

Then it falls.

Gold rains down over the desert, and everywhere it lands, the grey goes away.

Somewhere below, someone is shouting a name.

It's his father. On his knees in the sand a little way down the crest, below the place where his son stands, his arms around a crying prince, his face turned up into the falling gold. Shouting it over and over, the only name he was ever allowed to call his son.

"*Zayn! Zayn!*"

And the wind takes it, gently, and carries it out over the whole desert. Not to the dark this time.

There isn't any dark left.

---

*End of Chapter Nineteen*

# Chapter Twenty
## Ash into Gold

> *Ash into gold and gold into flame —*
> *The end is the start, and the start is the same.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born

---

The gold fell all night.

It came down soft and slow, like the first warm rain after a long drought, and everywhere it touched, the grey let go.

I watched it happen from the top of the Crown Dune. I couldn't have moved if I'd wanted to. Down at the foot of the dune, the great ring of Husks stood with their faces turned up into the falling light, and one by one, the white went out of their eyes. Brown came back. Black. Green. Grey skin warmed to every colour skin can be. An old herder looked down at his own hands and began to cry. A woman spun round and round in the sand, calling a child's name, and somewhere in the crowd a child's voice called back.

Then, all at once, there was *sound*. Thousands of people, talking and weeping and laughing and shouting names. Names everywhere. The whole desert was saying them.

Far off, over the walls of Veimore, I saw a light come on. Then another. Someone was lighting the lanterns again. And on the shrine walls, though I couldn't see them from there, I know the names of the dead were coming back, one by one, in gold.

The grey went out of the desert like a tide going out. The colour of the sand came back, and the colour of the sky. And just before dawn, I saw it fade from the last place it had ever been.

The small grey shepherd boy, standing alone at the edge of the crest.

---

The gold fell on Malek too.

It fell on his grey hair and his grey hands and his grey crook, and he didn't run from it. He stood still and let it land on him, the way you'd let snow land on you if you had never seen snow.

And the grey came off him like dust.

Under it was a boy. Just a boy. Brown and thin and barefoot, with a shepherd's crook of ordinary wood and a scab on one knee and sheep's wool caught in his sleeve. His eyes weren't black anymore. They were brown, warm brown, the eyes I had looked into at a thousand fires before the world was old.

He looked at me.

"Kiri?" he said, in a small voice.

I couldn't speak. I've told you that's rare. It was the second time in one night.

He looked down at his own hands, turning them over as if he'd never seen them. He looked out at the desert going gold, and at the people below calling each other's names. Then he looked back at me, and his eyes filled.

"I remember my name," he said.

It was the first true thing he had said in longer than there have been cities. My tail lit up so bright it hurt.

"I know," I said, when I could. "I never forgot it. Not once."

He smiled. Then he lifted his face to the last of the falling gold, the way the boy had lifted his face to the Ash, and he came apart into light. Not grey dust. Light. It rose off the top of the Crown Dune and went away east toward the coming Fire, and he was gone.

I've hated him for more years than I can count. I'll miss him for longer.

---

Brother Seven was kneeling at the very end of the crest, where the boy had stood.

The sand there had turned gold. Not gold-coloured. *Gold*. You know the old stories: sand that holds a bearer's ash turns gold and never moves again. It lay in a small, perfect circle on the top of the highest dune in the world, glittering in the last of the Ashlight. The wind went across it and didn't move a single grain. In the middle of it lay Hesset's golden feather, the one that had been found with the baby in the sand. It was whole. Every barb.

Tamir had put down his blades. As I watched, the white mask on his face split along its crack and fell away in two halves into the sand.

Under it was a young man's face. Twenty years old, pale-eyed, thin. He had his father's jaw and his brother's light, watchful look, and tears were running down his face, and he didn't wipe them away.

He was remembering. All of it, all at once. I could see it. A tent with its walls bowing in the wind. A man singing. A newborn baby put into his arms on a night of storm and screaming, and his father's voice saying the baby's name, very softly, so the wind couldn't hear it. And then the masks, and the grey, and fifteen years of nothing.

He opened his mouth. He hadn't spoken a word of his own in fifteen years.

"Aeron," said Tamir.

Daruun turned round.

I'll leave that there. Some things don't belong to a fox to tell. But I'll say that he crossed the crest of the Crown Dune faster than I've ever seen a man move, and that the sound he made when he caught his elder son in his arms was the opposite of the sound he'd made on the balcony in Veimore. And that Tamir held on to him, and hummed, and this time he knew exactly what song it was.

Idan stood beside them with Soren's ring clutched in his fist, looking out across the desert.

"Kiri," he said. "Look."

I looked.

The Fire was coming up.

And below us, across the whole Destiny Desert, the dunes had turned gold in great sweeping lines, miles long, catching the first light. They weren't moving anymore. They never would again. They were letters. Five of them, written across the desert from one edge of the sky to the other, so large you could have read them from the walls of Veimore, so large you can read them still.

*AERON.*

---

Old Hesset was lying on his back in the sand.

He'd been there all night. I'd forgotten him. Everyone had. That shames me now. I limped over to him, and he turned his face toward the sound of me.

"Fox," he said. His voice was barely there. "Is it morning?"

"Nearly."

"I'd like to—"

Then he stopped. Because the Fire had come up over the edge of the world, and its first light fell across his face, across his milky blind eyes.

And the milk cleared out of them.

I watched it happen. They went clear and dark and wet, the eyes of a young man, and they *saw*. He looked up at the sky going pink and gold. He turned his head and looked out at the desert, at the great golden letters lying across the dunes, at the rising Fire.

The last thing he had seen, three hundred years before, was the top of this dune in the dark. The first thing he saw now was the dawn.

"Oh," said Hesset. "Oh, *look* at it."

He looked at it for a long time. I didn't interrupt.

"Did he forgive me?" he asked at last. "The boy?"

"He held up your bell," I said. "In front of everyone. He kept it the whole way."

Hesset smiled. It was the same smile I've never been able to describe, fond and sad at once, the way a man smiles at an old letter. Except this time there was more fond in it than sad.

"The sand remembers," he whispered.

Then he closed his eyes, and his debt was paid, and he was gone. The truth bells at his belt were silent.

We gave him to the dunes, on the slope of the Crown Dune, facing east, so he'd always have the dawn.

---

They went down at noon. Daruun, and Tamir, and Idan between them, back toward Veimore, where every lantern was burning again and the people were already painting a new name on the shrine wall of the palace court. Idan would be king. He was ten. He'd be a good one. He'd had good teachers, at the end.

Daruun asked me to come.

I said no.

I stayed with the gold. I sat beside that small, glittering circle on the top of the Crown Dune through the whole long afternoon and into the night, and I didn't eat, and I didn't sleep. There was nothing there to guard. The gold would never move. Nothing in the desert would ever touch it.

I guarded it anyway.

And when the Ash came up that night, just past full, I stood on the top of the highest dune in the world and I shouted at the sky.

"*Not this one!*"

My voice went out across the whole desert. I didn't care who heard.

"You hear me? Not this one! *Not again!* I have walked beside twelve of them. I have watched twelve of them stand on this sand. I have buried every one. And this one, *this* one, he never had anything. Nobody ever gave him a single thing he didn't have to scavenge for, and he gave you everything, and you *took* it—"

The Weaver didn't answer. It never does. It never forces anything. It only waits.

The wind came up, gentle, from the east, and moved across the top of the dune. It went over the golden circle and didn't stir a grain of it. Then it went over me, and smoothed down my fur, the way you'd smooth a frightened child's hair.

I lay down beside the gold and put my nose on my paws. And I cried, little one. Foxes do. I don't care what you've heard.

---

There's something I have to tell you now.

I've been putting it off this whole story. You'll have noticed. Every time Soren's death came up, I went quiet, or I changed the subject, or I made a joke about scorpions. You're not stupid. You're a great deal cleverer than you look, all pink and wrinkled like that.

I saw it happen.

The night of the full Ash, one turn before the end, the night they argued and Zayn walked away into the dark. I wasn't by the fire. I was out in the dunes, in my other shape, the monster shape. There were Hounds tracking the boy's light that night, a whole pack of them, and I was hunting them. I didn't want him to see me like that. Not after the storm, when his light had burned me. Not after the way he'd looked at me.

So I was out in the dark being a monster, and that's why I was too far away.

I heard voices from the camp. Soren's. And another one, soft, that I didn't know yet.

By the time I came over the ridge, it was almost done.

Orrin was kneeling by the fire. He'd come alone, in plain robes, with no mask. Soren had known him his whole life. The man who'd taught him his letters, perhaps, or carved him toy soldiers, or sat behind his father's throne every day he'd been alive. So when Orrin held out a flask and said *you must be thirsty, the boy has left you, come home*, Soren drank.

There was stolen ash in that water. Bearer's ash, taken from a grave. False light. That's why he went grey, little one. That's why the golden cracks.

I was a moment too late. One moment. If I'd been a little faster, if I'd let one Hound go, if I'd turned toward the camp at the first voice instead of the second—

Orrin saw me coming over the ridge, huge and black with my ember eyes, and he ran. He had Soren's ring in his hand. He'd taken it while the boy was still falling.

I reached Soren. He was already gone. His eyes were open. He'd fallen facing the way Zayn had walked.

I tried to close them. I had to shrink back into a fox even to try. A monster's paws are too big for something like that, and a fox's aren't made for it either. They wouldn't stay shut. In the morning the boy did it for me.

And then I said nothing.

I've told myself a hundred reasons. I had no proof. Who would believe a half-Hollow beast against the king's own vizier? If the boy had known the truth too early, before he was ready, he'd have gone after Orrin with hate in his heart, and his light would have gone grey, and the Hollow would have had him. All of that is true, more or less.

But it isn't *why*.

The why is that I was ashamed. Ashamed of what I'd been out there in the dark, and ashamed of being slow, and ashamed that the boy I loved most would have to know that I watched his only friend die and couldn't stop it.

So I let him carry it alone. For the rest of his life, he thought it was his fault for walking away. I watched him whisper *I'm sorry* into the dark a hundred times, to a friend who couldn't hear him, and I lay a few paces off with my eyes shut and pretended to be asleep.

I'd like to tell you he would have forgiven me. He would have. That's the worst of it. He'd have looked at me with those too-light eyes and said *it's all right, Kiri*, and his mark wouldn't have burned at all, because he'd have meant it. I didn't let him, because I didn't think I deserved it.

I still don't. But I've decided that's not how it works. A boy taught me that, on a camel, on the way to the end of the world.

That's my confession. I've never said it to anyone. I'm telling you because you're the only one I've never lied to.

Not yet, anyway. Give me time.

---

Twelve years went by. Desert years. Long ones.

The Hollow never came back. The villages it had swallowed filled up again, slowly, with people who had remembered their names. Harrow's End was rebuilt, clay house by clay house, around the same deep well. And there was a new festival on the Night of Embers now, or rather, the old one with a new lantern in it.

Auntie Sabba lit it.

She was very old by then, so old that she had to be helped to the edge of the village, to the patch of hard ground between the last house and the first dune where a patched tent had once stood. Nobody had built there since. Nobody would.

She held the taper in both shaking hands. The lantern was clay, plain and small, with a feather scratched into its side.

"For the boy," she said, "who I should have spoken up for." Her voice was thin as paper. "Zayn. Who was Aeron."

She lit it. And from it, one by one, the whole village lit theirs, until the edge of the desert was a line of small gold flames. Then they hung up the other eleven, the old ones, and stood in the dark and said the names. Not the titles. The names. I'd told them, by then. Every single one. I'd decided someone ought to know.

Bram lit his lantern last.

His brother Tobin, nineteen now and taller than him, carried the taper for him. Bram had grown into a broad, quiet man with a little early grey in his beard, who kept the well his father had kept. He never talked about the oasis town. He never forgot it, either. Every Night of Embers he stood at the twelfth lantern longer than anyone, until his wife came and took his hand.

Her name was Talia. She was round as the Ash that year, and laughing, and she didn't let him brood.

The baby came that same night, after the lanterns were lit, with the Embers all out over the desert. A boy. Small and furious and very loud.

It was Talia who saw it first. She was holding him against her in the dark of the little clay house, and she went still.

"Bram," she whispered. "Bram, look at his chest."

There, just under his collarbone, was a mark the colour of honey. Shaped like a feather. With fine lines in it like the barbs of a real one.

It was glowing.

Bram looked at it for a long time. Then he went down on his knees beside the bed, the way his father had once gone down on his knees at a well, and he cried like a boy. And when he could speak again, he didn't ask anyone. He already knew. It was written across the whole desert in letters miles high.

"Aeron," he said. "His name is Aeron."

My tail glowed. I was sitting in the doorway. I'd been sitting in that doorway since the Ash had turned nine times, as a matter of fact. Talia had stopped asking why.

---

They help, the two of them. Daruun and Tamir.

They came to Harrow's End the year after and built a house where Hesset's tent used to be, and they hung it with herbs and little bronze bells. Daruun mends nets and knives and teaches the village boys to throw a spear. Tamir doesn't say much. He never will, I think. But every evening he sits outside the well-keeper's house and hums the phoenix song, and the baby stops crying to listen. He's going to be raised by more people than any bearer before him. A well-keeper. A laughing mother. A father who ran and came back. A brother who forgot and remembered. A king in a red city who sends a falcon every season with honey cakes from a certain kitchen.

And a fox.

I'll tell you one more thing, and then I'll stop.

Lately, on the darkest nights, the dunes out at the far edge of the desert have started writing again. Out where no one has ever come back from. I've seen the shapes from the top of the Crown Dune. They aren't letters I know. I have walked this desert since the first grain of it was laid down, and I can't read a single word.

The Light doesn't come back for no reason, little one. It comes back because it's going to be needed.

But not tonight.

Tonight the lanterns are burning all along the edge of the desert, and your mother is asleep, and your father is asleep in the chair beside her, still holding her hand. And I'm curled up beside your cradle with my nose on my paws, where I'll be every night from now on, whether anyone likes it or not.

You've been listening all this time. I know you have. Your mark has been glowing brighter at every part where he was brave.

So let me start again, the way I started it. The way I'll start it for you every night, until you're old enough to tell me to stop.

Let me tell you about a boy who had no name…

He was the one I loved most. Don't tell the others.

The end is the beginning, you see. It always has been. And out there in the dark, past the lanterns and the golden dunes and the far edge of the world, something is already waiting for you.

The Fire is coming up.

Wake up, little one.
