# Chapter Eighteen
## The Night of Lamps

> *On the Night of Lamps, every lamp is a name. When the lamps go out, so do the names.*
> — painted above the gate of the Sheikh's court, Ubar; repainted daily

---

Once a year, on the night the moon comes up perfectly round, the whole of the sands lights lamps for the lost bearers.

In the villages of Liwa it's a few clay lamps on doorsteps. Everyone goes to sleep feeling vaguely blessed and slightly relieved, the way you feel after a burial that wasn't yours.

In Ubar, every family carried one extra lamp through the streets at dusk, filled with oil saved for a year, a thumb at a time, and set it in the wall niches where the names of their dead were painted, so that for one night the names glowed and nothing could drink them. And on the great black stairs of the fort, eleven brass lamps with panes of coloured glass were set out in a row. One for each bearer who had come before.

I stood with my forepaws on the tower sill and watched an old lamp-keeper light them, one by one.

The Shepherd. The Weaver's Daughter. The Smith. The Twin Who Stayed. The Last Sheikh. The Sand Singer. The Boy of Salt. The Falconer. The Blind One. The Dreamer. The Runner.

I knew every one of them. I knew which had wet themselves on the last night, and which had cried for their mothers. The Boy of Salt was nine. Not one soul down there could tell you who they were lighting a lamp for, or how old, or what they'd smelled like burning.

Behind me Theyab sat against the wall with his eyes shut. The mark glowed through his shirt, brighter than I'd ever seen it, as though it knew what night it was and was looking forward to it.

"Eleven," he said. "There should be another one. Tomorrow."

I came and put my chin on his foot.

---

Before the moon was up I saw the dust.

Out on the dunes to the east, in the last red of the light, camels at the run, a dozen strung out. I put my nose to the window slit. Goat-hair tents. Coffee smoke. Ghaf wood. Children.

The Al Bu Ghaf.

They came down the last dune to the east gate in a spray of sand, men shouting hoarse up at the wall, and among them a small high voice shouting the same thing over and over, and a small shaved head in front of the headman's saddle.

The gate didn't open. Men on the wall laughed and pointed rifles down. Raiders, they thought, or beggars, or Nameless, with that shaved child.

But in the lane just inside the gate, doors opened. A man and a woman came out and stood listening to the shouting from beyond the wall. Then they went back in, took their family's lamp out of its niche and in with them, and barred the door. The house beside theirs did the same. Then the next.

I found out later what the voice was shouting. Three nights before, the eleven children had sat up in the Al Bu Ghaf tents all at once, in the dark, and said in the flat voice of the hold: *The lamps go out on the night of the full moon. Ubar first.* They'd heard it through the dhow's planks. They hadn't known they remembered it. The headman had looked at those eleven faces for one night and then saddled every camel he had.

The camels wheeled away round the wall, south, toward where Sharood was waiting.

The moon cleared the dunes.

---

The first lamp went out just after it rose.

One tiny flame on the western wall, and then it simply wasn't there. Then the one beside it. A gap of darkness opened along the wall, spreading, like a black thread drawn slowly through gold cloth.

Down in the city, the whispering of the names stopped. Ten thousand faces turned west.

In the silence, from under every street, came the knocking.

Not three knocks. Not patient. Hundreds of fists, all at once, hammering on the underside of the city like people trapped under the ice of a well.

Then somebody screamed, and every lamp on the southern wall went out together.

There were shapes on the walls. Grey robes, white masks. The Nameless walked the battlements in a line, and as they passed each lamp they cupped a hand over it, almost tenderly, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes, and it died. They were humming the wordless chant from the dhow.

And where the light died, the grey came in.

It poured over the walls like sea fog at dawn and slid down into the streets. Where it touched the wall niches, the names didn't fade. They *ran*. The red ochre went liquid and slid down the stone in long streaks, a hundred years of names bleeding into the gutters, and the grey lapped them up as they went.

Then the iron grates over the tomb shafts began to lift.

All over the city, slowly, pushed from below. Out of them came hands. Then arms. Then figures, grey and white-eyed, wrapped in rotted linen that trailed behind them. They had been down there a long time, knocking, waiting to be let in.

The people of Ubar saw them climbing out of the ground in grave-wrappings and thought their dead were rising.

Some of them were right.

---

And then the well spoke.

Ubar's great well lies in the square below the fort, a round black mouth as wide as a threshing floor, ringed with lamps. Those lamps were going out one after another, and as the last of them died, a sound came up the shaft. It came up the way the cold comes up out of a cistern, and every face in the square turned to it.

A voice. Not loud. It didn't need to be. The well was a throat a hundred fathoms deep, and it carried every word up to the moon.

"*Whoever frees me,*" it said, warm as a grandfather, "*I will make rich. Pearls by the basket, and gold, and camels till the dunes are hidden. And long life. A hundred years. A thousand.*"

I knew that voice from the ledge. I knew it from much further back.

My hackles went up stiff as a brush.

"*Whoever frees me,*" it said again, and the voice was different now, quicker, a merchant's voice, eager, leaning across the counter, "*I will grant three wishes. Any three. Only ask.*"

My hackles stayed up. Higher.

Then there was a pause, a long one, and the knocking under the streets stopped to listen.

"*Whoever frees me,*" said the well, and now it was hardly a voice at all, only a deep, patient grinding, like millstones turning without grain for a thousand years, "*I will kill. And everything he loves I will take. And everyone who remembers him. Until no one in the sands can say his name.*"

And every hair on my back lay down.

Flat. Smooth. As if a hand had stroked it.

That was the vow it meant. The other two it had meant once, perhaps, in the dark under the brass, in the first long years, while it was still counting the days and believing someone would come. Then the centuries went by, and nobody came, and whatever gratitude had been in it went the way of dates in a shut jar. It has said all three ever since. The sweet ones on top, where they'll be heard first. The true one underneath.

A shepherd boy heard the first one, a long time ago, frightened half out of his mind on the eve of his burning, kneeling on a ledge above black water. He thought it was the only one.

Behind me, Theyab had his hands over his ears. "It keeps saying it," he whispered. "Over and over. Who's it talking to?"

Everyone. It always has. It's a promise that went bad in the dark, and like all bad things in the dark, it wants company.

Down in the square the people of Ubar were backing away from the well. Some of them were nodding as they went. That was the worst of it. As though they'd heard the first promise, and nothing after.

---

There had been two guards on the stair. I'd heard nothing from them for some time. Not a step. Not a breath. The bolt slid back.

The Seventh stood in the doorway.

Through the crack in his mask I could see a strip of his real face: the edge of one pale eye, a line of cheek so white it had never seen the sun. He had Hazza's sword in one hand and Old Bakhit's bell in the other.

He held them out.

I didn't growl. A man in a Nameless mask, smelling of the hold and the grey blades, and every hair on my back lay flat, because there was nothing in him to be false.

"Saqr," said Theyab.

The figure flinched as if struck. The sword and the bell clattered to the floor. His hands came up to his mask and gripped its edges and pulled. I saw the skin of his jaw stretch where the rim met it. It didn't come away. It had grown there. He let go, shaking, with a sound like a hurt dog. I know that sound. I've made it.

He hummed four notes. Not the chant. The beginning of the fire-bird song.

Then he turned and ran.

Theyab picked up the sword, and tied the bell back on his belt by its camel-hair cord. "He's still in there," he said.

I licked his hand. I'd known since the first night the Seventh let him go. I put that on the pile with the rest.

---

The streets were full of grey.

Most of the lamps were out. The ones still burning lay smashed on the stones, oil spreading in burning pools, and people knelt beside them scooping the burning oil up in their bare hands to save it, screaming as they did it, and not stopping.

And everywhere, the emptied.

They moved through the crowds slowly, reaching out, touching. At every touch, someone stopped running and stood with their eyes going white and lost. Then the newly emptied began to speak, each saying the last thing they had said before the touch, over and over, in a flat, pleasant voice, smiling with faces that didn't know how to smile anymore.

*Bar the door. Bar the door.*

*Mother, I'm here. Mother, I'm here.*

*Where's the oil, where's the oil.*

At the corner of the oil market a merchant's stall was burning. The great jars had cracked in the heat, and the oil ran down the lane in a gold river, and the man who owned it was standing in the middle of it.

I knew his kind by the smell before I saw his face. Musk and stale tobacco, and the sour sweat of a man counting money he hasn't earned. Ghanim had smelled like that under his rose attar, over his crooked scales in Hilat al-Sarab.

This one was an oil-seller of Ubar, one of the brass-spoon men from the inner gates.

He'd hoarded twenty camel-loads of oil for the one night of the year the city would pay anything. His camels were gone, led off loaded through the east lanes by grey robes. His strongbox lay open and empty at his feet. His ledger was burning. And he stood in his own oil with the hem of his robe smouldering and his eyes gone white, holding up one finger and smiling.

"Two thalers the thumb," he said pleasantly, to no one. "Two thalers the thumb."

That was the last thing he'd said before the hand touched him. He'd been naming a price.

The awning went up with a roar, and when the smoke cleared the grey had carried him off down the lane with the others, still holding up his finger. I didn't growl. Two thalers the thumb was exactly what he'd meant to charge.

---

At a wall niche in the next lane a girl of six stood alone. One of the emptied had touched her cheek, and the grey was spreading across her face like a stain through cloth. She was looking up at the wall where her family's names had been. There was nothing on it now but red streaks running down into the gutter.

"Whose names were those?" she said to no one. "I can't… whose were they?"

Then she said it again, in exactly the same voice. The grey had reached the corner of her mouth.

Theyab was on his knees in front of her before I could get between them. I barked, once, hard: *every jackal in this city will come.*

"Yes," said Theyab, and put his hands on her face.

The Light came gold. It always did, by then, when it was for someone else. The grey shrank back from it like frost from the sun. Her eyes cleared. On the wall behind her a single name glowed faint and gold where the ochre had run. Her grandmother's. Then it faded. But the girl remembered it. I watched her remember.

And Theyab saw. An old woman's henna'd hands braiding her hair. A grown woman reading names at this same wall.

And then his own death, the way it always came now. Not a picture. A place he was *in*.

The full moon, close enough to touch. The cold sand of Tal Moreeb under his bare feet. And the heat, starting in his chest, under the mark, the way a coal starts in kindling. His skin splitting along the golden lines. Hair, cloth, and something under them that was himself. A small boy's hand slipping out of his. His father on his knees. Grey hands plunging into the gold. Letters moving across the dunes, a word he could almost read. And behind the letters, at the very edge of the world, something watching the fire the way a man watches a coffee pot come to the boil.

He came back screaming.

He'd never done that before. He knelt on the stones beating at his own chest, at flames that weren't there, and I pushed my head into him and licked his face, over and over, the way you lick a pup that won't wake, until he stopped. The cloth over his mark had scorched brown in the shape of a feather.

The girl's mother dragged her away into the dark without a word.

"I saw a word," Theyab said, raw. "In the sand. And something past it."

Across the lane three jackals had come out of the grey with their coal eyes on him, and the one in front was saying *Theyab, Theyab* in a little wet child's voice.

---

They never reached us.

A spear took the first through the chest, and it burst into black sand. The pale camel came down the lane like a ghost with Sharood on her back, his veil torn half away. And behind him, filling the lane, the hard brown faces of the Al Bu Ghaf, their old rifles going off like dry palm-ribs in a fire, the headman bellowing like one of his own bulls.

Sharood was always going to come. Fifteen years late, but always.

"*The fort!*" he shouted. "They're inside! Go!"

The third jackal sprang at Theyab's throat.

It met me instead.

I don't like to talk about the other shape. But it happened in that lane, and I didn't care who saw. The light of the burning oil threw my shadow up the wall, and the shadow didn't stay the size of a dog. It swelled up the wall and over the roofs, long and lean and wrong, a saluki the height of a watchtower with a silver coal for an eye. And I moved inside it with a strength no dog has ever had or should. I took the jackal out of the air and shook it once, and it came apart like smoke. It tasted like home.

That's the part I hate. Not the size. The joy. The silver half of me that is jinn howled with joy.

The people in the lane screamed at me the way they'd screamed at the things in the grates. An old Al Bu Ghaf rider covered his eyes and asked the One who made the sands to protect him from me. I didn't blame him.

The old burn on my left flank, where Theyab's Light had caught me in the storm, tore open.

Behind us, the grey was rolling down the lane like a wave full of smiling faces. I planted myself across the lane in front of it with my back to him, and growled. In that shape, my growl rattles shutters. He understood. The bearers always do.

He wasn't afraid of me. He hadn't been for a long time. I hadn't noticed when it changed.

"Don't die," he said.

I'm a great deal harder to kill than you, I thought at him. That's sort of the whole problem.

He ran.

---

The fort stairs were black stone, wide enough for twenty men abreast, rising in one long climb from the court to the carved doors of the majlis. Of the eleven brass lamps along them, seven had gone out. The other four threw coloured light across the steps: red, then green, then gold.

In the court below, Hazza's name was gone from the wall. The stone was perfectly clean. And in front of it, on his knees, a smashed pot of ochre beside him, was Zaid, painting his brother back onto bare stone with his fingers. He didn't look round.

He didn't see the jackal.

It came out from under the stairs on its belly, the way a jackal stalks a kid goat at the edge of the herd. No smell. No sound. Its coal eyes were fixed on the back of the boy's neck. Theyab was a whole court away, and I was a lane behind him holding back a wave.

And from the top of the court wall, someone whistled.

A herder's whistle. High and rising, thin, wobbling, stubborn. The kind the camel boys use, that cuts through wind.

Zaid's head came up. He turned, saw the jackal a body's length from him already gathering to spring, flung the ochre in its face, and ran. Up the stairs, two at a time, like a hare. The jackal's jaws closed on the place his neck had been.

Theyab cut it in half from behind before it could turn.

On top of the court wall, against the moon, stood a small shaved figure with two fingers in its mouth. Five years old. No name. He'd been told to stay with the camels. He had climbed the wall instead. Somebody was lost, and he had whistled, and somebody had come.

He was still whistling when the Al Bu Ghaf headman reached up and hauled him down by the ankles.

Remember that whistle. I do.

---

The Eldest let Zaid run past him through the great doors.

He stood at the top of the stairs with his feet planted wide and a grey blade as long as Theyab was tall. The largest man I have ever seen, and I had recently met Umm Shamma. Tonight he wore the mask, smooth and white and perfect. In all his years at Muhaisen's side he had never once been heard to speak. He watched the small boy run past him into the dark of the majlis and didn't lift a finger.

The most frightening thing I saw that night. The boy wasn't escaping. He was being *let in*.

The Eldest came down the stairs. Theyab met him halfway.

The first blow would have taken Theyab's head if he hadn't dropped flat. It struck the stone and threw sparks. The second came straight down. Theyab rolled, and the edge skimmed his shoulder.

It didn't cut skin. Grey blades don't need to. But the Light dimmed where it touched, and Hazza's face was gone.

Not blurred. Gone. He knew he'd had a friend, more important than anyone. He reached for the face and found a blank, smooth space, as smooth as the mask above him. He reached for the eyes, for *DON'T FORGET HIS EYES*, and there was nothing to not forget. And he could feel himself beginning not to mind.

Then, out of the grey, a voice, laughing at him by a fire a long way back.

*A short blade means you have to get close. Most men are afraid to get close. So be the one who isn't.*

Hazza' had said that on the third night at Bir al-Ghina', showing off. Theyab had rolled his eyes. He remembered rolling his eyes. And from that one small, stupid memory the rest came back like water into a footprint: the scarred jaw, the crooked grin, the dark eyes always laughing. *Dark.* His eyes were dark. *There* you are.

Theyab got up.

The Eldest swung wide to keep him off. Theyab went *in*. Under the swing, so close the long blade was useless, so close he could hear the man breathing behind the mask, quick and shallow, like someone very frightened.

He raised Hazza's broken sword, and the Light came down his arm, into the hilt, along the broken blade, and where the steel ended it kept going. For one heartbeat Hazza's sword was whole, the missing half made of gold as bright as noon. It met the grey blade where hilt joined steel.

The grey blade shattered.

The pieces rang down the stairs like bells. The Eldest stumbled, went down on his knees, and the white mask cracked from brow to chin and fell off him in two halves.

Under it was a tired grey-bearded man with a broken nose and old eyes. He'd been taught, I think, what to do when he lost. He lifted his chin, bared his throat, and waited.

Theyab stood over him with the golden half-blade a finger from that throat.

I'd come up the court by then, a dog again, limping, bleeding. I'll tell you what I wanted. I wanted him to do it. Every child in the dhow had been carried past that man. I'd have done it with my teeth.

The man's lips were moving. Trying, I think, to say a name. He'd been silent so long he'd forgotten how.

The gold on the blade flickered grey at the edges. Then gold.

Theyab lowered the sword.

"No," he said. Not to the man. To himself. Then, to the man: "Go home. If you remember where it is."

He stepped past him up the stairs and didn't look back. The Eldest stayed on his knees with his throat still bared, tears running into his beard. My hackles had never risen once. There was nothing false left in him. There was nearly nothing in him at all.

Then, from inside the majlis, a child screamed.

---

Inside, the dark had won.

Every lamp on its chain had gone out but one, high above the black bench, a single small flame swinging slowly. By its light I could see grey robes and white masks standing in rows along the walls where the men of Ubar had sat that morning with their coffee. Dozens of them. Humming. And beside the black stone bench, Wazir Muhaisen.

He had Zaid, held against his chest with one arm, quite gently, the way you'd hold a sleepy child. In his other hand was a grey blade, thin as a reed.

At the foot of the dais lay the Sheikh.

Theyab ran to him. Nobody stopped him. That was the worst of it.

Sheikh Mutlaq lay on his back with one hand still stretched toward his son. No blood. Grey blades don't do blood. The grey was spreading from his chest like ink through water. His beard was going white in streaks. His eyes were fading from the edges in, the brown draining toward the middle.

"He stepped in front," said Muhaisen softly. "The blade was meant for the boy, to make him easier to carry. My Sheikh stepped in front of it. I didn't expect that. He hasn't moved quickly in years."

Every word true. My hackles stayed flat and I hated them for it.

Theyab put his hands on the Sheikh's chest. The Light came, and the grey drank it, like water poured into sand. He pushed harder, until the cracks on his own skin blazed and began, at the edges, to smoke. The Sheikh's eyes cleared for a moment.

And Theyab saw. The Sheikh, young, swinging a small dark-haired boy up onto a racing camel. The same boy dragged out of this majlis in chains while the Sheikh didn't look up. Then no future. Only dark. And then his own death again, and this time he didn't scream, because he hadn't the breath.

"Hazza'," the Sheikh whispered. "I didn't listen to him. I should have… listened."

"I know," said Theyab.

"Tell him." The Sheikh fumbled at the chain at his neck and pulled, and it broke, and he pressed the ring into Theyab's palm. "Tell him I'm sorry. And give this to Zaid. When he's sheikh."

And Theyab said, "I'll tell him."

I was in the doorway, and I felt my hackles lie down along my spine, smooth, and I understood what it meant, and I wanted to be sick on the Sheikh's carpet. The boy couldn't lie. He believed with his whole heart that before the night was out he would see Hazza' to tell him.

The Sheikh's eyes went past Theyab to his younger son. He smiled at Zaid, a small, broken, loving smile, and the grey reached his eyes as he did it, and the smile stayed after the rest of him had gone. Fixed and pleasant. The smile of the emptied, on a sheikh's mouth.

Zaid stopped screaming.

---

"How touching," said Muhaisen.

High above the bench, the last lamp went out.

In the dark, Theyab's Light was the only light, and it wasn't gold anymore. It had gone grey, dim and flickering, because he was angry enough to tear that fort down stone by stone and everyone in it.

That was what Muhaisen wanted. Grey light is easy to take.

I barked. Once. Sharp, the bark a saluki gives when the hunter is about to ride his camel off a cliff after a lost falcon.

He heard me. He shut his eyes and breathed, and slowly the grey went out of him. Not all the way. Enough.

When he opened them, Muhaisen was at the door to the east roof, with Zaid under one arm. Beyond came the groaning of camels in the court and the howling of jackals.

Zaid wasn't crying. He was humming. Two notes. The chant. He'd been in the wazir's arms only a few minutes.

"Tal Moreeb, bearer," said Muhaisen. "Under the full moon. You know the way. You've been there every time you closed your eyes. Come and give me your name. Or the boy will give me his. He's already halfway to forgetting it."

Then he was gone, and the masks along the walls went with him like smoke.

From the east roof we saw the camels out through the eastern gate and running, jackals loping round them, Muhaisen at the front with Zaid before him on the saddle. At the very back a lone rider with twin blades turned and looked up at the fort a long time. Then he rode after the others.

---

Sharood came up the stair behind us with grey dust on his clothes and his spear black to the elbow. Below, the city was dark, every lamp out, except for one short lane by the east gate, where the families who'd heard a child shouting over the wall at dusk had taken their lamps inside and barred their doors. Their windows glowed. One lane. That was all.

In the streets the emptied had stopped moving and stopped speaking. They stood like grave-posts, every white face turned the same way.

East.

The desert had moved in the night.

It stood a little way out from the walls, so close you could have walked there before the moon went down. A dune higher than any dune in the world, higher than Jebel Hafeet. It rose out of the flat sand in one long, impossible curve, and as I watched, the sand on its flanks moved. Slowly. Up. Like the flank of something breathing. At its very top, white and enormous and perfectly round, sat the full moon.

Every herder in Liwa knows that dune. It stands at the edge of their date gardens, days from here. Their grandfathers' grandfathers grazed goats at its foot.

Tal Moreeb.

It had come to fetch him. Things that are hungry enough don't wait for their food to walk.

Sharood made a sound beside me that I have heard only a few times in my life. The sound a father makes when he understands he can't stop what is coming, and that when he ran from it at sixteen, all he did was keep it waiting for his son.

Theyab looked down at Hazza's ring, and threaded it onto the cord at his neck, beside the silver knot. Where his fingers touched it, it smoked.

"It's time," he said.

Neither of us answered. There was nothing left that was true and kind at the same time.

---

*End of Chapter Eighteen*
