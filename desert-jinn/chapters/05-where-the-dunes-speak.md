# Chapter Five
## Where the Dunes Speak

> *The sand writes; the wise read; the rest are buried. The sand writes on them too.*
> — a proverb of the old caravan guides of the Omani interior

---

The desert doesn't care about you. That's the first thing you learn out there, and the last thing most people forget.

It isn't cruel. Cruelty takes attention. If you happen to die in it, it covers you with the same patience it covers everything else, and a few hundred years later uncovers you again, just to see. I've seen faces come up after a winter of shamal winds, dry and perfectly kept, mouths open, as if they'd been calling for help the whole time.

Theyab knew the desert. It was the one advantage he had.

He knew to walk in the cool hours and lie up through the white glare of noon in the shade of a dune's slip-face, his headcloth over his eyes. He knew the bright yellow *handhal* gourds that trail across the hollows, round as oranges and bitter enough to kill you, and never to touch them however thirsty he was. He knew that a little brown water would sometimes seep into a hole dug at the bottom of a dune hollow, if you dug, and waited, and were lucky. And he knew to lick the dew from the undersides of flat stones at dawn.

---

We walked for six days.

The straight path lasted only the first morning. After that, every dawn the way ahead looked different. Theyab would climb the highest crest he could find and turn slowly in a circle until something told him which way to go. A tug in the feather mark. Some mornings he stood up there a long time, and I knew he was wondering whether the thing pulling him was the One who made the sands or something else, and whether there was a difference.

At night he kept us straight by the stars, the way every herder's child in Liwa learns before he can count: al-Thurayya rising in the east before dawn, the Jadi that never moves, and low in the south, burning red through the dust, Suhail. The caravan men say a man who keeps Suhail in his eye will never be lost. They don't say what happens to a boy who's being pulled by something else.

On the second evening, with the sky going violet, he looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "Bakhit says you understand. Everything."

I looked back at him.

"All right," he said. "Then we'll do it like this. If the answer is yes, you bark. Once. If it's no, you look away. Can you do that?"

I barked. Once. It echoed off the dune, and a sand-grouse went up out of a hollow with a clatter of wings.

He stared at me with his mouth open. None of the others had thought of it. Eleven children had talked to me, and wept on me, and slept against me, and not one of them had thought to ask me anything that could be answered.

Remember that bark. One bark, *yes*. I would use it to lie to him exactly once.

"Are you a jinn?" he asked, much more quietly.

I didn't bark. I didn't look away. I lay there with my chin on my paws and looked at him with my amber eye and my silver one, and after a while he looked away instead.

"Why did you watch me? All those years. In the village. Why did you stay?"

There's no bark for that. I came and lay down against his side, the whole length of me, the way a saluki lies against the person it's chosen, and put my head on his knee.

He put his hand on my neck.

"Because somebody had to," he said, answering for me.

My hackles lay flat as water. He looked down at them. He knew what that meant now. He didn't ask anything else for the rest of the night.

---

He didn't sleep much. When he did, he woke with his hands slapping at his chest, putting out a fire. Once he sat up and said, perfectly clearly, "It's so high up," and didn't remember it in the morning. I did.

He ate Umm Hanan's bread a little at a time, a strip at dawn and a strip at dusk, and a date from her fistful, the stone kept in his cheek for an hour after to fool a thirsty mouth. He shared it with me every time.

The bread was what kept him on his feet. I want to be plain about that. There was no water to speak of out there, and no food for a boy with a rusty knife who can't run down a hare. There was a round of flatbread and a fistful of dates wrapped by a frightened old widow at midnight, and it was enough. Exactly enough. Not a strip more. I've thought about that many times since.

On the fourth night he finished it. The four squashed honey-dates from the wreck of his tent he left where they were. He knew harder days were coming. He kept the indigo cloth, folded small in with the grey one, and didn't say why.

---

The dunes spoke on the fifth night.

We'd stopped at the foot of a long crescent ridge, the slip-face rising steep and smooth above us like a wall. Theyab had scooped a hollow in the sand to sleep in. The moon had risen late and lopsided. I lay with my nose on my paws and watched him not sleep.

Then the sand began to move.

Something small. Close. On the smooth face of the slope in front of us, the sand was rippling, the way a camel's flank ripples when a fly walks on it. Fine lines were drawing themselves across the slope. Curves and hooks and dots and long straight strokes, crossing and joining, and they weren't being drawn on the sand. They were being pushed up from beneath it, from inside the dune, as if by a single fingertip moving very slowly just under the surface. As they formed, they began to glow.

Gold.

Theyab sat up so fast he sprayed sand over me.

A line, then a second line beneath it, glowing across the dark face of the dune like fallen stars. They had the shape of meaning, the way a voice in the next tent has the shape of words even when you can't make them out. They ran from right to left, the way real writing does.

I could hear it, too. A faint dry whisper as the strokes moved, like many reed pens on old paper, as if a great many hands were writing at once under the sand, all of them patient, all of them in the dark. And it smelled, not of a lie, but of honey, and hot metal.

Theyab got up and stood at the foot of the slip-face for a long, long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "I can't read."

Nobody had ever thought to teach a foundling scavenger his letters, and it had never mattered before.

It mattered now. I could see it go into him like the word *monster* did.

"Ghareeb," he said, without turning round. "Can you read it?"

I could have looked away. He'd have believed me. I barked.

"Then tell me what it says."

I didn't move.

"You can't," he said. "Of course you can't. You're a dog." He laughed, a short ugly sound with nothing in it.

It was partly that. But it was also the thing I'd tell him if I had a tongue for it: it isn't written for me. The sands write, and the bearer reads. Never the dog. And I didn't want him to know what it said. Not that night. Not any night, if I could help it, and I knew I couldn't.

"It's written for me," he said, "in a way I can't understand. So it's written for nobody."

He said it flatly. That was worse.

"Something forgot me in the sand when I was a baby," he went on. "And now it writes me letters I can't read. That's very funny. That's the funniest thing I've ever heard."

He turned his back on the dune, walked to his hollow and lay down in it, facing away from the light.

The writing glowed on behind him for a long time. The whispering went on with it. Then, slowly, the sand smoothed itself over, and the gold faded, and the sound stopped. Except that just before it stopped, the strokes under the sand went still all at once, every one of them, the way a majlis goes quiet when the man everyone was talking about walks in.

---

I'll tell you something now that I didn't tell him.

I read it. Of course I did. The bottom line was a single word, written large.

I'm not going to tell you what it was. It isn't mine to tell. But I'd seen that line before, in that place on the dune, at the bottom, written large. Eleven times, over eleven different children, and each time the word was different, and each time it meant the same thing.

I lay awake the rest of that night watching the place where it had been, and I don't think I've ever hated the sky quite so much. It writes like someone who loves you. It writes like that every time.

If he'd been able to read it, would anything have been different? No. I know better. Nothing would have been different. It never is. That's not despair, by the way. That's arithmetic.

---

The ghoul came the next afternoon.

We were crossing a wide flat basin between two lines of dunes, a dead-pale floor of hard sand crusted white where some ancient water had dried and left its salt. It was the hottest hour, there was no shade, and the far dunes looked close. They always look close.

A lake lay across the middle of the basin, silver and still, the dunes upside down in it. Every child in Liwa knows that lake. It's the *sarab*, the mirage. His village was named for it. He didn't even look at it.

Then I heard a camel bell.

*Tonk. Tonk. Tonk.*

Theyab stopped. Out in the shimmer, at the edge of the false water, there was a man with a pack-camel, waterskins sagging dark and swollen against its sides. The man wore a white robe and a red-checked headcloth, and he was waving, big slow friendly waves, the way you wave across a wadi to a cousin.

"Ya walad!" he called, clear and warm. "Boy! Come! You're thirsty, I can see it from here. I have water. Come and drink. Come and be my guest."

Water, and the guest-right: the two holiest words in the sands, side by side. Nobody had ever called him guest. He took a step forward. Then another.

Every hair on my body went up at once.

All of me, and the growl that came out hurt my chest. The wind had turned and I could smell the man with the camel, and he smelled the way the inside of a lie smells. Not sour. Rotten. A lie so complete there was nothing else in it at all. No sweat. No camel. No man.

The bell around Theyab's neck began to ring, over and over, tiny and frantic, like a bird beating itself against a cage. Theyab stopped.

"Come," said the man, smiling. "Come, little one. Don't be shy. Your mother's here. She's been waiting for you so long."

The bell rang so hard it jumped on its cord.

And I looked at the man's feet.

The hem of his robe swung as he walked toward us, and under it, where his feet should have been, the sand was printed with small, neat hooves, like a donkey's. Every grandmother from the Hajar to the Empty Quarter teaches that sign: the *ghoul* can wear any face and any voice in the world, your father's, your wife's, a lost traveller's, but it cannot hide its feet.

The man stopped walking.

He looked down at his own hooves, as if he'd only just noticed. Then he looked up at me, and the smile on his face kept going. It went wider, and wider, past the corners of his mouth and up toward his ears, and the face around it began to sag inward like a waterskin being emptied.

"Run," Theyab whispered. He'd seen it too. "Ghareeb, *run*—"

We ran.

The ghoul dropped.

Man and camel together, straight down into the basin floor, the way water drops into dry sand. The bell rang once more, under the ground. *Tonk.* Then the sand behind us bulged in a long low ridge, like a blanket over something moving, the pale crust going black as if something were soaking up through it.

Then the ground *erupted*.

It came up out of the basin like a fountain of black sand, rearing, impossibly long, and it was still, in some horrible way, trying to be a man. There was the suggestion of a headcloth. There were arms, too many of them, too long. But its body was the black sand itself, endlessly pouring and re-forming, and where its face should have been there was a wide blunt shape that opened in the middle like a split seam, and below it all, holding it up, a cluster of thin grey legs ending in small neat hooves.

And through the pouring black of its body, as it rose, I saw what it had eaten.

A camel's ribcage, turning slowly inside it, the bell still on its rotten halter. A child's anklet of silver bells. A face, pressed against the inside of the sand as it poured past, the mouth open in exactly the shape of the faces the desert sometimes gives back. They went round and round inside it like things in a slow flood, and none of them would ever come out.

The *ghilan* lie under the dunes the way sand-skinks do, and when they feel something warm walking above, they put on a face it will trust and call it in. Then they swallow it, and keep it, and go back down.

It came down at us like a falling tower.

Theyab threw himself sideways. The ghoul struck where he'd been and went straight into the ground, and the whole basin floor heaved and knocked him flat. He came up spitting salt, knife in hand.

It burst up in front of him, a black wall of hissing dust, and he skidded, fell, scrambled back. It was herding him, the way a falcon works a hare out onto the open flat. It knew this basin. He didn't.

The feather mark was burning through his shirt. He held out his hand, and the light came.

Grey.

He was terrified. A thin sputtering light flickering in his palm like a lamp-wick in a draught. The ghoul didn't even pause. It reared up over him, blotting out the sun, the split in its face opening wide, and the air that came out of it was cold and wet and smelled of the bottom of an old well, and of the people in it. Out of that split, in a dozen voices, a man's and a woman's and a child's, it said, kindly:

*"Come and be my guest."*

And that was when I did something stupid.

---

I went for it.

I didn't think. I ran straight up the loose pouring sand at the base of its body, between those thin grey legs, and I bit one of them just above the hoof.

It tasted of nothing. Dust and cold. And, very faintly, of the people inside.

A coil of black sand whipped round and caught me, pouring and squeezing at once. I felt myself being pulled in and down, toward the darkness under the dunes, and something brushed against my side in the black, and it was a hand. It was dry and light and it closed, gently, in the feathering of my ear, the way you'd stroke a dog that had just come in out of the heat. Welcoming me home.

I heard Theyab shout my name.

Through a gap in the black sand, I saw him get to his feet.

And I saw the light change.

It's the same every time. One moment it was grey, the light of a boy afraid for himself. The next, he was looking at a skinny old dog being dragged into the sand, and he forgot his own fear the way you forget a thorn in your foot when the tent is on fire.

The light went gold.

It roared up out of his chest and out of his hands, and he didn't throw it, exactly. He *reached* for me, and the light reached with him.

It hit the ghoul square in the body.

Where the gold touched the black sand, it turned glassy and then, with a sound like a thousand tiny bells breaking, it shattered. The coil around me burst apart, and the dry hand let go of my ear last. Above me, the light raced up the ghoul's body the way fire races up a dead palm. The whole vast shape shrieked, high and thin, and in that shriek, I swear to you, there were voices. Many. One of them, I'm almost sure, said *thank you*. Then it collapsed in a slithering rain of black glass and ash that pattered over the basin for a long time.

What it had been keeping fell with it. Bones. The bell, rolling to a stop at Theyab's feet. The child's anklet, its little silver bells full of sand.

When it was over, Theyab was on his knees.

Golden cracks ran up his forearms and down his neck. And for a moment his eyes went far away and white, and his mouth opened, and I knew he was seeing it again, the moon, the height, the heat. It had come without a healing, this time. Just from using the Light. It was learning the way in.

He came back. He looked at me.

"Are you hurt?" he said.

I spat out a mouthful of black grit. I'd just been half eaten by a ghoul and petted by a dead man, and he wanted to know if I was *hurt*.

"Are you?" he said. "Bark for yes."

I looked away.

He let out a long breath and sat back on his heels.

"Good," he said.

And I had to keep looking away, at the black glass scattered across the basin, because there was something in my eye. Sand, probably. There was a great deal of sand.

---

I didn't smell him until it was too late. I'll never forgive myself for that. Light and noise bring things. We sat among the black glass, catching our breath, and the first I knew of it was a shadow falling across Theyab's shoulders, and a smell. Burned cloth. And under it something sharper and stranger, like hair singed in a lamp, like ink boiled off a page. I've smelled it in a great many places. It's the smell of a name being burned.

He looked up.

A figure stood on the dune-crest above us, against the white glare of the sun.

Grey robes, a grey headcloth wound round and round, and under it, where a face should have been, a mask. Smooth and white and perfectly blank, like a polished camel's shoulder-blade. Two narrow slits for the eyes, and behind them, darkness. Where the edge of the mask met the skin of his neck, the skin was raw and red and puckered, as though the mask had been worn so long it had begun to grow there, and every time someone had tried to lift it the face had come with it.

In each hand he held a blade, long and slightly curved, and grey. Not the grey of steel. The grey of the curse, dull and dead; they had been quenched, I'd learn later, in the Ifrit's own ash. Looking at them was like trying to remember a dream.

The Nameless. That's what they call themselves, the men in white masks. They burn their own names in a fire and take numbers instead, and they serve forgetting the way other men serve their tribe. They hunt the bearer the way the jinn-jackals do. But the jackals are animals. The Nameless know exactly what they're doing, and the worst of them do it kindly.

This one, we'd learn later, they called the Seventh.

He came down the slope.

---

He moved like water running downhill. Theyab scrambled up with his rusty knife and blocked the first blade, more luck than skill. The second blade was already coming low, at his ribs. He twisted. It missed his side by a breath and opened his sleeve from wrist to elbow.

It didn't touch his skin. It didn't need to.

Theyab staggered. For an instant his face went blank and puzzled, as if he'd walked into a tent and forgotten why. I saw him reach for something in his mind and close his hand on nothing.

That's what the ash-blades do. They don't cut flesh so much as memory. Even a whisper of one, passing close, takes something with it, and it doesn't leave a hole you can feel. It leaves smooth sand.

He swung at the masked figure, wild and desperate. The Seventh stepped inside the swing, hooked his ankle, and put him on his back. One grey blade came down and rested against his throat.

Theyab froze.

There was light in his hands, but it was grey again, flickering, useless. He'd spent everything on the ghoul. And blades quenched in the Ifrit's ash, I should tell you, are the only thing in the world that can cut the Light.

I went for the back of his knee at a full run. A knee caught me in the ribs and sent me tumbling. When I got up, nothing had changed. The white mask looking down. The blade against Theyab's throat, and a thin line of grey spreading on the skin beneath it, like frost.

The Seventh didn't move.

---

He didn't press down. He didn't draw back. He stood looking at Theyab through the slits of that blank white mask. I'm not sure there was anything left behind them to see.

Under the burned cloth and the burned names there was something else on him, faint and old and buried very deep, a smell I knew and couldn't place. It frightened me more than his blades.

It went on long enough for the hot wind to drop.

And then, under the mask, very softly, he began to hum.

You could barely hear it over the hiss of settling sand. A few notes, low and slow, climbing. A pause. The same notes again, a little higher, like something lifting off the ground. Like wings. It came out of a mask with no mouth, as if from somewhere very far back in him, a room he'd been locked out of.

Theyab's eyes went wide. I don't think he knew why. But something in his face changed, the way the grey woman's eyes had changed in Hilat al-Sarab. Something long buried turning over in its sleep.

I knew that song.

I'm not going to tell you who used to sing it, or to whom. Not yet. I stood in the sand with my fur up along my spine and made no sound.

Add it to the list. It's getting long, I know. Everything I don't tell is a stone in my belly.

The humming stopped.

The Seventh took the blade away from Theyab's throat.

He stepped back. One step, then another. The blank white mask tilted slightly to one side, as if he were asking himself a question and had forgotten what questions were for.

His hand came up to the edge of the mask. Touched the raw skin there. Pulled, a little, the way you'd worry at a scab.

Then he let go, and turned, and walked away up the dune, and over it, and was gone.

---

Theyab lay on his back in the sand a long time, staring at the sky.

"He let me go," he said finally.

I came and stood over him and licked the grey line on his throat. It tasted of cold. Of nothing.

"*Why?*" he said. "Do you know why?"

I looked away.

True, as far as it went. I knew a great many things about that masked figure. But why he lowered his blade that day, and would again and again, I truly didn't know.

Theyab turned his head and looked at me. He was learning to read the shape of what I didn't do.

"Do you know who he is?"

I got up and shook the sand out of my coat, very thoroughly, from my ears to the end of my tail, for a long time.

He looked at me a moment longer. I saw him decide not to push.

He got up, a pale mark on his throat like a line of old frost, and started walking toward the far dunes, and somewhere to sleep that wasn't the middle of a ghoul's larder.

That night, by a fire of dry *'arfaj* twigs, he sat for a long time with his knees drawn up, frowning into the flames.

"Ghareeb," he said. "There's something missing."

I lifted my head.

"In my head." He pressed the heel of his hand against his forehead. "There's a place where something was. I keep going back to it. I don't know what it was. I don't know if it was important." He looked at me, and his eyes were frightened in a way the ghoul hadn't made them. "How can I miss something if I don't know what it is?"

I knew what it was. It was the taste of Bakhit's tea. Black and bitter, smoke and something green. He'd hated it and loved it every night for years, and now it was gone, and he would never know it had been there. They always start with small things.

I put my head down on my paws and closed my eyes, which is how a dog says *go to sleep*.

He didn't. He lay on his side with his back to the fire, and after a while, very softly, almost without meaning to, he began to hum. A few notes, low and slow, climbing.

He got it wrong. He didn't know the rest.

I lay beside him and didn't correct him. I could have howled it for him, the whole of it, in a voice that wasn't mine. I didn't. And out in the dark, beyond the edge of the firelight, the sand shifted very slightly, as if something under it had turned over to listen.

---

*End of Chapter Five*
