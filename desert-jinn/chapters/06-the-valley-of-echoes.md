# Chapter Six
## The Valley of Echoes

> *Shout your name in the wadi, and it will shout back your fear. Whisper it, and it will learn it.*
> — a warning the goatherds of the Hajar give their sons, and their fathers gave them, and no one remembers who gave it first

---

The sands ended on the ninth day, and the ground fell open like a wound.

One morning we were walking the long red spine of a dune. By noon the sand had thinned to black flint that cut my pads, and the Hajar stood up in front of us out of the haze. Imagine the bones of the world left out to dry: folded rock the colour of rust and old liver, split by dry riverbeds that run with water one day in ten years and on that one day drown whoever is camped in them.

The one in front of us was Wadi al-Sada. The Wadi of the Echo.

Its mouth was a crack in the mountain no wider than a tent is long. The walls went up so sheer and so high that the sun could not reach the bottom, and the cold came out of it to meet us, carrying the smell of stone and standing water and, underneath that, something sweetish and old.

At the mouth, half buried in drifted gravel, stood a cairn of piled stones. Around its base lay a ring of small things. A sandal with the thong worn through. A wooden coffee cup. A child's clay whistle with the end bitten off. A string of blue beads, the kind mothers tie on babies against the eye. Offerings, or things dropped by people running. The desert doesn't label what it keeps.

Theyab looked at the cairn a long time. Then at me.

I had sat down. I want to be honest about that. I am a saluki, and a saluki will run a gazelle to death across open sand and think it sport, but I sat down in the gravel at the mouth of Wadi al-Sada and I put my ears flat to my skull and I would not get up.

"You don't want to go in," he said.

No. I did not.

"We have to, though." He touched his chest, where the feather mark lay under his shirt. "It pulls that way. Straight in."

It did. And on every other side the dunes had crept up behind us in the night, steep and soft and very patient, the way a crowd closes round a man the moment before they pick up stones. He knew about that. He had the scar under his shoulder blade to prove it.

---

I'll tell you what I would have told him, if I'd had a voice a man could hear.

The old men of Hilat al-Sarab say it too, around the coffee hearth, when the boys are listening: *Do not speak in Wadi al-Sada.* Not a word. Not a shout. If you fall, curse inside your head. If you see something *else*, hold your tongue harder still. Because the wadi answers. Not with your voice. With what's under it. And it remembers what it hears. Say your name in there, and the wadi keeps it, and goatherds will hear it calling up out of the rock fifty years after you're bones.

Theyab had heard those stories, sitting outside the edge of the men's majlis on cold nights, too low to be invited in. So when he looked down at me and said, quietly, "We don't talk in there. Do we," I only looked back with my one amber eye and my one silver one, and thumped my tail once on the gravel.

He had learned, by then, that I understood him. He hadn't yet learned how much.

I had been to Wadi al-Sada before. More than once. The last time, the boy I walked beside had not walked out.

I got up. I went in first. That's the only kind of answer a dog can give.

---

It was cold at the bottom.

Not cool. *Cold*, the creeping wet cold of a cistern, which is wrong in this country the way a smile is wrong on a corpse. There were pools in the shadows, black and still, fringed with maidenhair fern and the pink poison flowers of oleander, and a wild fig had forced its roots into a crack and grown pale and twisted, reaching up for a sun it would never touch. Somewhere ahead, water dripped. *Tik. Tik. Tik.* The sound came back from everywhere at once, like a great many small fingers tapping on stone.

We walked in silence.

The walls rose higher as we went. The sky narrowed to a ribbon, the way the light looks from the bottom of a well you have fallen into. Our footsteps came back to us from the rock, every one of them. They came back a little *late*. And there were more of them than we'd made.

Theyab noticed. He stopped once, and the footsteps went on for three more paces without him, and then stopped too, as if whoever was making them had paused to listen.

He looked at me. I looked at the path. We kept walking.

High on the walls were the old carvings the mountain people left before anyone remembers: stick camels, men on horses, an ibex. Those I didn't mind. The other marks were newer. Handprints in the soft red stone, long streaks of them, as if someone had walked along this ledge with a palm on the wall, slowly sinking. And scratched again and again into the rock, in many different hands, a few short words I couldn't read. I didn't need to. I've seen people write them before. They are names. It's what people scratch when they're trying to keep hold of their own.

Around midday the path narrowed to a ledge along the wall, with a long drop to the dry bed on one side. There were small bones down there. Goat, mostly. Mostly.

A loose stone turned under Theyab's foot.

He caught himself. He didn't fall. But he gasped, the small sharp breath anyone would make, and with it, before he could stop it, came a sound.

"*Ah*—"

It went up the wall like a bird let out of a box.

We both froze.

The echo came back. It should have come back as *ah*. That's what echoes do. They return what you give them.

This one didn't.

"*Thrown away*," said the wadi.

---

It wasn't loud. That was almost the worst of it. It was soft, like someone whispering a finger's width behind his ear, and it was in his own voice. Theyab's voice, but younger. The voice of a very small child who has been crying a long time and has stopped expecting anyone to come.

"*Thrown away*," it said again, farther up the gorge, and again, fainter, from down among the little bones. "*Thrown away. Thrown away.*"

His hand went to his chest. He pressed on the feather mark so hard his knuckles went white as the bones below.

"*Mistake*," said the wadi.

This voice was older. A man's voice, and I didn't know it. It said the word the way you'd speak of a camel calf born with a twisted leg. Without anger. A thing not worth the water.

"*Mistake. Should have left him in the sand. Should have left him for the foxes.*"

I pressed my shoulder against the back of his knees and pushed. *Walk.* It's what a dog does with a flock that wants to stop at the wrong place. He stumbled forward a pace, and another, and kept going.

Something moved in a hollow in the rock ahead. A shape the size of a crouching man, pressed back into the dark with its face to the wall. As we passed, it didn't turn. But under the other whispers I heard it panting. Panting like a dog. My own breath, already taken, and worn like a borrowed cloak.

"*No name*," said the wadi. "*No name. Nobody's son. Nothing.*"

Then came a voice I *did* know. Everyone in Hilat al-Sarab would have known it. Sharp, cracking, the voice of a boy who'd been taught his whole life that he was better than someone, and had chosen Theyab to be the someone.

"*Monster.*"

Theyab stopped walking.

---

"*Monster*," said Nadim's voice, high up the gorge. And then the others joined it, dozens, from every ledge and crack in the stone. The whole village, the way they'd shouted with the stones in their hands. Sheikh Mazyad. Ghanim the trader, who'd cheated Theyab on every scrap of copper he'd ever dug out of the ruins. Saqi at his well. Small children who'd learned the word before they learned to count.

"*Monster. Monster. Monster.*"

And under it, very faint, the thud of stones. Over and over. Landing on something soft.

He turned to face it.

I knew what was going to happen. I'd known since the cairn. You can warn a boy against a thing all you like, and some boys will do it anyway, because they would rather die than let the thing stand unanswered. In my experience, they usually get the chance.

"*I'm not!*" Theyab shouted.

It tore out of him, loud and raw. The wadi caught it in its red mouth and turned it inside out like a waterskin and threw it back.

"*NOT A HERO*," the wadi roared. "*NOT A HERO. NOT A HERO. NOBODY. NOBODY. FORGOTTEN.*"

It was his own voice again. Not a child's now. His own, exactly as it was, and that was the cruellest thing of all. Because none of the others had been true. Not *thrown away*, not *mistake*, not *monster*. Those were other people's words. This one was *his*. It was the fear he kept in the deepest cellar in him, under all the dreams he'd dreamed in his patched tent of being the hero in the old men's tales.

That he would do every terrible thing asked of him, and in the end it wouldn't matter. Nobody would remember him at all.

"*FORGOTTEN*," howled the wadi. "*FORGOTTEN. FORGOTTEN.*"

And then it did something I had never heard it do. It said his name.

Not *Laqeet*, foundling, which is what the village called him when they called him anything. The name he had given himself at seven, sitting on the lip of Saqi's well. The name he had never once said to the wind.

"*Theyab*," the wadi whispered, almost tenderly, from every wall at once. "*Theyab. Theyaaab.*" Tasting it, the way I'd taste a track. "*Wolves. He named himself for wolves. Who was Theyab? Nobody. Nobody remembers Theyab.*"

He went down on his knees on the ledge with both hands over his ears. It didn't help. It was coming from inside his hands.

---

I bit him.

Not hard. On the wrist, the way a mother dog takes a pup that's wandered toward the fire. Enough to sting. He yelped, and the yelp went up the gorge too, and the wadi sent back something wordless and wet, like someone crying with their face pressed into the dirt.

Then I took his sleeve in my teeth and I pulled. I braced all four feet on the ledge and pulled the way I'd pull down a hare that didn't want to die, and I growled at him through the cloth. Not the growl I keep for liars. The other one. The one that means *now*.

He got up. He walked.

And I'll tell you something I'm not proud of. When I growled, I growled a little louder than I meant to.

The wadi heard me.

"*Again*," it whispered.

I stopped dead.

I have never had a voice. Not one a man could hear. Whatever I am inside, it has only ever come out as a whine, a bark, a growl, the noises any dog makes, and once, in Hilat al-Sarab, a dead man's keening with no words in it. But I knew that voice the way you know the smell of your own bed. Old and thin and very, very tired. It was the voice I talk in, in here, to you. The wadi had reached down my throat and found it.

"*Again. Again. Again.*" And then, so faint I've sometimes told myself I imagined it: "*Too late. Too late. You're always too late.*"

Theyab turned his head and looked at me. He'd heard it too. He couldn't have known whose it was. But he looked at me as if he did.

I didn't look back. I kept my eyes on the path and my mouth shut, and I walked, and after a while he walked too.

Behind us, as we went, I heard the wadi practising. Trying out what it had learned that day, side by side, the way a child tries new words. *Theyab*, it said. And then a dog's short, sharp bark, perfectly made. *Theyab.* Bark. *Theyab.* Bark. Learning us. For later.

---

The wadi let us go a little before sunset.

The walls opened slowly, the way a fist unclenches when its owner has finally died, and spilled us out onto a wide plain of gravel where a few *samr* acacias grew crooked and black against the sky, every one of them leaning away from the gorge. There was a pool under an overhang of rock. Real water, brown and cold and tasting of iron, like a split lip. Theyab drank until he was sick, then drank again, more carefully. I drank beside him with my ears turned backward the whole time, toward the crack in the mountain.

The sun went down. The stars came out. The moon rose late, a little thinner than the night before, a pale paring low over the gorge.

Theyab sat with his back against a rock and the grey cloth from Hilat al-Sarab around his shoulders. Very quietly, he said his own name under his breath. Once. Testing. As if checking it was still there.

From the mouth of the wadi, faint across the gravel, something said it back.

He didn't say it again.

"Was it true?" he whispered. Not to me, quite. To the dark, and to me in it. "What it said. That I'll be—"

He couldn't finish. Not because the mark would burn. Because he was afraid it wouldn't.

I lay down against his side, the full length of me. The wadi doesn't tell the truth. It tells you what you're afraid of. That isn't the same thing. But nothing a dog can do says that, so I gave him what I had: warmth, and weight, and the sound of my breathing.

After a while he put his hand on my neck, in the feathered fur behind my ear, and left it there, and slept.

---

He dreamed.

He was standing on a plain, and the plain was grey.

Not the grey that had crept up to the palms of Hilat al-Sarab. Softer. A flat endless grey that ran to every edge of the world. No sky. No sound. Not even his own heartbeat. He put his hand to his chest to feel for it, found nothing, and his hand sank a little way into himself as if he were made of ash, before he snatched it back.

And there were goats.

Black goats, the long-eared mountain kind, moving slowly across the plain around him, grazing on nothing. They were made of grey mist. When they turned their heads he could see through them. They had no eyes, and their mouths kept chewing, and when one came close he heard that it was chewing words. Tiny, mumbled, half-swallowed words. *Mother. Water. Home. My name is—* and then the grinding of teeth, and the word was gone.

Among them, leaning on a long herding stick, stood a boy.

Small. Smaller than Theyab. Twelve, perhaps, thin as the stick he leaned on, in a patched brown *thawb* that was the only colour in that whole grey world. Barefoot. Face turned away.

"They always come back," the boy said. "Have you noticed? However far they stray. You just stand still and wait, and they come back to you. Everything comes back to the dark in the end."

His voice was soft and ordinary and a little hoarse, as if he hadn't used it in a very long time.

Theyab found he could speak. "Who are you?"

The boy frowned, the way you frown trying to remember where you put something down.

"I don't remember," he said. He didn't sound upset. "I used to. I'm almost sure I used to." He shrugged his thin shoulders. "It stops hurting, after a while. Not having one. Like taking off a sandal that's been rubbing."

He turned his head.

It was a boy's face, thin and brown and dusty, with a narrow chin and a crooked front tooth. All but the eyes. Where his eyes should have been there were two small round holes, very neat, as if someone had pressed them out with a thumb. Through them Theyab could see the grey. Going on forever inside his head.

"I heard you in the wadi," the boy said gently. "*Forgotten.* You're right to be afraid of it. I'm sorry. I wish someone had been honest with me, when I was where you are."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" The boy smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the terrible thing. The kindest smile anyone had given Theyab in his whole life, except Umm Hanan's, at the edge of the village, when she thought nobody saw her leave the bread. "They've told you the tales. The bearers of the Light. They burn like the fire-bird and rise again, they save everyone, their names are sung forever." He shook his head. "No one remembers the bearers."

The grey goats had stopped chewing. They had all turned their eyeless faces toward Theyab.

"In the great city they light lamps for them, once a year," the boy said. "The Night of Lamps. A whole sky of little flames. And not one person holding one could tell you a single bearer's name. Not their faces. Not whether they screamed at the end." He tilted his head. "They did, you know. Some of them. You'll want to know that later, when it's your turn. You won't be the first to scream."

"That isn't true."

The words came out of Theyab, and as they did, the feather mark gave one sharp throb. Not quite a burn. Not quite. A warning.

The boy saw it. Something in the grey behind his eyes stirred, like a fish turning in deep water.

"Isn't it?" he said softly. "You can't even say it without it hurting."

He came closer, walking without leaving footprints, and stopped an arm's length away and looked up with those two holes full of nothing. Up close he smelled of goat and woodsmoke and something burnt. Hair, maybe.

"Ask the dog," he whispered. "Ask the dog how many. Ask him what he isn't telling you."

And he reached one small dusty hand toward Theyab's chest, toward the feather, and where his fingers came near, the light under Theyab's skin began to go grey. Not dim. *Grey*. And the boy's fingers, where the light touched them, grew a little more solid. A little more real. As if he were drinking.

"There," the boy said, with his eyes closed. "Oh, there. I remember warm."

---

Theyab woke with a shout.

He was sitting bolt upright on the gravel with the grey cloth tangled round his legs, and his hands were glowing. Not gold. Grey. A dim cold sickly light, like the light on that endless plain. It flickered between his fingers and went out.

The stars were fading. Dawn was close.

And there, in the dust beside where his head had lain, were marks. Small, cloven, careful. Goat tracks, in a ring all round him, as if something had stood over him in the night and grazed.

I was standing over him with every hair along my spine up, growling at the tracks, a low ragged sound with something underneath it, like a second animal growling inside the first. The tracks smelled of goat, and smoke, and burnt hair, and under all of it a boy I had loved before the mountains had names.

"What is it?" Theyab whispered. "Ghareeb. What is it?"

He told me the dream. He told it to a dog, in the dark, because there was no one else, and I listened to all of it with my hackles up and my lips drawn back off my teeth. The grey plain. The goats chewing words. The boy with eyes like thumbprints. *No one remembers the bearers.* *Ask the dog how many.*

When he said that, I turned my head away.

"You know him," Theyab said slowly. He was watching me. He'd begun, by then, to watch me the way you watch weather. "Don't you? You know who that was."

My silver eye ached as if someone had pressed a coal into it.

"He said ask you how many. How many what?"

I turned my back on him and walked away across the gravel, up onto a tall flat rock at the edge of the plain, and I sat there with my tail wrapped round my feet and my face to the mountains.

He didn't follow. He'd learned that much about me, too.

---

I sat on that rock until the sun came up.

I looked at the sky the whole time, until al-Thurayya faded last of all. I don't know who listens up there. I know only that it's where I look when I need to be angry at something that doesn't answer.

*You let him in,* I thought at it. *Nine days out of Hilat al-Sarab, and you've already let him walk into the boy's sleep. Did you open the tent flap for him yourself?*

The sky said nothing. It never does.

I'll tell you what I couldn't tell Theyab, since you're listening too.

His name was Ghadban. *The angry one.* His mother named him for the way he came out of her, red-faced and roaring. He grew up quiet instead. A small, poor goatherd's boy in these same mountains, a very long time ago, before the glass ruins were glass. He had a crooked front tooth and a patched brown *thawb* that had been his father's, and he was afraid of the dark, and he talked to his goats because nobody else would listen.

Nobody except me. I was the dog that ran his flock. I slept across the door of his father's stone hut and walked behind him up every goat track in the Hajar.

He was the first. The very first child who ever carried the Light. And I loved him the way you love the first of anything, before you've learned what loving things is going to cost you.

At the end, he stood on a hill with the light pouring out of his chest and his eyes still brown and still his, and he looked at me and said, *You knew. You knew, and you walked me here anyway.*

He wasn't wrong. That's what the wadi found, on the ledge, when it spoke in my own voice. *Again.* That I would walk beside one more child to that dune, and watch it happen again. That I'd be the one at their heel every time, the faithful dog, bringing the lamb.

Behind me, I heard Theyab get up and come and stand at the foot of my rock. He didn't say anything. I noticed he was careful not to step in the goat tracks.

After a while, I climbed down.

He crouched so our faces were level. He had a dog-bite on his wrist, already bruising, and he hadn't mentioned it.

"Ghareeb," he said. "Was he lying? The boy?"

I looked at him. I didn't growl. I didn't do anything at all.

A dog can only say so much. And every liar I have ever known has told the truth sometimes. That's how they get you to keep listening.

He looked at me a long time, reading what wasn't there. Then he nodded slowly, as if I'd answered, and stood.

"There's a caravan track somewhere north of here," he said. "The old men talked about it. Buraimi to the coast. We need water. And people." He tried to smile. "So I'll stop talking to things in my head."

We walked out across the gravel toward the acacias, with the sun rising red on our right and Wadi al-Sada silent at our backs.

It was silent. I listened very carefully. But I could have sworn, just as we passed beyond hearing, that somewhere deep in the red stone, in a child's voice, sweet and patient and in no hurry at all, something was calling.

*Theyab. Theyab. Come back, Theyab. We'll wait.*

And after it, perfect, a dog's short bark.

*End of Chapter Six*
