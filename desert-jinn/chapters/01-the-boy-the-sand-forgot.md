# Chapter One
## The Boy the Sand Forgot

> *When the grey comes creeping and the names are lost,*
> *A child of the feather will carry the cost.*
> — from the Song of the Phoenix-Born, as sung over cradles in the goat-hair tents of Liwa

---

*The fire is ghaf wood, and it burns slow and sweet, and the children sit close because the night on the sands of Liwa is colder than any of them expected. Above them the stars are so thick they look spilled. Suhail is up, low and red in the south. The heat is breaking.*

*The old man pours the coffee himself. He will not let the boys do it. His hands shake, and he spills, and he does not care.*

*"You'll say it's a tale," he tells them. "Your fathers said so too, and their fathers. I tell you it happened. I was there. I was younger than some of you are now, and stupider than all of you put together."*

*At his feet lies a saluki, so old that the feathering on its ears has gone white as salt. One of its eyes is amber. The other is clouded silver, like a coin left in the sea. The children do not look at that eye for long. Something in it looks back.*

*"I won't tell it my way," says the old man. "My way has too much of me in it. I'll tell it the way the dog would tell it, for the dog saw all of it. All of it." He touches the dog's head, very gently, the way you touch a thing you owe. "And the dog forgives less than I do."*

*The saluki sighs through its nose. The fire settles.*

*"Listen, then."*

---

He is burning.

The moon hangs full and white above Tal Moreeb, the badr, so close and so swollen that it looks like something that has fed. The great dune of Liwa lies under it, the highest sand in the world, and on its crest a boy stands with his arms open, as if he is about to catch something falling out of the sky.

The fire doesn't start at his hands. It starts in his chest, under the mark, the small golden feather that has glowed in the dark his whole life. It splits from the inside, like a date-stone in wet ground, because the thing inside has grown too big to stay. Light comes through the cracks in his skin. It runs down his arms and up his throat. It comes out of his eyes, and his eyes go with it.

You can smell it. Nobody ever tells you that part in the songs. The light smells of honey and hot metal, and under that, very faintly, of hair. My nose is better than yours. I smell it still.

And the boy doesn't scream.

That's the part I always remember. I used to think it was courage. Lately I've wondered whether the fire takes the scream first, the way a thief cuts the bell from a camel's neck before he leads it away into the dark.

The light grows until it isn't a boy any more, only two great wings of gold unfolding across the night, wide enough to cover the sands from the Hajar to the sea. Then it falls. Gold rains down over the desert, and everywhere it lands, the grey draws back.

Draws back. I want to be careful with my words, because you'll hold me to them later.

Somewhere below, someone is shouting a name. The wind is listening to every letter.

---

But that is the end.

Let me begin where the boy had no name.

---

You'll want to know what I am. Everyone always does, and they never like the answer.

I am a dog. Don't make that face. I am a saluki, the hunting hound of the Bedouin, the only dog they let sleep inside the tent: sand-coloured, lean as a tent-pole, with feathered ears and a feathered tail. They call me Ghareeb. The stranger. It's the one name of mine they ever got right. I have one amber eye and one silver. We'll get to the silver one later. Don't look at it too long.

I cannot speak to you, or to any man. Everything you'll hear from me is the inside of my head, and you've been invited in only because I'm tired of being alone in it.

What I can do is smell a lie. A lie smells sour, like milk on the turn, like the sweat of a man who has just put a stone in his sleeve. When I smell it, the hair rises along my spine and a growl starts in my chest before I can stop it. When I smell the truth, nothing happens at all. That's the trouble with the truth. It doesn't raise the hackles.

I'm older than every fort in this desert. I'm older than the city under the sand that the old men call Ubar. I have walked beside eleven children who carried the Light, and I have watched nearly every one of them climb the same dune under the same full moon. Children. Notice that word, because the songs don't. *The Phoenix-Born*, *the chosen*, *the children of the 'Anqa'*: very good words for not saying *child*. The Boy of Salt was nine. He asked me, at the top, whether it would be like putting his hand in the cooking fire. I couldn't answer him. I licked his hand. He took that for *no*. I let him.

Some of them were brave. Some of them ran. I'm still angry at the ones who ran. I'm angrier at the sky for asking.

This is the story of the twelfth.

He was the one I loved most. Don't tell the others.

---

The village was called Hilat al-Sarab. The settlement of the mirage. Somebody's grandfather had named it as a joke, and the joke had outlived him.

It sat on the last hard gravel before the great sands of Liwa began, forty houses of mud brick and palm frond and a grove of thirsty date palms, crouched around a single deep well like old men guarding one coin. To the south and east the dunes rolled away gold and red and endless, and they moved. Every night the shamal pushed them, whole hills sliding in the dark with a sound like something very large breathing through its nose. Some mornings there were long dragging furrows in the sand that ended at the doorsteps and went no farther. Nobody talked about it. You only murmured a word to the One who made the sands when you poured water on the ground, so that nothing under it would be scalded, and you went on.

The people feared the sand the way you'd fear a large animal that hadn't decided yet whether to eat you.

They feared the boy a little more.

He was fifteen that year, as near as anyone could guess. The desert had dropped him on the edge of the village after a sandstorm, a baby wrapped in a grey cloth that no woman in Hilat al-Sarab could name the weave of. No mother. No camel tracks. Only a baby, lying in a perfect circle of smooth sand in the middle of the storm's wreckage, crying.

And a dog, lying a short way off, watching.

I'll admit nothing about that.

The village kept him the way you'd keep a stray kid-goat that wandered in during a storm. They fed him when they remembered and kicked him when they didn't. They called him *Laqeet*, foundling, when they were feeling kind, and *the jinni's whelp* when they weren't, which was most days. Nobody ever gave him a real name. You don't name the lamb that's set aside for the feast.

So he gave himself one. He was seven years old, sitting alone on the lip of the well, and he decided he would be called *Theyab*. Wolves. He didn't know where the word came from. It simply arrived in his mouth one evening, already shaped, as if someone had left it there for him. He told no one. A name was the only thing in the world that belonged to him, and he had already learned that the people of Hilat al-Sarab took things.

He told me, of course. Everyone tells the dog.

---

On the morning this story begins, Theyab was underground.

There were ruins an hour's walk into the dunes, half swallowed: the bones of some settlement so old that even the stones had forgotten who stacked them, with a dry channel where a *falaj* had once run. Every few weeks the wind opened a new room.

The village said the jinn held their weddings in the ruins. Which was convenient, because it meant the only person willing to dig through them was the boy they already thought belonged to the jinn. Theyab was a scavenger. It was the only work anyone would give him.

He crouched now in a chamber no taller than a goat, a blade between his teeth: an old khanjar whose silver hilt somebody had long since sold, bound now in rag, rusted along the spine. He was prying at a strip of copper along the foot of a wall. Every time he moved, sand hissed down from the ceiling in a long slow exhale, and then stopped, and then, a moment later, hissed again. As if the room were breathing with him. As if it were learning how.

I lay in the doorway with my nose working. The room smelled of dust and bat and very old smoke, and of something much deeper that I didn't like. Cold. Like the air at the bottom of a well that has been dry a hundred years and has had something lying in it all that time.

"Don't look at me like that," Theyab said around the knife. He talked to me all the time. He was the loneliest boy between the Hajar and the sea, and I was the only creature in the village that didn't throw things. "If I don't get this, we don't eat."

The copper came loose with a crack. Theyab grinned around the blade, the brief, crooked grin of a boy who doesn't get many reasons to smile and doesn't waste them.

Then the wall groaned.

It was long and low and unhappy, a great weight changing its mind. Dust poured from the ceiling. Theyab froze. His hand went to his chest, to the spot just under his collarbone where the mark sat beneath his shirt. He always did that when he was afraid.

The mark was a feather. A birthmark the colour of honey, with fine lines in it like the barbs of a real one. On dark nights it glowed. Faintly, so faintly you could pretend you hadn't seen it. He had spent fifteen years pretending he hadn't seen it.

Under his palm it was warm. Warmer than skin should be.

The wall held. The groaning stopped.

And in the silence afterwards, from somewhere deeper in the ruin, from a part of it no one had dug out in a thousand years, something knocked. Twice. Politely. The way a guest knocks at a tent pole when he wants to be let in and knows he is owed it.

My hackles went up from skull to tail, every hair. Not a lie. Worse. Something down there was pretending to be a guest.

Theyab didn't move for a long time.

Then he tucked the copper into his bag and crawled out into the sun, and he did not look back, and I loved him a little more for having the sense not to.

---

By the time we walked back into Hilat al-Sarab the sun was high and merciless, and his bag held enough copper to buy rice and dates for three days.

It was the qayla. The dead hour, when the sun stands straight overhead and your shadow crawls under your own feet to hide, and the gravel is hot enough to blister a camel's pad. Nothing moves in the qayla that has any sense. The goats lie panting in the strip of shade along the walls. The doves stop. Even the flies give up. And the whole village lies down behind its palm-frond screens to sleep through it, with wet cloths over their faces, until the sun has slid far enough west to let a person live.

Before they lie down, the mothers call their children in.

I had heard it every noon for fifteen years, from every doorway in Hilat al-Sarab, the same call in forty different voices, high and sing-song and not entirely joking:

"*Come inside! Come in, or Hmarat al-Qayla will get you!*"

The Donkey of the Midday. Every child on the edge of the sands knows her. She is a woman from the waist up, they say, with long hair hanging over her face, and from the waist down she is a she-donkey, grey and dusty, with small hard hooves that go *tik, tik, tik* on the hot stones. She walks the empty lanes and the palm gardens in the qayla, when everyone is asleep and no one is watching, and if she finds a child outside she takes him. Some mothers say she eats them. Some say she carries them off into the sands and they come back years later as old men who don't know their own names. The mothers never agree on what she does. They only agree on when.

So at noon, in every house, a mother would lean out into the white glare and call her children's names, one after another, *Mubarak! Moza! Salim! Come in, come in, she's coming*, and the children would come running and squealing and half pleased to be frightened, and the doors would close, and the lanes would empty.

Nobody ever called Theyab.

Not once. Not one mother in forty houses. Nobody leaned out of a doorway at noon and said *Laqeet, come in*, not even to frighten him. You don't call in the lamb set aside for the feast. You don't warn the jinni's whelp about the jinn.

So at the qayla, every day of his life, Theyab was the only child in Hilat al-Sarab who was out.

He'd learned to use it. It was the best hour for scavenging, because nobody was watching. He picked over the rubbish heaps behind the houses for bits of rope and broken pots. He gleaned fallen dates from under the palms in the grove before the owners woke. He drank from the trough by the well when Saqi wasn't there to charge him for it. He lay in the thin noon shade of the grove with his headcloth over his eyes and listened to the village sleep, and once, when he was very small, I watched him stand at the end of an empty lane and say, in a tiny, cracked voice, to no one, "Come in, Theyab. Come in, or she'll get you."

He said it the way the mothers said it. Then he went and sat in the grove by himself.

I have never wanted to bite a whole village more than I wanted to that day.

Today he did as he always did. He crossed the white, empty square with his bag of copper while the lanes ticked and creaked in the heat, and I trotted at his side with my tongue hanging, and the only sound was our feet, and from behind one shuttered window a mother's voice, low, telling her children a story to put them to sleep. I caught the word *hooves*. A child giggled, frightened.

Ghanim was the only grown man in Hilat al-Sarab who stayed awake through the qayla, because the qayla was when a starving boy would sell him copper cheap rather than wait.

He got enough for one.

"Copper's cheap this month," said Ghanim, under the palm-frond roof of his stall, sliding two worn copper annas across the mat. Ghanim was the only man in the village who ever saw silver. He kept his Maria Theresa thalers in a strongbox with a lock the size of a fist, and counted them at night by lamplight like a man telling the beads of his own heart. "Too many scavengers."

There was exactly one scavenger in Hilat al-Sarab, and both of them knew it.

I smelled it rise off him, sour and quick. My hackles lifted. A growl started in my chest, low as a camel's grumble.

"Get that animal away from my stall," said Ghanim, "or I'll have its other eye out too."

Theyab put his hand on my neck, and the growl went back down where it came from. He picked up the coins and turned to go. He never argued with Ghanim. Ghanim's brass scales had a thumb in them, and everyone knew it, and Ghanim was getting very slowly rich on it.

We sat out the rest of the qayla in the grove. When the sun had slid west and the doors opened again and the children came spilling out, blinking and safe, we crossed back through the square to the well.

We made it halfway before someone said, "Look. The jinni's whelp is back from his grave."

Nadim.

There's one in every village. Nadim was Theyab's age, broad in the shoulder and quick in the tongue, the son of Saqi the well-keeper, which made him almost a sheikh's son in a place where every drop came up on Saqi's rope. He sat on the low wall of the well with three other boys around him like flies around a date.

"Find any ghosts down there, Laqeet?" Nadim called, hopping down. "Find your mother? Maybe that's where they found you. In a hole, with the rest of the rubbish."

Theyab kept walking.

There was a scar on Theyab's back, a pale, puckered line just below his left shoulder blade. He'd been six. A crowd of children had decided that the strange boy with the too-light eyes had a jinn in him, and everyone knows you drive out a jinn with stones. Nadim had been five years old that day and standing at the front. Neither of them ever spoke of it. Neither of them ever forgot.

"Hey." A stone hit the gravel by Theyab's foot. "I'm *talking* to you, monster."

That stopped him.

It always did. He could take *Laqeet* and *rubbish*. But *monster* went into him like a hook. Maybe because, on the dark nights, when the feather glowed under his shirt and he lay awake feeling it pulse in time with something that was not his heart, a small and frightened part of him wondered whether it was true.

He turned around.

Nadim was grinning. Waiting. Hoping.

And Theyab said, very quietly, "I'm not a monster."

He said it because it was true. That was the thing about Theyab, and it's the first thing you must know about him, so pay attention: **he could not lie.** Whenever a lie got as far as his tongue, the feather on his chest would burn as if a coal from the coffee hearth had been pressed into it, deep enough that he could smell it, sharp enough to bring water to his eyes. Once, at eight, he'd tried to push a lie through the pain and woken face down in the sand with a blister the shape of a feather.

So he told the truth, always, even when the truth got him hit. Mostly *because* the truth got him hit.

"Not a monster," Nadim repeated, in a high, mocking voice. The boys laughed.

Theyab turned and walked away with his hand pressed flat against his chest, and nobody followed.

I stayed a moment in the shade of a doorway. I watched Nadim's grin fade once Theyab was gone. He looked down at his hands for a moment, as though he wasn't sure whose they were.

I smelled no lie on him. Only a boy, and something else, faint, like a bruise you press to see if it still hurts. People are rarely only one thing. Remember that. You'll need it, later, when you want to hate him.

---

Theyab's tent sat a little way outside the village, on a patch of hard ground between the last house and the first dune. It was patched from nine scraps that had been nine different things: black goat hair, an old sail from a pearling boat, a sack that had once held rice from Basra. The wind came through the seams at night and whistled, and on some nights the whistling sounded almost like words. It was his.

There was a bundle waiting outside it.

It appeared every few days, with no footprints leading to it. A square of faded indigo cloth, knotted at the top. Inside was flatbread, thin and blistered, still faintly warm from the baking plate. And sometimes, on the good days, a twist of waxed paper holding four sticky dates soaked in dark sidr honey from the mountains.

Today was a good day.

Theyab sat down in the doorway of his tent and held the dates in his palm for a long time without eating them. Dates and honey were the finest thing he knew of in the world. He always saved them, for the hardest days, as if he could store sweetness the way you store water against a drought.

He tore the bread in two and gave me half. He always did that. I never once asked.

He didn't know who left the bundles. When he was small he had decided that it must be the One who made the sands, who had made a mistake with him, and left bread to say so.

I knew. I'd followed the smell of the bread back to its oven the very first morning.

It was Umm Hanan.

She was the old widow who baked bread for half the village on an iron plate behind her house, whose husband and two sons had gone out to the pearl banks one summer and come home in nobody's boat. She never once spoke to Theyab in the square or defended him. She was old and alone and afraid, and in Hilat al-Sarab kindness out loud could cost you everything. So she was kind quietly, before the doves woke, every few days for fifteen years. She was never thanked. She did it the way you'd drop a date-stone in wet ground and walk away, not even thinking of a tree.

The world is held together by people like Umm Hanan: too frightened to be brave, good anyway in small, secret ways. But the sand remembers. That's what he used to tell himself on the worst nights, lying alone in his patched tent and listening to the wind try out its words.

*The sand remembers.*

He meant it as a comfort. He didn't think very hard about the other side of it, which is that a thing that remembers everything also forgets nothing. Not a kindness. Not a cheat on a set of scales. Not a name.

---

When the sun went down and the first stars came out, Theyab went to see Old Bakhit.

Bakhit's tent sat at the far edge of the village, farther out even than Theyab's, a long low tent of black goat hair hung with bunches of dried harmal and wild thyme, and with dozens of tiny brass bells on threads. The bells were silent most of the time. They rang only when someone in the tent told a lie.

He was the oldest man anyone in Hilat al-Sarab had ever seen. His skin was brown and creased like a dry wadi bed, his beard was white as salt, and his eyes were milky and pale. He was blind, and had been for longer than anyone could remember. Bakhit means *the lucky one*. That's how the old Arabs named a blind man, gently, the way you'd step around a hole in the dark. The village came to him for fevers and scorpion stings, and left as fast as they could.

Only Theyab came for the stories.

"You're late," Bakhit said, before Theyab had even lifted the tent flap. "And you smell of copper and disappointment. Ghanim cheated you again."

"Two annas," said Theyab.

"For three days' worth of copper." Bakhit clicked his tongue. "Sit. There's tea."

There was always tea. Not coffee: coffee was for guests, and Theyab had never in his life been anybody's guest. Bakhit's tea was black and bitter, smoky with something green he would never name. Theyab drank it every night and hated it every night, and would have died before he admitted that. Hold on to that taste for me. You'll remember it longer than he does.

I lay down across the doorway, the way I always did, with my back to the inside and my nose to the dark.

"Ah. And the stranger." Bakhit's blind face turned toward me. "Evening, Ghareeb."

I thumped my tail on the sand once. I never did that for anyone else. I'll tell you about Bakhit and me another time.

"Which one tonight?" Bakhit asked.

"The Phoenix-Born," said Theyab. He asked for that one every time.

Bakhit smiled. Fond and sad at once, the way a man smiles at a letter from someone he once let drown.

"Again?" he said. "You know it better than I do."

"Tell it anyway."

So he did.

He told of the time before time, when the One who made the sands first made them, and of how a darkness came up out of the deep places, an Ifrit's darkness, born from fear, that made people forget who they were. Whenever the darkness grew too strong, a piece of light was set inside one child, marked with the golden feather of the 'Anqa', the fire-bird that burns and rises. That child would carry the light across the desert to the highest dune in the world. There, under the full moon, the child would set the light free and drive the darkness back into the ground.

"Whenever the darkness grows too strong," Theyab repeated. "How often is that?"

Bakhit's hands paused on his cup. "Often enough," he said.

The bells didn't stir. My hackles lay flat as wet cloth. True. Horribly, precisely true.

"The Phoenix-Born," Bakhit went on softly. "That's what the old songs call them. They rise like the fire-bird from its ashes. They save everyone."

Theyab's eyes were shining. They always shone at this part.

You have to understand what that story meant to a boy who was nameless, thrown away and called a monster. It said that sometimes the forgotten ones are chosen. He had never told anyone his secret dream, except me.

He wanted to be a hero.

Not rich. Not even loved. He wanted to *matter*. He wanted the people who threw stones to look up one day and understand that they had been wrong about him.

I have heard eleven children want that. The sky has never once failed to give it to them. That's the cruelty of it. It always keeps its word.

"What happens to them?" Theyab asked. "After? When the darkness is gone?"

He had never asked that before.

Bakhit went very still. On their threads, the brass bells waited. I watched one of them tremble, very slightly.

"They are remembered," Bakhit said at last.

No bell rang. My hackles never moved. It was true, after all. Just not the whole truth.

*The whole truth* and *true* are two very different animals, and the space between them is where most of the pain in the world lives. Children fall into that space. Bakhit knew exactly what happened to the Phoenix-Born. He chose not to say. But that's later.

"It's late," Bakhit said, and the moment passed. "Go home. And Theyab—"

The boy paused at the tent flap. Bakhit was the only soul who knew that name, and he had never once said it where anyone could hear.

"Remember the saying," said the old man. "Never say your name to the wind."

It's an old saying of the Bedouin, older than the caravan roads. The darkness has ears, they say, and the wind carries everything to it in the end, and a name spoken carelessly can be found by things that collect names. In the old tents, mothers gave their newborns ugly names for the first year, *Jahsh*, little donkey, *Kulaib*, little dog, so that if anything came calling in the night it would pass over a child nobody seemed to love.

It never passed over. But it was a kind thought.

Theyab nodded. He stepped out into the night.

And because he was fifteen, and lonely, and because the only thing in the world that was truly his was a name nobody else would ever say, he climbed to the top of the nearest dune, faced the open desert, and whispered it.

"Theyab."

The wind took it, gently. Too gently. It carried it away south and east over the moving sand, toward the dark.

I stood at the foot of the dune and smelled the wind change.

It came back over the crest from the deep sands, much colder than a desert night has any right to be, and it smelled of nothing. Wind out here always smells of something: camel, salt, smoke from a far fire. This smelled of nothing at all. It brushed past his ear, and in it, very faint, very far away, something said:

*"Theyab."*

In his own voice. Exactly his own voice. Only empty, the way a water-skin is empty, the way a tent is empty after the funeral meal is cleared away. Said back to him the way you'd repeat a word in a language you don't speak, to see how it feels in the mouth.

Then he saw it.

Far out on the horizon, where the gold of the dunes met the black of the sky, there was a line of grey. It was low and flat, like the mist that lies on the coastal sabkha in winter dawns, except the sabkha was four days away. It hadn't been there in fifteen years of nights. It wasn't moving fast. It didn't need to.

It had no edge. Where the dunes ran into it they went on being dunes for a little while, paler and flatter, as if forgetting how.

Above it, the stars were going out.

Not dimming. Going out. One by one, low on the sky, the way lamps go out in a house where someone is walking from room to room, pinching the wicks. Al-Thurayya was rising, the little cluster the herders call the sisters, and as he watched, one of the sisters went out. Then another. And he understood, with the cold, sure understanding you have in nightmares, that the thing on the horizon was not coming toward the village. It was coming toward the place where his name had been spoken.

The feather on his chest began, very faintly, to burn.

And for one instant, no longer than a blink, he wasn't on the dune at all. He was somewhere higher, much higher, with a white moon swollen above him, and there was heat in his mouth, and the smell of honey and hot metal and hair. Then it was gone, and he was on an ordinary dune with his heart going like a trapped bird. He thought he'd imagined it. He was very good, back then, at that.

He did not see me at the foot of the dune behind him, standing with my legs braced and every hair on my body lying flat and still. Not one hackle. Everything about that moment was true, and I have never hated my own nose quite so much.

Twelve. I scratched it into the sand with one claw, the way I always do, one stroke for each of them, and then I lay down on the marks before he could see them.

The grey was coming. The old women would have had a word for it, if any of them had still been alive who remembered: *al-Ghasaq*. The dark that comes after the light has gone and before anything else has arrived. The Ifrit's breath.

And this time, it had learned his name. It was practising.

---

*End of Chapter One*
