# Chapter Nineteen
## Tal Moreeb

> *He'll walk where the dunes write the words of the sky,*
> *And the light will be born on the night he will die.*
> — from the song of the fire-bird; the verse the mothers of Liwa will not sing aloud

---

Umm Shamma found us on the fort stairs as we were leaving.

She'd come up out of the kitchens with a ladle like a club and a long red scratch down her cheek where something had reached for her in the dark. Her burqa was gone. She saw the Sheikh's ring on the cord at Theyab's neck and understood everything at once. She didn't cry. She didn't ask. She pushed a small cloth bundle into his hands, took his face between her two enormous palms, and kissed his forehead hard, the way you'd stamp a seal into wax.

She pulled back with her lips blistered. His skin was that hot. She didn't say a word about it.

Then she went back down toward the dark of the kitchens and the hatch, with a lamp in her other hand, to look for a stable boy among the dead. I never saw her again. I have never asked what found her first.

Inside the bundle were dates. Rolled in sesame and soaked in syrup.

---

We rode out through the eastern gate, Sharood in front and Theyab behind him on the pale camel, and me across the boy's lap like a sack of grain, bleeding quietly into his shirt.

The emptied still stood where they'd stopped, every one facing east. As we passed, they began to say their last words again, very softly, all together, so that we went out of Ubar through a long low murmur of *bar the door* and *Mother, I'm here* and *where's the oil*. Then, one by one, they stopped speaking and only smiled.

Theyab unwrapped the bundle and held the dates in his palm.

He ate one. He held one out over Sharood's shoulder, and after a moment his father took it. Then he gave one to me, and I ate it, though I prefer a hare and always will.

"This is the hardest day," Theyab said. "I don't think there's a harder one."

My hackles lay still.

Tal Moreeb rose ahead of us until it filled half the sky, and its flanks went on moving. Up. Pause. Down.

"Father," said Theyab.

It was the first time he'd called him that. I felt Sharood go rigid in the saddle.

"I forgive you."

Sharood didn't turn round. His shoulders shook. "You can't. I ran. If I hadn't run, none of this— your brother, the grey, all of it. You can't forgive that."

"I just did," said Theyab. "And I can't lie. So."

I lay across his knees with every hair on me flat and soft as a puppy's, and Sharood felt it through the saddle, I think. He reached back without looking, and Theyab took his hand, and they rode like that all the way to the foot of the dune. Theyab's hand was so hot that Sharood's palm blistered. He didn't let go.

---

The emptied were waiting.

Not only the ones from Ubar. All of them. They had come from the whole of the sands in the night, from the grey wastes and the emptied villages, from Hilat al-Sarab and the caravan roads and the pearling towns on the coast, and they stood in a ring around the foot of Tal Moreeb a hundred deep. Herders. Divers. Old women. Children. Thousands of white faces turned up toward the top of the dune, all smiling the same smile, all humming the Nameless's two notes so low that the sound came up through the sand into my paws. It was like standing on the skin of a drum at a wedding where everyone is dead.

They parted for us like water.

I saw the oil-seller of Ubar among them, still holding up his one finger. I saw old Sheikh Mazyad with sand in his beard. I didn't see Nadim. I looked.

We climbed. The sand slid under our feet, and under the sand something moved with the breathing, so that every step went down a little further than it should. Halfway up, Theyab turned and picked me up without a word and carried me the rest of the way, across his shoulders, the way he'd carried me out of the dhow. His skin singed my coat. I let him. I was too tired to be proud.

And then we were at the top, and I looked up, and it was the vision.

I had seen it in his eyes every time he healed. Here it was, every piece falling into place like the last stones of a tomb.

The moon hung full and white above Tal Moreeb, so close and so swollen it looked like something that had fed. The crest was pale sand, so cold after his skin that it ached. Below us, on every side, the sands went away silver and grey to the edge of the world.

Wazir Muhaisen waited at the far end of the crest, with Zaid. Around him stood twenty of the Nameless, white masks shining like small moons, and a fire of stolen lamp-oil burning in a ring of stones. A little apart stood the Seventh, the crack in his mask so wide now that half his real face showed through, the skin around it raw where he'd been tearing at it.

And behind the wazir, bare-faced among all those masks, stood the Eldest. Grey beard. Broken nose. His long grey blade had been replaced with another. He stood where he'd been told to stand. His lips were still moving.

Zaid stood quite still in Muhaisen's grip, eyes open, humming.

And between us and them, the grey was gathering.

---

It rose out of the sand like smoke from a fire put out with water. It thickened. It took a shape.

A boy. Smaller than Theyab, barefoot and thin, in a goat-herd's shirt, with a herder's stick. Everything about him was grey. Only his eyes were different: black, completely black, like two holes cut into the night, and if you looked too long you could see things moving far down in them, small and pale, like faces at the bottom of a well. Behind him, his shadow on the moonlit sand was not a boy's. It was far too large, and it had too many joints, and it had horns.

I knew that face. I had known it before there were towns on this coast, when it was brown and laughing and smelled of goats and woodsmoke.

*Hello, Ghadban*, I said. Not aloud. I can't say anything aloud. But he is jinn now, or the jinn's house, and jinn hear me. They always have.

"Hello, old dog." A child's voice, and under it a thousand others, a beat behind, and under those, something deep that wasn't a voice at all. "You've come to watch another one burn."

*I've come to watch one choose. It's different. You'd know, if you'd done it.*

The grey boy smiled. The smile went a little too far back on both sides.

Then he turned his black eyes on Theyab.

"Look at them," Ghadban said softly, pointing his stick down at the emptied. "That one threw stones at you when you were six. Those ones called you monster and watched you starve. And you'd burn for them?"

Theyab's Light flickered grey at the edges.

"And here's what the dog won't tell you," said Ghadban, gentle as sand. "He can't, can he? So convenient. Ask him how many. Not bearers. *Children*. Ask him how old the Boy of Salt was. Ask him why, every time, it's a child. Ask him what's waiting on the other side of the Light with its mouth open. It isn't me, Theyab. I'm only the one who said no."

Theyab looked at me.

And every hair on my body lay down flat. I couldn't stop it. I have never been able to stop it.

I saw it go into him.

Then the voice under Ghadban's voice spoke on its own.

The boy's mouth didn't move. His lips stayed in that too-wide smile. But the deep thing that had been a beat behind every word came up to the front, and the sand on the crest shivered under it, and the masks along the crest bowed without being told.

"*Or,*" it said.

It was the voice from the well. Warm. Grandfatherly. Pleased with you.

"*Or you could live.*"

Below us, the emptied stopped humming.

"*Let the shepherd go, and the dog, and the little lamps, and take the other road. I made a vow once, under the city, in the first hundred years of my prison. I will keep it for you. Whoever frees me, I will make rich. Pearls by the basket, little light. Camels to the edge of the sky. A house of your own in Al Ain, with a falaj through the courtyard and a lime tree, and no one ever again to throw a stone. And long life. Longer than Bakhit's. Long enough to see every man who called you monster laid in the sand, and to stand at their graves and remember their names when no one else does.*"

It was beautiful. It was the most generous thing anyone had ever offered him. I watched him hear it. I watched the boy who'd lived off the rubbish heap of Hilat al-Sarab see a lime tree and a courtyard and a door that was his.

And the growl came up out of me like a rockslide.

Not a warning. Not low. It came from the very bottom of me, where the jinn half lives, and it shook the sand off my coat, and my lips came back from my teeth so far my gums split. Every hair on my body stood. I have growled at a great many lies in my life. I had never growled at one that big.

I had heard the rest of it, the night before, from the bottom of a well. I knew what came after *rich*.

So did Ghadban. For one breath the grey boy's face slipped. The smile went small and young and frightened, and his black eyes came to me, and I saw a shepherd boy kneeling on a ledge in a limestone cave a very long time ago, with a cracked seal in his two hands, waiting for the riches and the long life that were going to save him.

Then the smile came back, too wide again, and the deep voice laughed, softly, as though I'd told a joke at a wedding.

Theyab looked at me. He looked at my back. He'd learned to read it better than any bearer before him.

"No," he said. His voice shook. "That one's a lie."

"*It was true once,*" said the voice, almost wistful.

My hackles stayed up. They know the difference between once and now.

"Then burn," said Ghadban, in the boy's voice again. "And after, they'll light a lamp. One more in a row of eleven. In a hundred years no one will remember what it was for. Bearers' names come off the walls like the names in Ubar. You'll be forgotten." A whisper. "You'll be forgotten, Theyab. Just like you always were."

My hackles went up so hard it hurt, from the base of my skull to my tail, and I growled from the bottom of my chest.

But it didn't matter what I did. It was the oldest fear he had, the one he'd carried since he was a baby crying in the sand. *Nobody will remember me.* And now, beside it, a new one I had put there with my silence: *and something is hungry for me, and always has been.*

His Light went grey all the way through.

"There's a way out," said Wazir Muhaisen.

He stepped forward with one hand on Zaid's shoulder and the thin grey blade in the other. His soft voice carried along the crest the way it had filled the majlis.

"Give me your name. That's all. Say it to the wind. Your true name, the one in your blood. Ghadban will take it, and the Light with it, and it's over. No burning. You'll simply forget. And then everyone will forget, every loss, every grief, every dead son." Something moved in his faded eyes, far down. "I promise you, child, it doesn't hurt. I burned my own name to stop remembering. Forgetting is the only mercy there is."

My hackles rose. Fell. Rose halfway, and stuck there.

He believed it. That's the worst kind of lie, little one: the kind the liar has told himself so long it smells like truth.

"Give me your name," said Muhaisen.

And Theyab, grey and shaking, opened his mouth.

---

Bells.

Small brass bells, a great many of them, ringing faintly somewhere below on the slope. Even the hum of the emptied faltered.

Someone was climbing Tal Moreeb on hands and knees through the sliding sand. Around his waist hung a belt of tiny truth bells that chimed with every movement, and all the way down the slope the emptied had turned to watch him pass, and none of them had touched him.

Old Bakhit.

For three hundred years he'd been bound to the edge of the Empty Quarter, the price of the night he ran. To leave it was to die.

He had crossed the sands anyway.

He was dying as he climbed. You could see it. His beard was thin as smoke. His skin had gone papery and was splitting at the knuckles, and what came out of the splits wasn't blood but a fine pale dust. He climbed toward our voices as if they were a fire on a cold night.

He reached the crest and knelt, gasping. Ghadban turned to him.

"Bakhit," said the grey boy warmly. "My old friend."

Every bell on Bakhit's belt rang at once.

"I was never your friend," said Bakhit. "You only told me what I wanted to hear, so I'd run. I've had three hundred years in the dark to work that out." He turned his blind face. "Boy. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"I asked you once, in my tent. *Would you die for them?* You couldn't answer." The old man swallowed. "Someone asked me the same, on this sand, three hundred years ago. I said no, and I ran, and the last thing I ever saw was the top of this dune. I hid from you that bearers die, because I was ashamed. And I hid something else. When I said no, up here, I *heard* it, child. I heard it be disappointed." His hands clenched in the sand. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

Not one bell rang. I crept to him on my belly and put my head in his lap, and his hand found my ears the way it used to, three hundred years ago, when he was a boy with a feather on his chest.

"Don't answer for me," whispered Bakhit. "Don't answer for them. Don't answer for whatever's listening. Answer for you."

Theyab stood very still on the top of Tal Moreeb.

He looked at Ghadban. He looked at me, and at my back, which had told him a thing about the Light he would never now stop knowing. He looked down at the emptied. He looked at Zaid, humming, forgetting his own name a little more with every breath. He looked at his father.

Then he untied Old Bakhit's truth bell from his belt and held it up in front of him, so everyone could see.

"Yes," he said.

The bell didn't ring.

And the grey went out of his Light like a shadow when the sun comes up. It turned gold. A deeper gold than I had ever seen, the gold of wild honey from the Hajar held up to the light. It was beautiful. I want you to know that. It was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.

It was also the colour of a coal just before it falls apart.

Ghadban took a step backward. His enormous shadow took two.

---

"Seventh," said Muhaisen sharply. "Cut him."

Saqr raised his twin grey blades, the only things in the world that could cut the Light. The blades were shaking, and through the great crack in his mask one pale eye stared, wet and wide.

He was humming. Not the chant. The fire-bird song. He didn't know he was doing it.

Theyab walked straight up to him, until the grey blades were a finger's width from his chest and the steel began to frost and smoke at once where the Light met it.

"You held me," he said. "When I was born. I saw it. You were five, and you held me, and you were scared you'd drop me."

The blades trembled. Then, slowly, Saqr lowered them.

Theyab walked past him to Muhaisen. The wazir backed away, dragging Zaid, and laid the grey blade against the boy's throat. Theyab didn't stop. He put his hand over Muhaisen's hand on the grip, and the gold went into it.

Muhaisen cried out and let go. His hand was grey to the wrist where the Light had touched it, crazed with fine cracks like an old glazed pot. The blade fell into the sand.

Zaid stopped humming. He blinked. He said, in a small, astonished voice, "Zaid," as if he'd found it in a pocket.

Then he ran.

"*Eldest!*" Muhaisen shrieked. His soft voice was gone. "Cut him down!"

The Eldest stood directly in the boy's path. Bare-faced, with a fresh grey blade in his fist and fifteen years of obedience in his arms. I saw the blade come up. I saw it rise to the height of a ten-year-old's neck, and Zaid saw it, and couldn't stop, the sand sliding under his bare feet.

The Eldest looked down at him.

Then he looked past him, at Theyab, who had stood over him on the fort stairs a few hours before with a golden blade at his throat and said *go home*.

He stepped aside.

He just stepped aside, one pace, onto the slope, and let the boy go by, so close the boy's sleeve brushed his arm. Then he opened his hand and let the grey blade fall into the sand, point down, and stood there with his empty hands at his sides, looking at them as if they belonged to someone else. His lips were still moving. I was close enough now to hear. It was a woman's name. I think it was his mother's.

Zaid ran straight to Sharood, and Sharood caught him, and the boy buried his face in the grey wrappings and shook.

My hackles had not risen at the Eldest once. Not on the stairs. Not here. I don't know what that tells you. I know what it told me.

Theyab knelt before Zaid. He untied Hazza's ring from beside the silver knot and pressed it into Zaid's hand. It left a red mark on the small palm. Zaid closed his fingers on it anyway.

"Your father wanted you to have this. And so would Hazza'."

Zaid was ten. He wasn't stupid.

"Are you going to die?"

"Yes."

"Like Hazza'?"

Theyab thought about it. "No," he said. "Hazza' didn't get to choose. I do."

---

Sharood let go of Zaid and took his son's hands, and didn't flinch at the heat.

"Let me," he said.

It was not the voice of the Veiled Rider.

"Let me do it. It was mine before it was yours, and I ran. Give it me. I'm old, I'm nothing, let it have me instead. I ran so you could live. Don't make that for nothing. Please, son. Not you. Not you."

Theyab shook his head. He couldn't speak for a moment.

"It doesn't want you," he said, very gently. "It wants the Light, and the Light's in me." He tightened his grip. "And it wasn't for nothing. You left me in the sand, and the desert kept me, and I grew up alone, so I know what it's like to be the one nobody wants. That's why I can do this. It all meant something. Even the bad parts. *Especially* the bad parts."

Every hair on me lay down.

I'll tell you what I thought, little one. I thought: *it meant something. And I no longer know whether that's a comfort or the worst thing in the world.*

Sharood bowed his head over their joined hands. I have never seen anything as brave as what he did next.

He let go.

One finger at a time, slowly, like a man lowering something precious into a deep well. The skin of his palms stuck to his son's hands and tore, and he let go anyway. When the last finger slid free, he stepped back beside Zaid and Bakhit and didn't move again.

The hardest choice of his life. He made it.

---

Theyab came to me last.

I was lying in the sand by Bakhit, too tired to stand, my coat dark with my own blood, my silver eye aching the way it always aches near the end. He knelt so our faces were level. Heat came off him like an open bread oven.

"You lied to me once," he said. "With your whole body."

I didn't look away.

"It's going to hurt," he said.

My hackles lay flat. More than anything has ever hurt.

"It'll matter."

Flat.

"Ghadban's right, isn't he. Something's waiting."

And my hackles rose halfway up my back, and stayed there, and slowly lay down again, and rose. Neither. I didn't know. Some truths are too big even for a dog's back.

He watched them a long moment. Then he smiled, the small, crooked, rusty smile he never wasted, and put his forehead against mine. It singed the fur between my ears. I'll carry the mark until the world ends.

"Look after them," he said.

Never in all my long life have I wished so hard that I could speak. I've wished it every night since.

He walked to the very end of the crest, where the dune fell away on every side into the silver dark, and stood under the full moon with his back to us all.

"I always wanted to be a hero," he said quietly. To the boy in the patched tent, maybe. "Like in the stories the old men tell."

He took a breath.

"I am the hero."

It wasn't a boast. It was only true. I have never lain so still.

Then he lifted his face to the moon, and to whatever was beyond it, and he said the last words he would ever say.

"I'm coming, Hazza'."

---

And here I have to tell it the way I told it at the very beginning, because it has never stopped happening. It's happening now, somewhere behind my eyes, and I can still smell it.

He is burning.

He stands at the top of Tal Moreeb with his arms open, as if he's about to catch something falling out of the sky. The fire doesn't start at his hands. It starts in his chest, under the mark, the small golden feather that has glowed in the dark his whole life. It splits the way a date stone splits in the ground, from the inside. I hear it crack. Light comes through every golden line that every healing left on him, and the lines split wider, and his skin curls back from them like palm leaf in a fire. It runs down his arms and up his throat. It comes out of his eyes, and his eyes go with it.

The smell reaches me. Honey and hot iron, and under that, his hair. I will never stop smelling it. A dog can't.

And Muhaisen lunges.

Grey robes, grey hand, grey eyes wide and starving. He has waited his whole life for this light, and he throws himself into it with both hands, grabbing, as if he could tear it out of the boy's chest and keep it.

"*Mine,*" he says.

It isn't. It never could be. The Light can't be taken. It can only be given.

It turns grey in his hands. The gold he's grasping goes dull, then ashen, and the grey runs up his fingers and wrists and arms like frost climbing a window, cracking as it climbs. For a single moment his face changes. He sees something in the stolen light: a small boy, and a brother, and a name he burned long ago. *Helper*, it meant. His mouth opens to say it.

"Mu—"

His jaw goes first.

It doesn't fall. It crumbles, like an old mud-brick wall in the first rain in forty years, and the rest of him follows from the face down, still kneeling, still reaching. The last of him to go is his hands, clenched in the light, trying to hold on. The wind lifts the dust off Tal Moreeb and carries it into the dark, the way it carries away a name said carelessly.

There is nothing left of him. Not even the name. He never finished it.

And the boy doesn't scream.

That's the part I always remember. He is burning to the bone and he doesn't scream, and I have never understood how, and I have never wanted to.

He looks down. Below him, all across the sands, the dunes are moving, shifting and folding and rising, writing in letters miles high. The same shapes they wrote for him on the first nights after Hilat al-Sarab, when he couldn't read a word and I couldn't have told him what they said if I'd wanted to.

He can read them now. A young man with a broken sword taught him, in the sand, with a stick, laughing the whole time.

*Nun*, the dunes write, from right to left, the way he learned. *Alif. Ha. Dad.*

*NAHIDH.* In the old tongue, *the one who rises.*

His true name, the one the One who made the sands wrote for him before he was born, the one the wind could never find, because the desert kept it safe all this time. The way you keep something safe that you mean to use. He reads it, the last thing he will ever read, with eyes that are more light than eye now, and I see what's left of his mouth smile.

Then he's looking at something only he can see. But I see it too, for a moment, in the light pouring out of him.

Bir al-Ghina', under a thin moon. The sand humming. A young man on the edge of the well with half a sword across his knees, a scarred jaw and a crooked grin and dark eyes, laughing at him the way he always did. Holding out his hand.

*You took your time,* says Hazza'.

And behind Hazza', in the dark beyond the well, standing in a row as if they've waited a very long time, I see the others. Eight small figures, the eight who gave themselves. Some barely taller than the well's edge. A boy of nine with salt-white hair. A smith's apprentice with burned hands. A girl holding a weaver's shuttle. All looking at Theyab. None of them smiling. Their faces are patient and gentle and very, very tired, the faces of children who have stood in a line too long.

Theyab laughs, the way he laughed the first time, by a fire, when a sheikh's son tried to eat a scorpion. He takes the hand. The row of children opens to let him in, and he takes his place at the end of it.

There's a space beside him. An empty space at the very end of the row, the width of one small child.

I see it every time. I have never once been able to look away from it.

The light keeps growing until it isn't a boy anymore. It's a shape, two great wings of gold unfolding across the dark, the wings of the 'anqa from the old song, wide enough to cover the dunes from one edge of the sky to the other.

Then it falls.

Gold rains down over the sands, and everywhere it lands, the grey draws back.

Somewhere below, someone is shouting a name.

It's his father, on his knees in the sand a little way down the crest, his arms around a crying boy, his raw hands, his face turned up into the falling gold. Shouting it over and over, the only name he was ever allowed to call his son.

"*Theyab! Theyab!*"

And the wind takes it, gently, and carries it out over the whole of the sands. Not to the dark this time.

There isn't any dark left.

That's what I believed, that night. I want you to know I believed it. For one whole night, I did.

---

*End of Chapter Nineteen*
