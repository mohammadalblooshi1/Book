# Chapter Seven
## The Caravan and the Beast

> *When the sky goes dark at noon, couch your camel and cover your face. If you hear your mother calling from inside the dark, do not answer, and do not turn around.*
> — a saying of the caravan men between Buraimi and the coast, older than any caravan still on the road

---

You smell a caravan long before you see it.

Camel, mostly. Cud and camel piss. Then dung smoke, and cardamom, and dried shark from the coast, and sixty men who haven't washed in a month because water is money. After a wadi full of voices, it smelled like hope.

We found the track in the middle of the morning, worn by a thousand years of feet and marked with little cairns, running north and west toward the sands and the sea. And on it, strung out like beads on a long brown thread, came the caravan of Abu Kharbash.

Sixty camels roped nose to tail in strings of seven, carrying dates from Buraimi to the coast. Riders with old Martini rifles across their backs. A merchant from Sharjah on a white riding camel with a hooded saker falcon on his fist. And two salukis trotting along the flanks, who looked at me as I came over the ridge and then looked away, quickly, the way dogs look away from something they'd rather not know about.

Theyab watched it pass with his mouth slightly open. I sneezed at him. It's the closest a saluki comes to sarcasm.

---

Abu Kharbash was a broad, bald, sun-blackened man with a beard dyed orange with henna and a voice that could have cracked flint. *Father of scratches*: one cheek was ploughed with old white lines where a leopard had caught him in the mountains as a boy. He looked Theyab up and down the way he'd look at a camel he suspected of being lame.

"Name?"

"In my village they called me Laqeet."

"Foundling. That's not a name, that's a complaint." Abu Kharbash spat. "One of my boys ran off at the last well. You'll work for water and dates and a place by the fire. You don't like it, the desert's that way." He squinted down at me. "Now *that's* a dog."

He crouched and took my muzzle in one hard hand and turned my head to the light. Then he saw my eyes. One amber. One clouded silver, like a coin left in the sea.

He let go rather fast, and wiped his hand on his *thawb*. "My grandmother would say there's a jinn sitting behind that eye. I'll give you twenty thalers for him anyway."

"He isn't mine to sell," said Theyab.

Abu Kharbash laughed and stood, already bellowing at someone about a loose saddle girth. "Keep him away from my falcon," he called back. "And at dusk, *couch the camels in a ring*, all of you, or by my beard I'll leave you for the wolves."

*Couch the camels in a ring.* He said it at every halt, the same way, like a man saying his prayers. You'll see why I remember.

The ring was for wolves, he told the new boys, and for raiders. And for the other things. A man who stepped outside the ring at night to relieve himself took a friend, and a stick, and he didn't go past the last camel. If anyone called to you from out in the dark by name, you looked at their feet before you answered. If they had no feet you could see, you didn't answer at all. And if you saw a lantern on the dunes where no lantern should be, you lay down with your face in the sand and counted your sins until morning. "My cousin's son followed a light out of the ring at Bir Hamad," Abu Kharbash said, "and we found his sandals in the morning, side by side, neat as a bride's, and all round them, in the sand, the hooves of a donkey. And we had no donkey." He spat in the fire. "Couch the camels in a ring."

---

Theyab worked.

He'd spent his life doing the jobs no one else wanted, and a caravan is nothing but those. He loaded bales and gathered dung for the fires and never complained. He kept his ghutra wound high around his face and his shirt tied close at the throat, so nobody would see the faint glow of the feather mark after dark.

He was happy. I don't think he knew it. I tried hard to remember his face like that, the way you'd press a flower in a book you knew was going to burn.

On the second night a boy came and sat beside him at the fire. Jasir, the youngest of the camel men, sixteen or so, with a gap in his front teeth and far too much laugh. He rode a milk-pale she-camel called Shaha and talked about her the way some men talk about their mothers. He held out a cloth of dates without being asked, and after a moment Theyab took one.

"You're from the Liwa villages," Jasir said. "I can tell. You walk like the sand's going to bite you."

"It does, sometimes."

Jasir laughed, easy and loud and unworried.

They talked late. Or Jasir talked, and Theyab listened. A lot of grey out east this season, Jasir said. A well-keeper's family near Ibri found round a cold hearth with their coffee cups in their laps, smiling at nothing, saying *more coffee, more coffee, more coffee*. And deep in the sands, if you believed the old men, the sheikh of Ubar had thrown his own eldest son out for hiding one of the emptied in the palace and trying to cure him.

"You can't cure the grey," Jasir said. "You take them out to the sands and leave them, quick, before they start smiling at you."

Theyab's hand had gone to his chest. I lay with my nose under my tail. Every word was true as Jasir knew it. My hackles stayed flat. I've never been sure that's a comfort.

"Liwa," Jasir said after a while, poking the fire. "Which village?"

"Hilat al-Sarab."

Jasir stopped poking. Then he laughed, but the laugh was different, shorter, with a little edge of salt in it. "The mirage," he said. "Of course it is. Of course you'd come from there." He threw the stick on the fire. "You want to know why I ride camels? I'm a coast boy. Ras al-Khaimah. My father had a boat. Not a big one. A *shasha*, palm-rib and rope, the kind you can carry on two men's shoulders. Fished the shallows inside the reef, and the creek mouths, and when the tide went out across the flats he'd walk out after crabs with me on his back."

Theyab said nothing. He was good at that. People told him things.

"I was eight. Nine. He was sick, that winter, lying in the house coughing, and I took the boat out alone because I wanted to bring him something. I wanted him to see me come in with fish." Jasir shrugged with one shoulder, the way men do when the thing they're shrugging off is heavy. "And out past the creek mouth, on the water, where the sea goes flat and white in the noon heat, I saw gold."

"Gold?"

"A shine. Like a whole bank of pearls lying on top of the water. Like someone had spilled a merchant's chest out there and left it floating. And it moved. Not far. Only a little, every time I got close. So I rowed. And it moved. And I rowed. And every time, it was right there, a few more strokes, so bright it hurt, and I thought, my father will never cough again, we'll buy a real boat, we'll buy two." He laughed again. Not much of a laugh. "My grandmother had told me a hundred times. Khattaf Rafai. The Snatcher. He shows the fishermen whatever they want most, gold, pearls, a fat shoal, a woman on the water, and he pulls it away a stroke at a time, and they row after it until they've no land behind them and no water in the jar. I knew that story better than my own name. I rowed anyway."

"How did you get home?"

"I didn't. Not in the boat." Jasir held up his hands and looked at them in the firelight, as if checking they'd come back. "The shine went out at sunset, all at once, like a lamp. There was nothing there. Just sea, every side. I'd no idea where the land was. The boat filled in the night, and I held onto it, and a pearling dhow coming in from the banks found me two days later with my lips cracked open and the gulls already interested. My father's boat they never found. He sold his nets to pay the dhow's captain for my rescue, and he died that summer without one." He picked up a date and didn't eat it. "My uncle took me inland to his camels the next year. He said a boy who'll row after Khattaf Rafai had better live where there's no water to row on."

"But there are mirages out here," said Theyab.

"There are." Jasir grinned, and the gap in his teeth showed. "That's why I don't look at them. I don't trust anything that shines. Not water on the gravel, not a lamp on a dune, not a merchant's silver, not a pretty face at a well. If it shines, I keep my eyes on Shaha's ears and I keep riding." He tapped his temple. "You'd do well to learn it, mirage-boy. The things that shine are the ones that want you to follow."

I was watching Theyab's hand. It had crept back to his chest, to the place where the feather mark was faintly warm under his shirt, where, after dark, if anyone looked, it shone.

He didn't say anything. He wound his ghutra a little higher round his face, and they talked about camels after that.

---

On the third day, at noon, the falcon wouldn't fly.

It went up three wingbeats and came straight back to the merchant's fist and sat there with its feathers flattened, shivering, its head turned east.

Then the camels knelt. No one told them to. All down the line they sank onto the gravel, groaning, and stretched their necks flat along the ground and closed their eyes. The way camels lie for the knife at a wedding feast.

The two salukis crept under the nearest camel with their tails between their legs. I stood out on the open gravel and howled, long and high and broken, the warning-cry I hadn't given in a hundred years.

The wind had stopped. Out here the wind is never still. Now the air hung hot and dead, and the sky was clear in every direction but the east, where, low on the horizon, there was a line of black.

At first the caravan men took it for a sandstorm, and relaxed a little. A sandstorm you know. Abu Kharbash was already bellowing: *couch the camels in a ring.*

But a storm runs *with* the wind. This came out of air with no wind in it, and it was black, like the inside of a closed eyelid. And as it came, it drew itself up. The middle rose, and kept rising, and became a pillar.

The little dust devils dance across the flats every summer afternoon, and the old women say every one has a jinn riding in it, and spit, and call them *zawba'a*.

This was a zawba'a the height of a mountain.

It stood on a single spinning foot that tore the gravel up in a roaring skirt, and went up from there, black and turning, until its top was lost above the sun. It *walked*. It came across the plain the way a man walks, swaying from side to side, and it was silent. The silence came ahead of it like a smell.

And the light was going.

Noon, and the sun at its height, and the light was draining out of the world. The colours went first. The red of the camel blankets. The orange of Abu Kharbash's beard. Everything went the colour of old bone. Then the shadows went, all at once, as if something enormous had put its mouth to the sky and was drinking the light out of it like milk from a bowl.

"When the sky goes dark at noon," said Jasir, very softly.

I took Theyab's sleeve in my teeth and pulled him away, so hard the cloth tore. Nobody else moved. Nobody ever does.

---

It hit us like the end of the world.

The wind came first, a wall of it, knocking Theyab flat and rolling me twice across the gravel. Then the dark came down over the caravan like a lid on a cooking pot.

And inside it, something moved.

I have seen most of the jinn's children. I'd seen nothing like this. No one who has seen it all at once has kept their name.

It was the whirlwind, and it was something *in* the whirlwind. Its body was the black wind itself, spinning and folding on itself like a great gut, and in the folds were the suggestion of arms, huge and slow and jointed in too many places, trailing out of the spin and feeling across the ground the way a blind man feels for a dropped coin. One passed over us. It was *heavy*, the way grief is heavy, and where it brushed the top of a fallen bale the dates inside went grey and soft and slumped like wet bread.

High up in the spin there were lights. Red ones. Dozens, scattered across the black, blinking slowly and not together. Some were large as a well-mouth. Some were small and set in pairs, at about the height of a man's head, and there were a great many of them, and they went round and round with the wind, round and round, and every time they came past they were looking.

Eyes. All the eyes of everyone it had ever taken, still open in there, still looking out.

And below them, in the very heart of the spin, a hole.

Not a mouth. A mouth has lips. This was a great round silent place where even the darkness was darker, the still eye at the centre of the whirlwind, and everything went into it. The roaring wind went in and didn't come out. Gravel flowed toward it in long hissing streams, like water to a drain. My ears popped. Something warm ran out of my nose. And the hole made a sound, at last, the only sound I ever heard it make.

It *breathed in*.

---

The front of the caravan went first. The dark closed over Abu Kharbash's kneeling camel, and when it lifted there was smooth gravel, raked as neat as the floor of a mosque.

Then the camels behind. The zawba'a took the end of the rope and *pulled*, and they went up one after another like beads off a string, still kneeling, eyes still closed, turning slowly in the air as they rose into the black. Not a sound out of any of them.

The men screamed. And then the screams changed.

They didn't stop the way screams stop when someone dies. They *frayed*. A man would be shrieking for his brother, and halfway through the brother's name the scream would forget what it was for. It would trail off into a puzzled little note, then a hum, then nothing.

That's what a zawba'a does, a great one, one that has fed long enough. It doesn't eat you. Eating would be kind. It swallows you up into the spin, and inside the spin is the Ifrit's grey, all of it at once, like being drowned in a well of everyone's forgetting. Whatever comes out the other side, if anything does, walks on your legs and doesn't know your face.

Theyab was on his feet. I don't know how. His ghutra had torn away and the feather mark was blazing through his shirt, so bright it lit a circle of gravel round him in the dark.

And high up in the spin, the red eyes turned toward it.

All of them. At once. The great ones and the small ones in their pairs. They stopped going round with the wind. They held still while the black turned around them, and they looked at him.

I put myself in front of him, a dog the weight of a goat snarling at a mountain, and behind my silver eye something woke and *pulled* against my skin like a camel against its hobble. *Let me out. I could hold it.* I didn't. I was more afraid of that than of the zawba'a.

Behind me, the light in Theyab's hands came grey.

It came grey and thin and terrified, a dead lamp-wick in a dead wind. I could feel the fear go down into him, like a stone dropped down a well that never hits the bottom.

And then the voices started.

Soft and close, the way the wadi had spoken, but these weren't echoes. These were the people it had taken, and it was *wearing* them.

"*Couch the camels in a ring*," said Abu Kharbash, very calmly, from the black. "*Couch the camels in a ring. Couch the camels in a ring.*"

"*Where is my*—" said a man's voice. "*Where is my*— *Where is my*—"

And a child's: the merchant's little daughter, going home to her mother on the coast. I never knew her name. "*Yumma, it's dark. Yumma, it's dark. Yumma, it's dark.*"

Last words. That's all that's left of you, inside it. Your last words, going round and round in the black like a bell that won't stop swinging.

Through the flying sand I saw Shaha down, kicking, and Jasir was pinned beneath her by one leg, screaming the only name he had for Theyab. *Laqeet! Laqeet!* And the foot of the whirlwind was sliding toward him across the gravel like a tide that knows exactly where you've buried your feet.

Theyab ran.

He ran into the wind, toward the boy who'd shared dates with him, and in those few steps the light went from grey to gold, like a coal catching. He threw himself down over Jasir and the kicking camel with his arms spread wide, and the light came out of him in a dome. A shell of gold, closing over the three of them like cupped hands over a moth.

The zawba'a came down on top of it.

---

I was outside the light. I want you to know that. I lay flat in the lee of a dead camel with my silver eye screaming at me, and I stayed small. I saw the whirlwind stand on the little gold glow, its spinning foot grinding down until the gold went thin as a soap bubble, and the eyes crowded down the spin toward it, round and round.

He told me much later, as if a dog could tell anyone, that from inside the dome it wasn't black at all. Close up it was *grey*, soft and folded and turning, like the inside of a lung. And pressed against the gold, whirling past and catching, were faces. Hundreds. Abu Kharbash, his orange beard gone the colour of ash. The little girl, her small hands flat on the gold in front of his face as the wind dragged her past and brought her round again, saying *Yumma, it's dark* with no sound, smiling, and not knowing what a smile was for.

They were trying to get in. Not to hurt him. They were cold, he said. They wanted the warm.

He held the dome with the gold cracking open across his skin like the glaze on a dropped jar. He said it was like being leaned on by every lonely person who had ever lived.

And then, slowly, like a tide going out, the darkness lifted.

The zawba'a walked away west, swaying, its red eyes winking out one pair at a time. It was still noon. It had taken less time than a pot of coffee.

The caravan of Abu Kharbash was gone. Sixty men. Sixty camels. No bodies. No blood. One wooden saddle-frame lay on its side, still rocking, *creak, creak, creak*, in the dead air.

Far off across the gravel, small grey figures were walking away, slow and aimless, the way the emptied walk. Their arms didn't swing. One of them had an orange beard. As he went, faint on what little wind there was, I heard him calling, cheerful and flat: "*Couch the camels in a ring. Couch the camels in a ring.*" He didn't look back.

In the middle of that great swept emptiness, a small golden glow went out.

---

Theyab lay in a drift with his arms still spread, golden cracks even across his eyelids, his teeth clattering. All round him, pressed into the sand in a ring, were hundreds of small hand-shaped hollows, where they had leaned.

Of Jasir there was only a hand, sticking up out of the drift.

Theyab dug with his bare cracked hands, and I dug beside him the way I'd dig out a jerboa, until we found a face, and he dragged the boy out by his collar.

Shaha was dead beneath him. Her open eyes were grey all the way through.

Jasir was alive. Mostly.

His leg was broken. And the left side of him, arm and shoulder and face, had gone grey where the edge of the dark had licked him before the gold closed over. His left eye was white as a boiled egg. His mouth smiled on that side only, slack and sweet, and he was mumbling a name, half finished, trailing off. "*Sha*— *Shaha*— *Sha*—"

I put myself across his lap and leaned all my weight on his arms. *Don't. You have nothing left.* He moved me off gently, and laid his hands on Jasir's face anyway. Of course he did.

---

It came gold this time without being asked. Warmth, flowing out of him and into the boy, and the grey began to lift, peeling back from Jasir's cheek the way frost peels from a stone on a winter morning.

He saw Jasir's life. A small boy refusing to cry when a camel calf stepped on his foot. Then forward: Jasir older, lean and grey at the temples, riding ahead of other people through a dark place with his hand raised, picking a way between walls. A guide. The people behind him were frightened, and following.

And then the other vision.

Longer now. Much longer. It didn't flash. It *took* him.

The full moon above him, so vast and white it seemed to be falling. A dune under his bare feet, higher than any dune he'd ever seen, red sand going up into the sky. He knew its name now, though no one had ever told him. *Tal Moreeb*. The Hill of Fear. He felt his arms rise. And then the fire started in his chest, and this time it didn't stop at a flash, and this time he *felt* it.

Not light. Fire. The skin of his chest tightened and split. The heat climbed his throat and filled his mouth so he couldn't scream, and came out through his eyes. He smelled his own hair burning, and under it something like meat on a spit. Below him, a man's voice, cracked down the middle, was shouting a name.

Then he was on his knees on the caravan track, retching, with the smell of burnt hair still real in his nose. It was his own. The front of it had crisped where the light came out of his eyes.

---

Jasir opened his eyes. Both of them brown.

The grey was gone. His leg was straight. His lips were split and bleeding, his tongue thick in his mouth. He tried to speak and nothing came out but a dry click.

Theyab reached behind him for his waterskin.

I knew what was in it. I'd listened to it slosh against his hip for two days. A mouthful, perhaps two. All the water he had in the world. The caravan's water had gone up into the black, we were three days from any well I knew, and the sun was climbing toward the worst of the afternoon.

He didn't think about it. I watched him not think about it. He pulled the stopper with his teeth and held the skin to Jasir's mouth, and held the boy's head up so he wouldn't choke, the way you'd water a lamb. Jasir drank all of it, and went on sucking at the empty neck, and Theyab let him.

It was a poor thing, that skin, old goat leather patched twice, with a knot of red wool round its neck that Umm Hanan had tied there so it could be told from the others at the well. Theyab put it into Jasir's hands and closed the boy's fingers round it.

Then Jasir looked up.

At Theyab, cracked all over with glowing gold, light still leaking from the corners of his eyes, his hair smoking. At the swept and empty plain where an hour before there had been everyone he knew.

I watched him put it together. I watched him do the sum, the way frightened people always do, quick and wrong. Everything he'd loved had gone up into the dark, and the only thing left on the whole swept plain was a boy who shone. A gap-toothed child in a palm-rib boat, rowing after gold. *If it shines, it wants you to follow.*

"It looked at you," Jasir whispered. His voice was wet now. Theyab's water had given it back to him. "I saw it. All those eyes. It came, and it stopped, and it looked straight at *you*."

Theyab didn't answer.

"It came for you." Jasir was scrambling backward, the empty waterskin clutched to his chest. "Didn't it? You brought it. You're what it was looking for. Everyone's gone, Abu Kharbash is gone, Shaha's gone, they're all *gone*, and you—"

His face twisted.

"*Monster.*"

Theyab flinched. Like a slap.

"I'm not a monster," he said.

The feather mark didn't burn. He couldn't have said it if it weren't true. But I'll tell you the thing I have never been able to forgive, and Theyab saw it, because by then he had begun to watch me.

When Jasir said *it came for you*, I didn't growl.

My hackles stayed down. Flat. Not a single hair along my spine moved.

Jasir got to his feet and ran, stumbling, out across the empty gravel with Theyab's waterskin bouncing against his back, and he didn't look back. Not once.

I hope some old man by some fire tells it differently now. I hope he tells it wrong.

Theyab knelt where Jasir had left him and watched him go, with his hands open on his knees and nothing in them.

---

The ground began to sink and turn under us like water going down a drain. From the bottom of the hollow, very faintly, came the creak of the saddle-frame, still rocking, somewhere underneath.

Far off to the west, the black pillar had stopped walking. Its red eyes had turned back toward us.

And very faintly, on no wind at all, a little girl's voice: "*Yumma, it's dark.*"

I barked at him, high and sharp. *Up. Now.*

He tried. There was nothing left. The ground slid out from under him, pulling him toward the centre of the hollow, toward the creaking. I took his collar in my teeth and pulled, and felt the silver half of me straining to *grow*. *Let me. I could hold the whole desert in my jaws.* I almost let it.

The sand was at his waist. At his chest.

Then came the soft heavy padding of a camel at full gallop.

---

The pale camel came over the lip of the hollow at a dead run. A hand wrapped in grey cloth closed on Theyab's arm and hauled him up across the saddle face down.

"The dog—" he choked. "Ghareeb—"

The rider caught me up by the scruff, the way the Bedu lift a saluki pup, and dropped me across the boy, and the camel was already running.

Behind us, the zawba'a was coming back, faster now, eager, swaying as it walked. And on the low ridge between us and it, a grey robe, flapping. A smooth white mask with no face on it at all.

The Seventh was standing in our path.

He drew one of his two grey blades as the camel thundered down on him, and swung it at the boy slung across the saddle. A clean, fast, perfect stroke. It went past Theyab's throat a finger's width away and cut nothing but air.

The Seventh doesn't miss by accident. He stood on the ridge and watched us go, and the whirlwind came down over him and he didn't move, his white mask tipped up toward all those turning eyes as if they were old friends. Just before the dark took him, I heard it under the roar. Four notes, low and slow, climbing.

---

The rider didn't stop until the sun was going down and the zawba'a was a smudge behind us. He slid Theyab off onto the first red sand and set me beside him, and crouched over the boy without touching him, looking at the golden cracks and the singed hair a long, long time. His wrapped hands were shaking. I put that away with all the other things I wasn't saying.

He hung a waterskin on a dead *markh* bush, without a word, and pointed with his long hunting spear. North and west.

"Wait," Theyab croaked. "Who are you?"

The rider was gone into the dusk, the way he'd gone the first time.

"That's twice," Theyab whispered.

I lay down against him. You know by now that I knew who the rider was, and that I said nothing.

---

We walked the way the spear had pointed. Theyab made the rider's water last. Near dusk he ate one of the squashed honey-dates from the wreck of his tent, Umm Hanan's, from the bottom of his bag, sticky with sand. He held it in his mouth a long time, the way a child holds a hand.

He didn't say anything about Jasir, or about my hackles lying flat when Jasir said *it came for you*. He'd seen. I know he had.

Once he stopped and sniffed the back of his own wrist. He could still smell burnt hair on himself. So could I. I'm not sure he ever knew, after that, which fires were real.

The moon came up late that night, the thinnest I'd seen it since Hilat al-Sarab, a pale scratch of white low over the dunes. It gave almost no light at all.

And somewhere ahead of us, so faint only a dog could feel it, something was humming.

I felt it in my teeth, then in the pads of my feet. A deep, low note rising out of the sand exactly where the spear had pointed, like a mother humming a child to sleep. Or like a well, waiting.

I knew that sound, and the water that lies beside it, and what that water does to those who drink. The rider had not pointed us at it by accident. He was walking with his head down and his hand pressed to the feather mark, and he hadn't heard it yet. In a little while he would.

That night I didn't know the desert had saved one more thing for him beside the singing sand. Not a jinn, or a vision, or a stranger in a white mask.

Something worse, in the end. Something he could love.

If I'd known, I'd have made him walk faster. Or slower. I've never decided which.

---

*End of Chapter Seven*
