# Chapter Twenty
## Ash into Gold

> *Ash into gold and gold into flame —*
> *The end is the start, and the start is the same.*
> — from the song of the fire-bird; the last verse, which is also the first

---

The gold fell all night.

It came down soft and slow, like the first rain after a seven-year drought, and everywhere it touched, the grey let go.

I watched from the top of Tal Moreeb. I couldn't have moved if I'd wanted to. Down at the foot of the dune, the great ring of the emptied stood with their faces turned up into the falling light, and one by one, the white went out of their eyes. Brown came back. Black. Hazel. An old herder looked down at his own hands and began to cry. A woman spun round and round in the sand, calling a child's name, and somewhere in the crowd a child's voice called back.

Not all of them. I promised you no more silences, so I'll tell you. Some of them woke screaming, because the gold gave them back everything, including what their hands had done while they were empty. And some didn't wake at all. The gold touched them, and they lay down in the sand, still smiling, and didn't get up.

Ghanim woke with his memory and nothing else, a cracked white mask at his feet. Camels, strongbox, the silver sewn into his saddle-bags against bad years, all gone to the Nameless. They say he sweeps out a coffee house in Al-Nakhlatain now, and counts every coin that crosses the floor twice, and none of them are his.

Then there was *sound*. Thousands of people talking and weeping and shouting names. The whole of the sands was saying them.

Far off, over the walls of Ubar, a light came on. Then another. Someone was lighting the lamps again. And in the wall niches, I'm told, the names of the dead came back in gold, and stayed.

The grey went out of the sands like a tide going out over the sabkha.

That's what everyone says. But I have one silver eye that sees what jinn see, and I'll tell you what I watched. It didn't go *away*. It went *down*. It drained into the dunes the way the rain drains into a wadi bed, sinking between the grains, until there was nothing on the surface but gold.

The tide goes out over the salt flats, little one. It doesn't stop being the sea.

---

The gold fell on Ghadban too.

The small grey herd-boy was standing alone at the edge of the crest, and he didn't run. He stood still and let it land on him, the way you'd let snow land on you if you had never seen snow.

And the grey came off him like dust.

Under it was a boy. Brown and thin and barefoot, with a stick of ordinary acacia and a scab on one knee and goat hair caught in his sleeve. His eyes weren't black anymore. They were brown, warm brown, the eyes I'd looked into across a thousand fires before the towns on this coast were built.

"Ghareeb?" he said, in a small voice.

I couldn't have answered if I could talk. It was the second time in one night.

He looked at his own hands, then back at me, and his eyes filled.

"I remember my name," he said.

Every hair on my back lay down so smooth it was like being stroked. It was true. He remembered his name.

I pushed my head into his chest, the way I used to when he was six. *I never forgot it*, I told him, the way jinn hear me. *Not once.*

He smiled. And I have spent every night since trying to decide what kind of smile it was. A boy's smile, healed and sorry and tired. Or the smile of someone who has finally put down a very heavy thing, and doesn't care where it lands, or who picks it up next.

He lifted his face to the last of the falling gold and came apart into light. It rose off Tal Moreeb and went away east toward the coming sun.

But his shadow didn't go with him.

It lay on the sand where he'd stood, and it was not a boy's shadow. It was the other one. Too large, too many joints, horned. For one breath after the rest of him was gone it lay there. Then it sank. Into the dune. Down, with the rest of the grey, the way a stone sinks into still water, and the sand closed over it without a ripple.

My hackles did nothing at all.

I've hated Ghadban for more years than I can count. I'll miss him for longer. And I still don't know whether I watched him healed that night, or only watched him put down the Ifrit and walk away from it.

---

The Seventh was kneeling at the very end of the crest, where the boy had stood.

The sand there had turned gold. Not gold-coloured. *Gold.* You know the old stories: sand that holds a bearer's ash turns gold and never moves again. It lay in a small, perfect circle on the top of the highest dune in the world. The wind went across it and didn't move a single grain. In the middle of it lay Old Bakhit's golden feather, the one found with the baby in the sand. It was whole. Every barb.

Saqr had put down his blades. As I watched, his white mask split along its crack and fell away in two halves. It took skin with it. Fifteen years it had been on. There were raw red lines down his cheeks where its edges had grown into him.

Under it was a young man's face. Twenty, pale-eyed, thin, with his father's jaw and his brother's watchful look.

He opened his mouth. He hadn't spoken a word of his own in fifteen years.

"Nahidh," said Saqr.

Sharood turned round.

He crossed the crest faster than I've ever seen a man move and caught his elder son in his arms. For a moment, a single moment, it was what it should have been.

Then Saqr began to remember.

Not the tent and the song and the baby. That came first, and it was sweet, and it lasted perhaps three breaths. Then the rest came. Fifteen years of it, all at once, the way the gold had given it back to the emptied. Every child he had carried into the hold of the buried dhow. Every name he had watched burn. The exact moment each child had stopped crying. The things the grey blades had done in his hands, which he had not been able to feel at the time and could feel now, every one, all together.

He screamed.

He tore himself out of his father's arms and clawed at his own face, at the raw lines where the mask had been, as though he could put it back. Sharood caught his wrists. Saqr fought him, and screamed, and then stopped screaming and simply shook, and Sharood held on, the way he had held on to Theyab's burning hands. He didn't let go. He'd learned something about letting go, and something about not.

And after a long time, in the middle of it, through his teeth, Saqr began to hum. The fire-bird song. Over and over. As if it were the only rope left down the well and he was hanging off it by his fingers.

The Eldest sat a little way down the slope, saying his mother's name.

Zaid stood beside Sharood with Hazza's ring in his fist, looking out across the sands.

"Ghareeb," he said, and put his hand on my head. "Look."

The sun was coming up.

And below us, across the whole of the Empty Quarter, the dunes had turned gold in great sweeping lines, miles long. They weren't moving anymore. They never would again. Four letters, written from one edge of the sky to the other, so large you could have read them from the towers of Ubar, so large the herders of Liwa read them still.

*NAHIDH.*

---

Old Bakhit was lying on his back in the sand.

He'd been there all night. I'd forgotten him. Everyone had. That shames me now. I limped over, and he turned his face toward the sound of me. The dust was running out of the cracks in his hands like sand from a split sack.

"Dog," he said. His voice was barely there. "Is it morning?"

I licked his hand. Nearly.

"Did he forgive me? The boy?"

I laid my head on his chest, where he could feel my hackles lying flat. He'd been a bearer. He understood me.

"He kept it," Bakhit whispered. "The whole way."

He smiled, the smile I've never been able to describe, fond and sad at once, the way a man smiles at an old letter.

"May the sand remember him," he whispered.

Then the sun came up over the edge of the world, and its first light fell across his milky eyes.

And the milk cleared out of them.

They went clear and dark and wet, the eyes of a young man, and they *saw*. He looked up at the sky going pink and gold. He looked out at the great golden letters lying across the dunes. The last thing he had seen, three hundred years before, was the top of this dune in the dark. The first thing he saw now was the dawn.

"Oh," said Bakhit. "Oh, *look* at it."

Then his eyes moved. Past the letters. Past the dunes. Out to the far rim of the sands, where the dunes meet the sky, out where no caravan has ever come back from.

His face changed.

I have tried, every night since, to decide what was in it. Wonder. Terror. Both. The face of a man who has been in the dark three hundred years and, in the first light, sees the thing that has been standing beside him the whole time.

"Ghareeb," he whispered. "Ghareeb, at the edge. There's something— it's getting *up*. Dog, it's so big, it's been lying there all along, and it's—"

That was all.

His eyes stayed open, fixed on the edge of the world. His debt was paid, and he was gone. The bells at his belt were silent.

Then one of them rang. Once. Very softly. Though nobody had said a word.

I looked where he'd been looking, with both my eyes. I saw the far dunes, and the heat beginning to shimmer on them, and a mirage like a lake, and nothing else.

That isn't the same as there being nothing else.

We buried him on the slope of Tal Moreeb, facing east, so he'd always have the dawn. Sharood dug, and I dug beside him, because digging is a thing a dog can do. I nosed his eyes shut. They stayed shut. I was glad of that. I didn't want them looking.

---

They went down at noon. Sharood and Saqr, and Zaid between them, back toward Ubar, where every lamp was burning again and the people were painting a new name on the wall of the fort court. Zaid would be sheikh. He was ten.

I stayed with the gold. I lay beside that small glittering circle through the long afternoon and into the night, and I didn't eat, and I didn't sleep. There was nothing to guard. The gold would never move. Nothing in the sands would ever touch it.

I guarded it anyway.

Some time after dark, I put my ear to the sand beside the circle.

Something was breathing down there.

Very deep. Very slow. Not the way the tombs of Ubar breathed, in and out. It went in, and then it didn't come out. It held. Like breath held under water by a pearl diver who can hold it longer than any diver who ever lived, and is patient, and is listening to the footsteps going by overhead.

The Ifrit wasn't gone. It was sleeping. And it had pulled the gold over itself like a blanket.

When the moon came up that night, just past full, I stood on the top of the highest dune in the world and howled at the sky.

Not at the One who made the sands. I'm old, not foolish, and I have never been certain He is the one doing the asking. At the dark past the sky, and the far edge where Bakhit had looked. I put every word into it that I've never been able to say.

*Not this one. You hear me? Not this one. Not again. I have walked beside twelve of them. I have watched nine of them burn on this sand. And they're children, every time, children, you only ever want the children. He never had anything. Nobody ever gave him a single thing he didn't have to dig out of the rubbish heap, and he gave you everything, and you took it, and it isn't even finished, is it? It's never finished. You'll want another—*

Nothing answered. Nothing ever does. It only waits.

The wind came up from the east, gentle, and went over the golden circle without stirring a grain. Then it went over me and smoothed down my coat, the way you'd smooth a frightened child's hair.

Or the way you'd smooth the coat of a hound you mean to keep.

I lay down beside the gold with my nose on my paws. And I wept, little one. Salukis do. Ask anyone who has ever kept one.

---

There's something I have to tell you now.

I've been putting it off the whole story. You'll have noticed. You're a great deal cleverer than you look, all pink and wrinkled like that.

I saw it happen.

The night of the full moon, one month before the end, when they argued and Theyab walked away into the dark. I wasn't by the fire. I was out on the dunes in my other shape, my shadow as tall as a watchtower, hunting the jackals that were tracking his Light. I didn't want him to see me like that. Not after the storm, when his Light burned me. Not after the way he'd looked at me.

So I was out in the dark being a monster, and that's why I was too far away.

I heard voices from the camp. Hazza's. And another one, soft, that I didn't know yet. I caught the smell on the wind before I caught the words. Rosewater.

By the time I came over the ridge, it was almost done.

Muhaisen was kneeling by the fire. He'd come alone, in a plain brown cloak, no mask, on a single camel. Hazza' had known him his whole life: the man who had sat at his father's right hand in the majlis every day he'd been alive. So when Muhaisen held out a waterskin and said *You must be thirsty. The boy has left you. Come home,* Hazza' drank. You don't refuse water in the sands. Not from a man who knew you as a child. Not ever.

There was ash in that water. Bearer's ash, stolen from a grave. False Light. That's why he went grey, little one. That's why the golden cracks. I watched them open across his face like a dropped coffee cup, and I watched him not understand what was happening to him.

I was a moment too late. One moment. If I'd let one jackal go. If I'd turned at the first voice instead of the second.

Muhaisen saw me coming over the ridge, huge and black with my coal eye, and he ran. He had Hazza's ring in his hand. He'd pulled it off while the young man was still falling.

I reached Hazza'. He was already gone. His eyes were open. He'd fallen facing the way Theyab had walked.

I tried to close them. I had to shrink back into a dog even to try, and a dog has no hands. I pushed at them with my nose. They wouldn't stay shut. They kept opening, looking the way Theyab had gone. In the morning the boy closed them himself.

And then I said nothing.

A dog can't speak. That's my whole excuse, isn't it? But I could have led him to the tracks of a single camel going north. I could have found a hundred ways. I've had a hundred reasons not to. No proof. Who'd believe a jinn-touched dog against a sheikh's wazir? If the boy had known too early, he'd have gone after Muhaisen with hate, and his Light would have gone grey. All true, more or less.

But it isn't *why*.

The why is that I was ashamed. Ashamed of what I'd been out there, and of being slow, and that the boy I loved most would have to know I watched his only friend die and couldn't stop it.

So I let him carry it alone. For the rest of his life he thought it was his fault for walking away. I heard him whisper *I'm sorry* into the dark a hundred times, and I lay a few paces off with my eyes shut and pretended to be asleep.

He would have forgiven me. That's the worst of it. He'd have put his hand on my head and said *it's all right, Ghareeb*, and his mark wouldn't have burned at all.

That's my confession. I'm telling you because you're the only one I've never lied to.

Not yet, anyway. Give me time.

---

Twelve years went by. Desert years. Long ones.

But first, the morning after. I wasn't there. I've heard Nadim tell it at the fire so often I could tell it in his voice.

The grey had taken Hilat al-Sarab that summer. Every palm-frond roof the colour of ash, every face, the well water grey in the bucket. Only one lamp in the village had kept burning through it: a little clay lamp in a widow's window, which she filled every night with oil she couldn't spare, though she could no longer have told you why, or for whom.

When the gold came down over Liwa that dawn, it came to Umm Hanan's house first.

Nadim was standing grey in the lane with his brother's name gone out of his head. He says the old peeling blue came back into her door first. Then the red of her coals. Then the brown of the bread on her shelf. For a long breath that was the only colour in the village, before the gold spread out from it down the lane like water from a broken jar.

He says he never knew why it went there first. It's true. He never knew.

---

The grey never came back up. The villages filled again with people who remembered their names. Hilat al-Sarab was rebuilt around the same deep well. And there was a new lamp on the Night of Lamps.

Umm Hanan lit it.

She was so old by then she had to be helped to the patch of hard ground between the last house and the first dune, where a patched tent had once stood. Nobody had built there since. Nobody would.

She held the taper in both shaking hands. The lamp was the same clay lamp from her window, plain and small, with a feather scratched into its side.

"For the boy," she said, thin as paper, "who I should have spoken up for. Theyab. Who was Nahidh."

She lit it.

And as the flame caught, a wind came. Out of nowhere, out of a still night, cold, from the east, from the far edge. It went down the whole line of the village like a hand, and every lamp in Hilat al-Sarab went out at once. Every one. Doorsteps, windows, the eleven on the wall of the well-house. Dark from end to end.

Except hers.

Her flame leaned. It leaned away from her, out toward the open sands, east, toward the far edge, the way a dog's head turns toward a sound you can't hear. For a breath it burned blue-grey, the colour of the emptied. Then it straightened and went gold again, and stayed lit.

Umm Hanan blinked at it, and said nothing, and nobody else said anything either.

From it, one by one, the whole village lit theirs again. Then they relit the other eleven and said the names. Not the titles. The names. Nadim had them from me, the only way he could, sitting up with me night after night until he understood. I'd decided someone ought to know they were children.

Nadim lit his last, with Shibl, nineteen now and taller than him, carrying the taper. Nadim had grown into a broad, quiet man with early grey in his beard who kept his father Saqi's well. He never talked about Al-Nakhlatain. Every Night of Lamps he stood at the twelfth lamp longer than anyone, until his wife came and took his hand.

Her name was Hessa. She was round as the full moon that year.

The baby came that same night, after the lamps were lit. A boy. Small and furious and very loud.

Hessa was holding him against her in the dark of the little mud house when she went still.

There, just under his collarbone, was a mark the colour of honey. Shaped like a feather.

It was glowing.

She didn't call out. She didn't smile. She put her hand over it. Flat, hard, the way you'd cover a lamp in a house where something is looking in at the windows. She'd grown up on the story, every child in Hilat al-Sarab had, and she'd listened to it closely, and she knew exactly what that mark was for.

"No," she whispered. "No, no. Not him. Nadim. *Nadim.* Make it stop."

Nadim looked at it a long time. Then he went down on his knees beside the bed and cried like a boy. And when he could speak again, he didn't ask anyone. There was no point. It was written across the whole of the sands in letters miles high, and the sands don't take requests.

"Nahidh," he said, and his voice broke in the middle of it. "His name is Nahidh."

I was lying in the doorway. I'd been lying in that doorway since the moon had turned nine times. Hessa had stopped trying to chase me off. She looked at me now, over her son's head, and I saw her understand, and I saw her hate me for it. She was right to.

She did everything the old women told her.

There was a silver amulet case on a cord at the baby's neck before he was a day old, with a folded paper inside that she never opened. There was a blue glass bead pinned to his swaddling, an eye with a white ring and a black heart, to catch envious looks and turn them back. She kept the lamp burning all night, every night, for the forty nights a mother and her newborn don't leave the house. And under her pillow, where her hand could find it in the dark without waking anyone, she kept a blade.

Not Nadim's khanjar. A narrow knife of black iron, wrapped in rag, that had lain under the pillow of every mother in her family through their forty nights. Iron, the grandmothers say. They can't abide iron.

Who *they* were, she didn't say. Every girl in Hilat al-Sarab grew up on the name. Umm al-Sibyan. The Mother of Children. She comes for the newborn and the newly delivered in the first forty nights, when the mother is weakest and the child has hardly been named. She comes as someone you'd let in.

Hessa slept with her hand on that iron. And when she couldn't sleep, she lay and looked at me across the cradle: a dog who had buried eleven children, lying in her doorway. I don't think she could decide whether I was what the iron was for.

---

Nadim doesn't sleep much. He sold one bearer, once, to the Nameless, for his brother's life. He has never said so aloud. He sleeps across the inside of the door with a khanjar under his hand. He carries the boy everywhere on his shoulders, and if a stranger so much as asks the child's name in the souq, Nadim puts himself between them. He's been wrong once about who to protect. He means to spend the rest of his life on the other side of it.

Sharood and Saqr help. They built a house where Old Bakhit's tent used to stand and hung it with herbs and little brass bells. Sharood is an old man now, older than his years. Every night, when the sun goes down, he takes his long spear and sits at the edge of the well-keeper's house, facing the dunes, and he doesn't go in until morning.

Saqr doesn't sleep at all. I don't think he has slept a full night in twelve years. He walks the edge of the village in the dark, and sometimes I hear him counting under his breath, the numbers he gave the children in the dhow, as if counting them could bring them back. And every night, at the darkest hour, he sits under the baby's window and hums the fire-bird song, and the baby stops crying to listen.

---

I'll tell you one more thing, and then I'll stop.

Some nights the wind talks.

It comes up out of the sand at the darkest hour, not a sound so much as a pressure, and it comes to the cradle. The goats break their tethers. The village dogs slink off under the palms with their tails clamped. Even Sharood, out at the edge with his spear, puts his face in his hands.

It's a voice I know. I heard it under Ubar. I heard it in Ghadban's mouth, a beat behind.

*Go, old dog,* it says. *You're tired. You've done enough. Lie down somewhere else tonight. Somewhere warm. Just one night.*

My hackles go up so hard they ache, every time. I growl until my ribs shake.

And I don't move. Not once. Not a paw's width from that cradle. It's tried every night since you were born, and I'm still here. I'll still be here when it stops trying. I've been frightened off from a great many things in my life, little one. Not from you.

It tried something else, once. I'll tell you, and then I won't speak of it again.

It was the thirty-ninth night. One short of the forty. The house was asleep, even your mother, and that was the first wrong thing, because Hessa never slept. Your father was asleep across the door with his knife, and I was lying across his feet, and the lamp had burned low.

Someone scratched at the door. Softly. The way a neighbour scratches when she doesn't want to wake the baby.

Then a woman's voice, through the wood. Warm. Tired. A mother's voice.

"*Hessa,*" it said. "*Hessa, let me in. I've come to help with the boy. You haven't slept in a month. Let me hold him. Only till morning.*"

Nadim didn't wake. Out at the edge of the house Sharood had put his face in his hands. Under the window, Saqr had stopped humming.

The door was barred. It opened anyway, a hand's width.

She was tall. Taller than the doorway, so that she had to stoop to look in, and her hair hung down over her face in long grey ropes, dripping, as if she'd come up out of the falaj. Her arms were bare and very long, and she held them out in front of her in the shape of a cradle. Empty. She smelled of sour milk. And behind the hair, where her face should have been, there was a face. I won't tell you whose. It was one Hessa would have known; I'll swear to that. Hessa's eyes had come open across the room, and couldn't close.

"*He's going to burn,*" said the woman at the door, so gently. "*You know he is. You've heard the story. Give him to me now, while he's small, and he'll never feel it. I'll keep him. I keep all of them.*"

My hackles came up so hard the old burn split open.

That wasn't the worst. The worst was Hessa's hand, moving under the pillow. Not toward the iron. *Away* from it.

So I got up.

I stepped off Nadim's feet and stood in that hand's-width gap with my nose a finger from those empty arms. I didn't let the shadow out. I was an old dog in a doorway, that was all, and I put my teeth where she would have to come through them.

She bent down to me. Under the sour milk I smelled the grey, and under the grey, holding its breath, the thing from the well.

"*Go, old dog,*" she said. "*Lie down somewhere else.*"

I growled until the bead on your swaddling rattled.

And behind me I heard the scrape of iron on the floor, as Hessa, sobbing, dragged her grandmother's blade out from under the pillow and held it up in the lamplight in both hands.

When I looked back at the door it was shut. Barred. The bar had never moved.

In the morning there were no tracks outside.

But the blue bead on your swaddling had split clean in two, straight down the middle of its eye. The old women say that's what a bead does when it has caught something meant for the child.

Hessa buried both halves at the foot of Umm Hanan's lamp. She didn't look at me for three days. On the fourth she put a bowl of milk down beside the cradle, and I drank it.

I want that recorded.

The dunes out at the far edge of the sands have started writing again. Out where no caravan has ever come back from. Out where Bakhit was looking.

The first year, I could only see the shapes from the top of Tal Moreeb. It's back where it belongs now, at the edge of Liwa, and the herders say it's taller than it was. The shapes aren't letters anyone knows.

The fifth year, Sharood could see them from the towers of Ubar.

This spring, I climbed the well-house roof and saw them, very faint, on the rim of the world. They're coming closer. Every season, a little. The way the grey once came, low and flat and in no hurry. It doesn't need to hurry.

And under the gold, when I put my ear to it, something is still holding its breath.

The Light doesn't come back for no reason, little one. It comes back because it's going to be needed. Because something is hungry. On one side, or the other. Or both. I've stopped being sure there's a difference.

But not tonight.

Tonight the lamps are burning along the edge of the sands, and your mother is asleep with her hand over your chest, and your father is asleep across the door with his knife. Old Sharood is out at the edge of the house with his spear. Saqr is humming under the window. And I'm curled beside your cradle with my nose on my paws, where I'll be every night from now on, whether anyone likes it or not.

You've been listening all this time. I know you have. Your mark has been glowing brighter at every part where he was brave. It's warm against my coat. Warmer every night.

So let me start again, the way I started it. The way I'll start it for you every night, until you're old enough to tell me to stop.

Let me tell you about a boy who had no name…

He was the one I loved most. Don't tell the others. Don't tell yourself, either. Not yet.

The end is the beginning, you see. It always has been. That's what the knot means. That's what I'm for. I was made to walk beside you, and to love you, and when it's time, to be the one who wakes you. I've done it twelve times. I've never once been able to stop.

And out there in the dark, past the lamps and the golden dunes and the far edge of the world, something is already waiting for you.

The sun is coming up.

Wake up, little one.

---

*The old man stopped.*

*The fire had burned down to coals. None of the children moved. Over Liwa the Pleiades had climbed high, and in the south, low on the dunes, Suhail burned red.*

*"You'll say it's a tale," he said at last. "I told you at the start. It happened. I was there." He turned his hands over in the firelight, big hands, old, with a well-keeper's calluses. "My name is Nadim. I sold him. And then I raised him."*

*A girl at the back whispered, "And Nahidh? Your son? Where is he?"*

*The old man didn't answer. He looked out past the firelight, east, toward the far edge of the sands, for a long time.*

*"Umm Hanan's lamp still burns," he said instead. "Forty years. Nobody has ever had to fill it."*

*At his feet, the old saluki lifted his head. One eye amber, one eye silver. He got up, stiffly, the way the very old get up, and shook the sand from his coat, and walked away from the fire without looking back, across the camp to a small tent where a lamp was burning and a newborn had been sleeping for three nights.*

*Inside, by the lamp, a young mother slept with one hand under her pillow, closed on something long and dark that was no knife a man would carry. A blue bead winked on the baby's swaddling. A small silver case lay at his throat.*

*He lay down across its door.*

*At the edge of the firelight the smallest of the children saw a woman standing. Tall. Her hair hanging down over her face. Her long arms held out in front of her, empty, in the shape of a cradle. The child blinked, and there was only a dune.*

*The old saluki didn't lift his head. But a growl came out of him, very low, and went on for a long time.*

*Out on the dunes, very faintly, the sand was writing.*

---

*End of Chapter Twenty*
