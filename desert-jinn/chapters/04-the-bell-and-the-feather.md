# Chapter Four
## The Bell and the Feather

> *The bell rings for the liar, and the liar is often you. It does not ring for silence. Nothing does.*
> — words stamped around the rim of an old healer's bell, in a script no one in Liwa can read

---

His tent was gone.

Not burned. The people of Hilat al-Sarab were too frightened that night even for fire. Fire makes light, and light, they had just learned, brought things out of the dark.

So they had torn it down. Somebody had pulled up the pegs and slashed the nine patched scraps and trampled the whole thing flat into the gravel, the way you'd stamp on a scorpion you weren't brave enough to pick up. His blanket and his water-skin were gone. The little clay jar where he kept his annas had been smashed, and the annas taken. Fear doesn't stop people from being practical.

And all around the wreckage, someone had poured salt. A thick white ring of it, unbroken, glittering faintly under the stars. That's what the grandmothers do around a place where a jinn has been, so that whatever lived there cannot follow you home. In the middle of the ring, on the flattened cloth, someone had smeared a hand in soot. Five black fingers. *Keep out. What was here is not of us.*

Theyab stood looking at it for a while in the dark.

I stepped over the salt ring, quite deliberately, and lay down in the middle of the wreckage, and yawned. You see the trouble with salt, for something like me. It's mostly for show.

He didn't laugh. But he stepped over the ring after me. He knelt in the wreckage and began to dig through it with his hands, quietly, methodically, the way he dug through the ruins. A scavenger to the last.

He found the twist of waxed paper first. Four dates soaked in honey, the ones he'd been saving. Somebody had stepped on them. They were squashed flat and full of grit. He brushed them off carefully and put them inside his shirt with Umm Hanan's bread.

Then he found the cloth.

It was buried under the broken tent-pole: a square of grey fabric, softer than anything else he owned, woven with small strange marks no woman in Hilat al-Sarab had ever been able to name. The desert had wrapped him in it fifteen years ago. He'd slept with it under his head every night of his life.

He held it against his face. Then he went still.

"It smells of smoke," he said.

It always had. He'd simply never noticed, the way you never notice the smell of your own tent. Burned cloth, very old, very faint. I could have told him what kind of smoke, if I'd had the words, and I'm glad I didn't. I'd smelled it on the night the desert left him, rolling across the dunes from the place where his family had been, along with another smell, the one that clings to the robes of men who burn their own names.

He folded the cloth small and tucked it away with the rest.

"That's everything," he said.

A knife, a cloth, some bread, four ruined dates, and a dog. Fifteen years, and that was everything.

---

Bakhit was waiting for us.

Of course he was. The lamp was still burning when we reached his tent, the brass bells turning slowly on their threads in the draught. Ghaf wood smouldered in the hearth-pit, and tea was already poured, two glasses. Bakhit sat cross-legged on his worn rug, his milky eyes fixed on nothing, his white beard falling over his folded hands.

"Sit," he said, before Theyab had even ducked under the tent flap. "You're bleeding on my harmal."

Theyab didn't sit.

He stood in the opening with the firelight on his face. The golden cracks along his jaw had faded to a faint shimmer. His shirt was torn at the back and stiff with blood. He was shaking. Not from cold.

He was looking at the fire. At the little flames licking up the ghaf wood. He couldn't seem to stop looking at them, and he couldn't seem to bear it, and every time one of them flared, his hand jerked toward his chest.

"You knew," he said.

Bakhit turned his face toward the sound. For a long moment he said nothing.

Then he said, "Sit down, child. Let me see to your back. And then you can shout at me, and I promise to listen to every word."

The bells were silent. I lay down across the opening of the tent, and my hackles lay flat. Every word of it was true.

---

He sat, in the end. I think he was too tired not to.

Bakhit's hands were old and dry and very gentle. He peeled the torn shirt away and washed the wound with water from a clay bowl, then packed it with a paste of myrrh and burnt harmal seed. Theyab hissed through his teeth. He didn't cry out.

"Close to the old one," Bakhit murmured, his fingers moving over the pale scar under Theyab's left shoulder blade. "The sand does like to write in the same place twice."

He turned his blind face toward the opening where I lay.

"And you," he said. "You've finally let them see you, I hear. Howling like a dead man. Walking into the grey and back for an evening stroll."

I lifted my head from my paws and looked at him. My tail thumped the rug once.

"Ghanim screams very loudly," said Bakhit. Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's been a long time, Ghareeb. Since I've seen you do anything at all."

Theyab twisted round to stare at us. "You *know* him? Not just— you *know* him?"

Bakhit and I said nothing at the same moment, which was its own kind of answer.

I'll tell you this much, since you're listening and he wasn't: yes. We knew each other. We had known each other for a very long time, longer than the boy could have guessed. The last time Bakhit and I had stood in the same place under an open sky, he'd had eyes, and he'd been younger than Theyab, and he'd been running. He ran so fast that when he finally turned around, the last thing he ever saw was the place he was running from, white and enormous under a white full moon. Then the dark came down over his eyes and never lifted.

That's Bakhit's story, and he never told it to the boy. So I won't tell the rest of it to you. Not yet.

"Drink your tea," said Bakhit, and pressed the glass into Theyab's hands.

Theyab drank it. Black and bitter and smoky and green, boiled half to tar. He hated it as always. It was the first thing all night that felt like home.

Remember that. I asked you to, before.

---

"What am I?" he said at last.

The tea was finished. The fire had burned down to embers. Outside, the wind was whispering along the goat-hair walls, testing the seams.

"You know the story," said Bakhit.

"The Phoenix-Born." Theyab's voice was flat. "A piece of light gets put inside a child. The child carries it to the highest dune in the world and sets it free and drives the darkness back into the ground."

"Yes."

"That's a story. It's just a story you tell."

Bakhit said nothing.

"I'm not one of them," said Theyab. "I'm not—"

He stopped. His face twisted. His hand went to the feather mark and pressed hard, and I saw him bite down on the inside of his cheek until it bled.

It was burning.

He'd been about to say *I'm not one of the Phoenix-Born.* He'd wanted it to be true so badly he'd nearly said it. And the mark had caught the words before they reached his lips and set them alight, and for a moment the smell of scorched skin filled the little tent, sharp and unmistakable, and the bells all trembled on their threads without ringing.

The smell of the lie that had nearly got out of him went up my nose like a needle, and the growl came out of me before I could stop it.

He sat with his eyes squeezed shut, breathing through his nose, waiting for the pain to fade. When he opened them, there was a thin brown mark on his shirt, over his heart, where the cloth had singed.

"Ah," said Bakhit softly. "Yes. That's the trouble with you, isn't it? You can't even lie to yourself."

"I saw it," Theyab said. "When I healed Shibl. I saw myself on a dune. Burning." He swallowed. "Is that the story too?"

Bakhit's hands went very still in his lap.

"What did you feel?" he asked. Not *what did you see*. *What did you feel.*

"Everything," said Theyab.

The old man didn't answer. He turned his blind face away toward the wall of the tent, and for a moment he looked like someone listening to a sound very far off, a sound he had spent three hundred years trying not to hear.

---

Bakhit rose. It took him a while. His knees cracked like dry ghaf twigs. He felt his way past the hanging herbs to an old wooden chest in the far corner, a sea-chest studded with brass nails gone green. Theyab had never once seen it open.

Bakhit opened it now. I caught a glimpse inside before his body blocked it. Other bundles. Small ones, wrapped in old cloth, laid in a row like loaves. A child's sandal of plaited palm-leaf, burned black along one side. I looked away. Some things I've had a lifetime to stop looking at, and I'm still not good at it.

Bakhit brought out a bundle wrapped in oiled leather.

"When the desert left you on the edge of this village," he said, "you were wrapped in that grey cloth you carry. And tucked inside it, against your chest, was this. Mazyad's father would have sold it. So I took it before anyone saw, and I've kept it for fifteen years. Open it."

Theyab opened it.

It was a feather.

Gold, or something like gold, as long as his hand and so fine you could see the firelight through its edges. Every barb was perfect. But it was broken, snapped clean across the middle, the two halves clinging together by a few bent barbs. Along one side the barbs were twisted and crushed, as if a hand had closed on it and tried to wring it in two. At the break, the gold had gone dark. Not tarnish. Grey, the dead flat grey of the lane outside Nadim's house.

And it was warm.

Theyab held it in both palms and felt the warmth soak into his skin. It was the same warmth as the mark on his chest. The same shape. The same fine lines. And as he held it, very faintly, it beat. Once. Twice. Out of time with his heart, as if it were keeping time with something else.

A feather ought to smell of bird. This one smelled of honey and hot metal.

"It's broken," he said finally.

"Yes," said Bakhit.

"Can it be fixed?"

"Some things mend themselves," the old man said, "when they're ready."

True. The bells didn't stir. My hackles stayed down.

I'll tell you now, because it doesn't spoil anything: it did mend. Slowly. One barb at a time. Every time the boy learned something true about himself, something hard and painful, the feather grew a little more whole. I watched it the whole way. I've watched eleven of them, and they all mend, and they're always whole on the same night, under the same moon.

A feather is mended so it can fly. That's all I'll say. Every time it healed, I hated it a little more.

---

There was a second gift.

Bakhit reached up without looking and unhooked the smallest bell from its thread, no bigger than the tip of Theyab's thumb, brass worn smooth with age, strung on a cord of braided camel hair.

"This one," Bakhit said, "is for you."

He tied it around Theyab's neck himself, old fingers fumbling at the knot.

"You cannot lie. You know that by now. But the rest of the world can, and the rest of the world will. This bell will ring when someone lies to you. Not loudly. Not always in time. But it will ring."

Theyab touched it. It lay against his chest just above the feather mark, cold against the heat of his skin.

"How do I know it works?"

Bakhit lifted his glass and smiled. "My tea," he said, "is the finest in all of Liwa."

The bell rang.

A small, clear sound, like a single drop of water falling into a brass bowl at the bottom of a well. And at the same moment the lie hit my nose, sour and harmless and a little bit funny, and my hackles went up from my neck to the base of my tail, and a growl rolled out of my chest, low and long, like a camel complaining at being loaded.

Theyab looked at the bell. Then at me. At the ridge of hair standing up along my back like the crest of a dune.

"He…" Theyab said slowly. "Ghareeb. When somebody lies, he…"

"Yes," said Bakhit. "Every lie in his hearing. He's always done it. You've seen it a thousand times and thought he just didn't like Ghanim." He drank his terrible tea. "Nobody likes Ghanim. But that isn't why. Now you have two ways of knowing when somebody's lying to you. Most people don't get one."

I could see Theyab going back over every time. Ghanim's stall. The sheikh's door. Fifteen years of a dog telling him, and him not knowing how to listen. Most people don't. It's one of the great sorrows of the world. Not the greatest. I'll get to that.

"And does he ever lie?" Theyab asked. "Can a dog lie?"

Bakhit laughed, a dry little cough of a laugh. "A dog? A saluki? Never. Everyone knows a dog can't lie. Look at him. He couldn't if he tried."

The bell didn't ring.

Of course it didn't. Bakhit believed it. The bell rings for the liar, and Bakhit was not lying. He simply didn't know.

I put my head back down on my paws.

Later, that would matter more than any of us knew. A dog who has never once lied only has to do it once.

---

"Would you die for them?" Bakhit asked.

He said it quietly. He was turning his empty glass slowly between his palms. He might have been asking whether Theyab wanted more tea.

Theyab looked up. "What?"

"The people of this village. Mazyad, who called you Laqeet and sent you out with no guest-right. Ghanim, who cheated you every week of your life. The children who threw stones when you were six, and the men who threw them tonight. Nadim." A pause. "Would you die for them?"

The tent was very quiet. Even the wind outside had stopped, as though it, too, were waiting for the answer.

Theyab opened his mouth.

I'll always see it. He opened his mouth to say *yes*, because it was what a hero would say, what the Phoenix-Born said in every story Bakhit had ever told him. The feather burned before the word was out. He flinched, and the singed mark on his shirt darkened.

He closed his mouth, and opened it again, to say *no*. Because they had stoned him. Because they had called him monster and salted the ground where he slept. Because he owed them nothing at all.

That burned too.

And between the two burnings, for just an instant, the embers in the hearth filled his eyes, and I saw him flinch from them as if they had reached for him. The vision came uninvited. Only a flicker. The white moon. The heat in his mouth. He made a small sound, like a child woken from a nightmare who doesn't yet know he's awake.

He sat with his hand pressed hard against his chest, eyes wet, mouth half open. Neither word would come.

"I don't know," he whispered finally.

The bell was silent. My hackles lay flat.

Bakhit nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd expected, and dreaded.

"Good," he said. "That's honest. Hold on to it. It won't be your answer forever."

"Why are you asking me that?"

Bakhit didn't answer. He turned the empty glass in his hands, round and round, round and round, the way the dunes turn, the way the moon turns. The way everything out here comes back to where it started.

---

It was almost dawn when Theyab asked the question that mattered.

"Where do I go?"

"Ghidd al-Zujaj," said Bakhit. "The glass ridge."

Theyab blinked. "That's a story too."

"Most things are, until you walk into them. It lies out in the sands, farther than anyone from Hilat al-Sarab has ever gone. Ruins, older than Ubar, and the sand around them is glass, black and green, in runnels and blisters, the way sand goes when lightning strikes it, only miles of it. The old caravan men say it's where the first bearer said no to the Light, and the sand around him melted." His blind eyes moved, as though following something a long way off. "There are carvings on the rocks there. The rocks remember. Everything you need to know about what you are is cut into them. The faces of the ones who came before you. Older than me. Older than the dog, almost."

I lifted my head and looked at him. *Almost*, I thought. *Careful.*

"How do I find it?"

"You don't. You walk, and the dunes find it for you. They'll open a way if you're meant to reach it."

"And if something else opens a way?"

Bakhit's head came round. "Why do you ask that?"

Theyab hesitated. Then he told him about the lane behind Umm Hanan's oven at noon. The hot smell that Theyab hadn't smelled but had watched me smell. The little round prints, two by two, stopping at a door. The ring of them round Shibl in the grove.

Bakhit listened without moving. When it was done he was quiet a long time, and the bells hung still.

"The mothers are right to call their children in," he said at last. "They think they're telling a tale. Most tales are only a warning that has forgotten why it was given." He felt for the teapot, found it empty, and set it down. "Listen to me now, because I'll say this once, and you'll have no one out there to say it again. The sands are full of things that are hungry. The worst of them are the ghilan."

"Ghouls," said Theyab. "Like in the stories."

"Like in the stories. The stories are the only maps we have." Bakhit's fingers found a bead on a string at his wrist and began to turn it. "A ghoul lives in the empty places. Old wells, old graves, ruins like the ones you dig in. It sleeps under the sand by day and wakes at night, and it has no shape of its own, so it borrows. It takes the shape of a traveller who has lost his way and calls to you for help. It takes the shape of a woman, a beautiful one, standing alone at the edge of the firelight with her hair down, crying. It takes the shape of your own mother, if it knows you have none. It calls you by name. It knows the way to water. It only wants you to step off the path and follow it a little way, a very little way, over the next dune, where it can be alone with you."

"How do you know it, then? If it looks like anyone?"

"By the feet." Bakhit tapped the floor with one finger. "Whatever shape a ghoul wears, it can't change its feet. It has the hooves of a donkey. Always. Under the hem of the robe, under the long skirt, in the sand where it stood. That's the only honest thing about it." He turned his blind face toward me. "And the dog will smell them before you see them. Watch his back. When his hackles go up and there's no one lying to you, look down."

I lay very still. He had never told any of the others that. Not like that.

"Don't leave the path," said Bakhit. "Whatever calls. Whoever cries. Don't follow a voice over a dune at night. And don't follow a light."

"A light?"

"Out in the deep sands, you'll see lanterns. Far off, low down, swinging the way a lantern swings when a man walks with it. You'll think it's a camp, or a caravan, or someone come looking for you. The Bedouin call it Abu Fanous, the Father of the Lantern. It walks ahead of you all night, and you follow, and it never comes any closer, and when the sun comes up it's gone, and you're three days from water with no tracks behind you but your own." He let go of the bead. "The ghilan hang their lamps on him, the old men say. Or he hangs his on them. Either way it's the same grave."

Theyab sat with the feather in his hands. "Why didn't you ever tell me that one? In all those nights."

"Because you'd never had anywhere to go," said Bakhit.

The bell was silent. That one was true too, and it hurt him to say it. I could smell that.

Theyab turned the broken feather over in his hands. "Will you come with me?"

Something passed across the old man's face. Quick, gone almost before I saw it. But I've known Bakhit a long time, and I smelled it at the same moment I saw it. Shame. And under the shame, older and worse, fear.

"I don't leave the edge of the sands," he said. "I can't. The desert and I have an agreement. I made it a long time ago, and I'm still paying for it."

True. The bell didn't stir.

Theyab was quiet. Then he asked it. The same question he'd asked two nights ago, when his tent had still been standing.

"What happens to them? The Phoenix-Born. When they get to the dune and set the light free. What happens to them, after?"

I held my breath.

And Bakhit, who had sat among his truth bells for more years than the boy could have guessed and never told a lie that mattered, looked at him with his blind eyes and said:

"Go to Ghidd al-Zujaj. The answers there are older than mine, and truer. You'll understand it better when you see it carved."

The bell didn't ring.

Of course it didn't. Every word was true. My hackles never twitched. There was nothing in it for either of us to catch.

But I looked at Bakhit across that tent, and Bakhit, who could not see me, turned his face toward me anyway. And we both knew.

He had not lied to the boy. He had done something worse. He'd stood in front of the truth with a cloak spread out and let the boy walk past. He'd sent a child who can't read to a place made of writing, and he knew exactly what was written there.

That night an old man I had once loved like a son pushed a fifteen-year-old boy into the gap between *true* and *the whole truth*, and wished him well.

I didn't growl. Remember that, when you're judging him. I was lying right there, and I could have growled until the bells shook, and the boy would have understood. I didn't. I never have. Eleven times, I never have.

---

"May the sand remember you," said Bakhit at the tent flap. It's what the old ones say at partings in Liwa. The answer to it is *and you*, but Theyab didn't know that. Nobody had ever said it to him before.

The stars were fading. The eastern sky was turning the deep, bruised blue that comes just before the sun. Beyond the mud houses of Hilat al-Sarab the grey lay low and flat along the edge of the world. It hadn't moved in the night. It hadn't needed to. Along its edge, quite still, stood a line of small grey shapes. From that distance you might have taken them for the stumps of dead palms. They were all facing the tent.

"Will it follow me?" Theyab asked. "If I go. Will it leave them alone?"

"I don't know," said Bakhit.

The bell was silent. He truly didn't.

Theyab nodded. Then he did something I'd never seen him do in fifteen years. He stepped forward and put his arms around the old man, quickly and clumsily, like someone who's never been taught how. Bakhit went very still. Then his dry hands came up and rested on the boy's back, either side of the new wound, and held there a moment too long. The way you hold someone at a graveside.

"Go," Bakhit said roughly. "Before I make you drink more tea."

Then he bent, slowly, his knees cracking, and found my head with his hand, and held it. He didn't say anything to me. He didn't need to. He smelled of myrrh and smoke and three hundred years of being sorry.

We went.

---

We walked east into the deep sands as the dunes were finishing their night's work.

If you have never seen the sands of Liwa move, I'm sorry for you, and also not. In that last grey hour before dawn the dunes were settling into their new places, whole hills sliding sideways with a sound like a vast animal breathing in its sleep, the crests smoking with blown sand. Paths that had been there yesterday were gone, and new ones lay smooth and fresh between the dunes, untouched by any foot.

One of those new paths opened right in front of us.

It ran straight out from the edge of Hilat al-Sarab, between two great red-gold slopes, smooth as a swept floor. It hadn't been there an hour ago. Theyab stopped at the start of it and stared.

"It's for me," he said.

I sniffed the start of it. Nothing had ever walked on it. Nothing had been allowed to.

It was very straight. Desert paths are never straight. This one went east like a line drawn with a ruler, toward the place where the sun would come up over the far Hajar, like a road laid down to one single place by someone who did not intend you to wander. Like a throat.

He looked back once.

Hilat al-Sarab sat behind us in the blue light, small and dark and huddled around its well. He could make out the white ring of salt where his tent had been. At the western edge, a thread of smoke rose from Bakhit's fire straight up into the still air. And from the little mud house with the oven behind it, another thread. Umm Hanan was baking. It was the hour she always baked. Even then. Even that morning.

And along the eastern side of the village, grey.

He touched the feather mark. He touched the bell. He touched the folded grey cloth inside his shirt, where the broken feather now lay wrapped against Umm Hanan's bread, beating faintly, out of time.

"The sand remembers," he whispered.

Then he turned his back on the only home he'd ever had and walked out into the desert, and the dunes closed softly behind us. I listened for it, and I heard it: a long, slow, satisfied sound, like a mouth closing.

---

Before the sun was up, I found the other tracks.

They came over the crest of the dune on our left, down the steep face where the sand was still cold and damp from the night, and they reached the edge of the new path, and they stopped. Two neat cups in the sand, side by side. Round. Hard-edged. A donkey's hooves, and nothing else of a donkey: no drag of a tail, no second pair behind. Then they turned, and went along beside the path, a little way up the slope, keeping pace with it. Never stepping onto it. Walking where we would walk, a few paces to one side, the way a man walks beside a friend he hasn't decided yet whether to rob.

They were fresh. The smell on them was cold, cold as the air in the ruin where something had knocked. Under it, very faint, a woman's hair-oil.

My hackles went up. Theyab felt it against his leg and looked at me, and then, because he had been listening to Bakhit, he looked down.

He didn't say anything for a long while. Then he said, "It's not on the path."

No. It wasn't. It was very careful about that. I put myself between him and the slope, and we walked like that, me on the uphill side with my hair standing in a ridge, until the sun came up and the prints went over the next crest and didn't come back.

He walked in the exact middle of the path all morning after that, as if it were a beam over a well.

Not long after, as the sun cleared the eastern dunes, red and swollen, I caught a *dhub*.

It was a fat one, a spiny-tailed lizard as long as my foreleg, waddling out of its burrow to warm itself in the first light. The Bedouin roast them in the embers. I didn't. I took it behind the head with one snap and sat down in the middle of the path to eat it.

I took my time. I chewed with my mouth open. The spines of the tail made a wonderful noise, a wet, splintering crunch that echoed off the dunes, and I made sure to crunch every single one individually.

Theyab stopped and turned around.

"Do you have to?" he said.

I looked at him with the tail hanging out of my mouth. I crunched it.

"That's disgusting."

I swallowed. I licked my chops, both sides, slowly. Then I came and sniffed, very pointedly, at the back of his shirt, where Bakhit's paste was, and the blood, and the bitter mountain herb, and I sneezed, hugely, all over him.

He stared at me. And for a moment, just a moment, something happened at the very corner of his mouth. It wasn't a smile, not yet. He wouldn't truly laugh for a long time, not until he met someone who could make him. But it was the place a smile would go, one day, if things went well.

Things never go well. But they go, and sometimes there are smiles along the way, and I've learned to take those where I find them, like water seeping into a hole dug under a dune.

He turned and kept walking. I finished my lizard, and licked my lips, and trotted after him.

My tail was up. Salukis carry their tails low, mostly, curled under. Mine was up, feathering in the breeze like a flag. I'll let you work out why. And I'll tell you that I didn't let myself think the other thing, the thing I wanted to think, that this time would be different. I knew what my own hackles would do.

---

*End of Chapter Four*
