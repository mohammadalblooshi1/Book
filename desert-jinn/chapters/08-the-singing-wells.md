# Chapter Eight
## The Singing Wells

> *Drink at Bir al-Ghina', and the past will drink from you. It is always thirstier than you are.*
> — a saying of the Bedu water-finders, older than any tribe that uses it

---

The moon was a thin new crescent the night we found Bir al-Ghina'.

Thin as the smile of a trader who has just cheated you and would like you back tomorrow. Ghanim smiled like that, in Hilat al-Sarab, every time he weighed Theyab's scraps of copper on his crooked scales. The moon hung low over the western dunes like that smile, a pale scratch on the dark, and gave almost no light at all.

That suited Theyab. He'd had enough of being seen.

He'd barely spoken since the zawba'a came walking out of a clear sky and the caravan was simply *gone*. Since he'd dug one boy out of the sand and poured the Light into him and given him his last water, and the boy had drunk it, and said the word, and run.

The cracks were still fading on his forearms. Every so often he'd sniff his wrist, and I'd pretend not to see. Dogs are good at that. People think we're stupid. It saves a great deal of trouble.

The rider's waterskin had been empty since the afternoon before. Once, in the dark, I heard him say very softly, "*Yumma, it's dark*," and then stop, appalled, as if someone else had used his mouth.

Then he stopped walking.

"Do you hear that?"

I did. I'd been hearing it for some time and hoping he wouldn't.

It was a hum. Low and steady and very large, as if the whole desert were humming to itself with its mouth closed. You felt it come up through your feet into the bones of your skull, until it was hard to tell whether the hum was outside you or whether you'd always been humming and had only just noticed.

"Water," Theyab whispered.

I sat down in the sand and sighed through my nose, long and heavy. It's what a saluki does instead of saying *unfortunately*.

---

You'll want to know why a dog would be unhappy about water in the middle of the sands.

There are dunes in this country that sing. On still evenings, when the sand along a great dune's crest lets go and slides down the steep face, the whole dune *booms*: a deep droning note like a hundred men humming, like a great drum struck softly far off. Men who know the sound say it is the jinn at their music, and won't camp within earshot.

Bir al-Ghina', the Well of Singing, lies at the foot of the biggest singing dune I know. Three old well-mouths in a hollow of half-wild date palms, with the dune standing over them like a wave about to break. Two are long dry. The third is deep and sweet and cold, and its water hums with the dune all the time, even when the sand is still.

What I know is this. The water remembers. Drink it, and it will hand you back something you had forgotten so completely you didn't know there was a hole where it used to be.

I once watched a man drink there and remember, in one swallow, what he had done to his brother when they were boys. He walked into the sands that night and took no water with him. The well doesn't give you your past. It gives your past *you*.

Then Theyab stopped again, and this time it wasn't the hum.

From ahead, past the black shapes of the palms, came a snarl like tearing cloth. Then a clang of steel. Then a sound I had not expected to hear within three days' walk of any jinn.

Someone was laughing.

---

We came over the last low dune and looked down into the hollow.

The well glowed faintly between the palms, a silver mist rising off the water like breath on a cold morning. Above it the great dune stood pale in the starlight, humming. And in the middle of all that quiet, beautiful wrongness, a young man was fighting five jinn-jackals by himself.

I've told you about the emptied. Let me tell you about the jackals.

They are jackals, or the memory of jackals, pulled long and thin and wrong, made of shadow the way smoke is made of fire. Solid in the middle and fraying at the edges. You feel the cold of them before you see them. Their eyes are coals, the angry red coals you find under the ash of a tent where someone didn't get out. They make one sound only, that low wet tearing snarl. And when they bite you they don't take flesh. They take whatever you were thinking at the time.

They hunt the Light. That's what they are for.

The young man didn't seem to care.

He was tall, a few years older than Theyab, with a short dark beard, a ghutra wound round his head like a fighting man's, and a jaw that had been cut once and healed into a pale seam. He held his sword the way only the guards of great sheikhs hold them, high and easy. Or the half of it he had left. It was an old straight *sayf*, snapped a hand's width above the guard, so what remained was barely longer than a khanjar, with a jagged end like a broken tooth.

He moved like water poured from a coffee pot.

"No, not you," he was saying, sidestepping a lunge. "You. The ugly one. Yes. You know who you are."

He spun. The broken blade went through the nearest jackal's throat. It didn't bleed. It burst, the way a dropped sack of flour bursts, into a cloud of fine black ash that drifted down onto the sand. Inside the cloud, for half a breath, I heard a small surprised voice. Somebody's last thought, let go.

"Four," said the young man cheerfully. "Who's next? Don't all rush at once."

---

Theyab had dropped flat behind the crest, his hand on his scavenged knife. The stranger was enjoying himself; nothing in me rose against that laughter. But he was also bleeding from a long scratch down his left shoulder, and one of the four jackals left had begun, very quietly, to circle round behind him while the others kept him busy.

And I'll tell you what else I knew, and couldn't stop knowing. Jinn-jackals hunt the Light. They weren't here for him. They'd been lying up at Bir al-Ghina' waiting for *Theyab*, nosing the wind, for days. This stranger had only got in the way.

Everyone who stands near Theyab gets in the way, sooner or later.

I must have looked at the jackals and then at Theyab. I must have put it all in my face, because he looked at me, and I saw him understand it.

He got up.

---

By then Theyab didn't trust the Light. Worse, he had begun to be afraid of it.

Every time it saved someone, the person it saved had looked at him afterwards with the same face, and that face had a word in it. And every time, it had shown him the dune, and the dune was getting closer, and now it came with the smell.

So when he ran down that slope, what he felt first was a cold little voice with a goatherd's patience in it, saying: *He'll see. He'll see what you are, and then he'll look at you like they all do. And then he'll be gone too. Everyone near you goes.*

The light came, and it came grey.

It dribbled out of his fists like dishwater, and the jackal circling behind the young man stopped. It turned its coal eyes toward Theyab. It lowered its head and grinned, the way only shadows can, a grin that went too far back along the jaw.

Fear does that. It turns the Light into something the dark can chew.

The jackal sprang. Theyab threw himself sideways and felt its teeth snap shut beside his ear, and for a moment he couldn't remember where he was. The palms, the well, the reason he had come down the dune: gone, bitten clean out, a gap like a pulled tooth. Then it came back in a rush. Most of it. I've never been sure it was all of it.

I hit the jackal from the side.

We were bred to bring down gazelle at a full run. I took it by the back of the neck and it was like biting a winter night, and my silver eye *burned*. But it let go of him.

Over its smoking back Theyab saw the young man stagger as a second jackal caught his wounded shoulder and dragged him half off his feet.

The young man didn't cry out. He set his teeth and swung the broken sword one-handed. He kept standing up.

And Theyab thought, without any words at all, *Not him too.*

The light went gold.

---

It came up out of his chest warm and steady, like the sun climbing over a dune, and ran down his arm into the pitted blade of his knife until the iron shone like honey.

The jackal in front of him flinched.

Theyab lunged, and for the first time in his life his knife went *into* one of the jinn's creatures instead of sliding off. It burst into ash. He slammed his shoulder into the jackal that had the young man's arm, and the light went through it like lamp-oil catching.

Then there was a back against his back.

A warm, solid weight, someone else's shoulder blades pressed to his own. He felt the other boy's breathing. The last two jackals paced in a slow circle, snarling their tearing-cloth snarl, and the young man shifted his feet so the two of them could turn together, like two men working a well-rope.

The young man glanced back over his shoulder. At Theyab's face, and Theyab's arms, and the gold pouring out of Theyab's skin and lighting up the palms, the well, the great pale dune and the drifting ash.

"You glow," he said.

A pause, while a jackal feinted and drew back.

"That's weird."

"I know," said Theyab.

"I didn't say it was *bad*." The young man turned forward again. "Duck."

Theyab ducked. The broken sword whistled through the air where his head had been and took the leaping jackal across the chest. Ash rained down on them, warm, like the ash that falls round a funeral pyre in the Indian quarter at Muscat. The last jackal seemed to do some quick arithmetic, and turned to run.

I ran it down before it reached the palms. It's what I'm for.

---

The hollow went quiet. Only the dune hummed, and the well under it, patient, waiting for someone to get thirsty.

The gold drained back into Theyab's chest and left him swaying, with new cracks running up both forearms, brighter than the old ones. And then it came, the thing that always came. It didn't wait any more for him to close his eyes.

The full moon, white and enormous.

The great dune, Tal Moreeb, so high its top was lost in the sky.

Heat. Not light. Heat, real heat, climbing up through the soles of his feet as if the sand had become a griddle for bread. His own arms opening, and the skin of his palms blistering as he watched. The smell again.

And something new. A sound, below him on the dune. Someone shouting. Shouting a *name*, over and over, in a voice cracked down the middle like a dropped bowl, and he couldn't hear the name. He leaned toward it with his hair already burning, and couldn't hear it.

"Hey. *Hey.*"

A hand on his shoulder. Theyab blinked, and the desert came back. Palms, well, stars, the thin scratch of moon in the west. He was sitting in the sand. He didn't remember sitting down. His palms were stinging. There was nothing on them. The young man was crouched in front of him with the broken sword across his knees, watching him with frank, dark-eyed interest.

"You went somewhere," he said. "Your eyes did a thing. And you were saying *it's hot, it's hot*. It's freezing."

Theyab looked up at the sky. At the moon. It was thin. So very thin. He let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding.

I didn't show him how fast a moon fills. I couldn't have, and it would have been unkind. Kindness and cowardice wear the same *bisht* in the dark.

"Does it hurt?" the young man asked.

Theyab stared at him.

He nodded at the golden cracks on Theyab's arms. As if it were a perfectly ordinary thing to ask someone who'd just turned into a lamp in front of you.

In fifteen years of being Theyab, no one had ever asked him that.

"Yes," said Theyab.

The young man looked at him for a moment, and then, oddly, at me, sitting at Theyab's side with my ears up and my hackles flat. Then he nodded slowly.

"Thought so," he said. "Your face looked like mine does when I've been punched." He stood and held out a hand, and when Theyab took it, pulled him to his feet in one easy movement. "Right. Excuse me a moment. I have to collect my wages."

---

He took a small clay jar from a saddlebag by the nearest palm, knelt, and began to scoop up jackal-ash with a flat wooden spoon.

Theyab watched him for a long time.

"What are you doing?"

"Making a living." He didn't look up. "An old perfume-seller in the souk at Nizwa pays a silver thaler a pinch. He burns it with frankincense, and they say the smoke keeps the other jinn off a house for a year." He tapped the jar. "A good jackal's worth a week of water. You've just doubled my income, glowing stranger. I'd thank you, but I'll have to give you half, and I'm sad about it already."

"You can keep it."

"Absolutely not. I have principles." He thought about it. "One. It's that one."

He sealed the jar and sat back on his heels. In the silver mist off the well I saw the ring on his right hand for the first time. Too heavy and too gold for a young man who scraped jinn-ash off the sand for a living. A seal ring, cut deep with a crest I'd seen before: a tall tower with a lamp burning on top of it. I had seen that crest painted on the gates of a city most men think is a story.

I kept that to myself. You'll have noticed I keep a great deal to myself. I'm not given a choice.

"Hazza'," said the young man, and touched his own chest.

He said it aloud. Right into the open night, with the wind moving in the palm fronds over his head and the great dune humming above him.

Theyab flinched and looked round at the dark between the palms. "You shouldn't— The wind *learns* them. There's a wadi, it—"

"The wind and I have an understanding," said Hazza'. "It's never done me a single favour, and I've never asked for one." He raised his eyebrows. "You've got one too, I assume. A name. Or do I keep calling you glowing stranger?"

Theyab's hand went to his chest. He looked at the wind in the palms for a long time. I knew what he was hearing. Red stone, and a child's voice, tasting him. *Theyab. Theyab. We'll wait.*

Then he leaned in close to Hazza''s ear and said it so quietly I only just caught it. "Theyab."

"Theyab," Hazza' repeated, at a perfectly normal volume. "Wolves. Good. Easy to shout when you're about to be eaten." He looked down. "And who's this?"

That was me, by the well, cleaning jackal-ash off my forefoot with considerable dignity.

"That's Ghareeb," said Theyab.

"*The stranger*." Hazza' crouched and held out the back of his hand properly, the way a man raised among hunting dogs does. I sniffed it. Steel, and lamp-oil, and an old sadness. "He suits it. Look at those eyes. One gold, one silver. My father's falconers would say there's a jinn behind that silver one."

"People say that."

"Is there?"

Theyab looked at me. I looked at him.

"He's not my dog," said Theyab, which was not an answer, and which was true. I have never been anyone's dog. Not even the first boy's, not really. I have only ever been theirs.

"Fine," said Hazza', and shrugged. "I've had a strange year."

And he went back to binding up his shoulder with a strip torn from his headcloth, one end held in his teeth.

I decided I liked him. I'd never have said so, even if I could. I know what happens to the people I like. It was already too late for him. It usually is.

---

Theyab's knees chose that moment to remember he hadn't drunk in a day and a half. He sat down rather suddenly by the well.

The water down in the stone throat was black and still, and the worn lip, grooved deep by a thousand years of well-ropes, shivered with the hum under his hand. For a moment he could have sworn the face looking up at him from the water was smaller. Younger. Not his.

"Careful," said Hazza'. He'd stopped binding his shoulder. "You know what this is?"

"Bir al-Ghina'," said Theyab. "The old men say it remembers."

"The old men are right." Hazza' crouched beside him, and for the first time his voice wasn't joking. "The first drink's the worst." He looked down into the black. "I drank once. My first night out here. I saw my mother's face. She died when I was three. I didn't know I remembered her at all." He was quiet. "I saw her dying. That's the part no one tells you. You don't get to choose which bit you get back." Then, lighter: "Now I boil it and strain it through my headcloth. It still hums a little. It doesn't sing."

Theyab looked at the water.

I knew what he was thinking. A boy found alone in the sand had nothing behind him to remember. Whatever the well gave him couldn't be worse than the emptiness he already had.

He was wrong. Emptiness is the kindest thing this desert gives anyone.

I came and put my head on his knee.

I could have stopped him. I could have stood in front of the well with my teeth bared, and he'd have listened; he'd begun to listen to me. I didn't. I've thought about why many times, and I'll let you decide whether it was kindness.

He took the leather bucket from its hook on the palm, let it down on the rope, drew it up dripping, cupped his hands in it, and drank.

---

It was cold enough to ache. He swallowed, and the humming came inside him, filling his chest and skull until it wasn't humming any more.

It was singing.

And the desert went away.

He was small. Smaller than he had ever been, wrapped tight in something soft, warm all the way through for the first time in his life. He couldn't see faces. Only the flicker of a fire, and the low black roof of a goat-hair tent above him, and the smell of smoke and milk. And outside, very far off, a sound he didn't have a word for yet. Many voices, chanting. No words in it. Just breath, rising and falling, rising and falling, coming closer.

But he could feel a hand.

A child's hand. Small, a little sticky with dates, very warm, and it had hold of his whole fist. It wasn't letting go. It squeezed, the way you'd squeeze something to make sure it was real. Someone small had decided he was worth holding on to, and was not going to let go, whatever came through the tent flap.

And above him, somewhere in the warm dark, a man was singing.

His voice was low and rough and a little out of tune. It was a song about fire, and about wings: about a great bird the old people called the *'anqa'*, that burns and falls into its own ashes and rises out of them again. The words slid away. But the tune went into him and stayed, the way a fish-hook stays.

The chanting outside got louder. The man's voice shook, and kept singing.

The small hand squeezed harder. And then—

Then it was gone. Not let go. *Pulled*. Torn out of his fist, the small fingers dragging across his palm, and there was a scream, a child's scream, high and furious, getting farther away. And the singing stopped.

He knew that tune.

He'd heard it hummed out in the dunes, behind a smooth white mask, by a man with two grey blades who had cornered him in the dark and then, for no reason at all, let him go. He'd heard four notes of it under the roar of the zawba'a, two days ago, as a sword went past his throat.

---

Theyab came up out of the memory gasping.

He was on his knees by the well, his face wet, and not all of it was well-water. His right fist was clenched so hard his nails had cut half-moons in his palm. He couldn't open it. He sat there trying, and it wouldn't. As if it were still holding on to something that had been torn out of it fifteen years ago.

Hazza' didn't say anything.

That was the second thing he did that night that no one had ever done for Theyab. He didn't ask questions. He sat down in the sand beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the palms, and waited. After a while, without a word, he reached over and began gently prying Theyab's fingers open, one at a time, the way you'd help an old man with a stiff hand.

When the last finger came loose, Hazza' said, "Good one or bad one?"

Theyab wiped his face with the heel of his hand.

"I don't know," he said.

It was the truest thing he could have said. I lay with my chin on my paws and every hair on me as flat as water.

Later, when Hazza' had built a fire of dead palm fronds between the dry wells, Theyab came and sat by me. He didn't look at me. He kept his eyes on the flames.

"Ghareeb," he said softly. "That song. You've heard it before. Haven't you."

He'd begun to watch for my answers in my ears and my eyes and the fur along my back. He was better at it than any bearer before him. That frightened me more than the jackals.

So I yawned, wide and bored, the way any dog yawns by any fire, and put my head down on my paws, and gave him nothing to read at all. It wasn't a lie. A dog can't lie by yawning.

That's the trouble with silence. You can hide a whole child in it, and no one will ever catch you.

---

Hazza' shared his bread, hard rounds of it baked in the ashes days ago, and boiled, strained water from his little brass pot. It only hummed a little. Theyab drank it with his eyes shut, braced, and nothing came.

"You can stay, if you like," Hazza' said, poking the fire with his broken sword. He said it lightly. It mattered a great deal. "Three days at least. The guest's right." He glanced at me. "Your stranger too."

He tossed me a piece anyway. I ate it. Dignity has limits.

Theyab lay down on the far side of the fire with his patched cloak pulled over him. And I watched him do a thing I had never once seen him do in fifteen years. He fell asleep with his knife lying in the sand beside him instead of in his hand. His right hand lay loosely on the sand instead, palm up, fingers half closed, as if waiting for someone small to take it.

Hazza' sat up a while longer, turning the gold ring and looking north. Then he lay down too.

I stayed awake. Someone has to.

The dune hummed. Around midnight, the wind changed, and a long sheet of sand let go along the crest, and the whole dune *boomed*, one deep shuddering note that went on and on, and for an hour afterwards the well beneath it sounded almost exactly like breathing. I sat with my back to the fire and watched the black water and did not let either of the boys go near it.

The stars turned. Suhail climbed out of the south, the great white star that rises at summer's end and breaks the heat. And low over the western dunes, the thin moon hung like a white thread, like the first stitch in a shroud that would take exactly one turn of the sky to sew.

I didn't count the nights, that first night. I should have. If I'd begun at the beginning I'd have known exactly how many they had, and I could tell you every one.

I began the next night instead.

I've never been able to stop. Some nights I still count them, and every time, there's one less than I remember.

---

*End of Chapter Eight*
