# Chapter Thirteen
## Nameless Again

> *The loneliest grave is the one no one visits. The second loneliest is the one that visits you.*
> — a saying of the old women who wash the dead, in the villages under the Hajar

---

For four days, he didn't say a word.

He walked. That was all. In the cold before dawn, in the hammering noon sun that kills goats where they stand, at night under a moon already beginning to thin, as if something had taken a bite out of it. He let the dunes choose the direction. If the sand was writing that week, it was writing for a boy who had stopped reading.

Hazza's sword was tied across his back, wrapped in his ghutra. It knocked against his spine with every step, and every time it did he flinched, and every time he flinched he kept walking. It had a rhythm to it after a while. Like a knock on a door. Like someone behind him, tapping his shoulder, wanting to be let back in.

He'd buried Hazza' the way the poor are buried, in a scrape, with no stone, and the wind had finished it. Then he'd walked away with the travellers' word chasing him the way stones had once chased him out of Hilat al-Sarab.

*Prince-Killer.*

He'd been called a lot of things. Nameless. Son of the sand. Monster. Now he had a new one, and the worst part, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, was that he couldn't be sure it was wrong.

I followed a dune behind. I'd have walked closer, but I wasn't walking well.

---

You remember what his light did to my jinn half in the storm.

There was a patch on my left side, below the silver eye, where the coat had come away. What was underneath wasn't dog. It was dark and faintly smoking, like a coal dropped in water that hadn't decided whether to go out. Sometimes, at night, when it was very quiet, I could hear it whispering. Not to me. To itself. The same three words, over and over, in a voice I knew.

It hadn't healed. I'd stopped expecting it to.

The night of the full moon had made it worse. I'll say that much and no more.

And my shadow, since the storm, lay a hand's width longer than the sun said it should. A goatherd we passed stared at it, then drove his goats the long way round.

So I limped. And he walked. And neither of us looked at my shadow.

---

On the second day he found water: a wet finger to the wind, a hollow where a desert gourd trailed its green ropes and its bitter yellow fruit, digging until the sand went dark. I dug beside him. I wasn't going to stop being useful just because I'd stopped being forgiven.

At night he hardly slept. He sat at the top of whatever dune he'd stopped on, and with one finger he wrote in the sand.

The same word, every night. Four letters, right to left, slow and careful, the way you write a thing you've only just learned, tongue between his teeth. *Ha*, round as a knot. *Zay*, a little hook with its one dot riding on top. *Alif*, standing straight as a tent pole. *Ayn*, the open mouth at the end. Hazza' had made him practise it fifty times by the fire, laughing every time he forgot the dot on the *zay*. *Without the dot it's a different letter, Theyab. You've written a word that isn't me. It's my name. Have some respect.* He never forgot the dot anymore.

Hazza'.

And every morning the dunes had moved in the night, and the word was gone.

---

On the third morning, the word was still there.

Not where he'd written it. He'd written it on the crest, where he sat. This one was at the bottom of the slope, fifty paces down, in a smooth bowl of sand where the wind hadn't reached. Four letters. The dot on the *zay*. Pressed in deep with one finger, the way he pressed.

"Did you write this?" he said. Not to me, really. To the air.

I hadn't. I have paws. And I'd been beside him all night, and I'd have sworn on every bearer I ever buried that he never moved.

But there were his own footprints, going down to it from where we'd slept, and none coming back. I put my nose to them. They smelled of him. Only of him. And very faintly, under that, of cold ash.

He rubbed it out with his foot, carefully, until the sand was smooth. Then he looked at his finger for a while, as if it belonged to somebody else.

That night he didn't write anything at all.

---

On the third night he tried to lie.

He was sitting with his knees up and the sword across his lap, and he said, so quietly I nearly missed it, "It wasn't my fault."

My hackles came up before I'd decided anything. A growl started in my chest, low as distant thunder.

His face twisted. His hand flew to his chest. The feather mark flared under his shirt, hot and sharp, and he doubled over and pressed his palm against it until the light went out and there was a smell in the air of scorched cotton.

Most people get to tell themselves small kind lies in the dark. *There was nothing I could have done.* Lies like that are how people get up in the morning. Theyab never got any. The truth was all he had: he had walked away, and watched the fire go out, and called it nothing.

"The sand remembers," he whispered afterwards. The old words.

The mark didn't burn. It wasn't a lie. My hackles lay down.

But I think he'd started to hear it the other way round. Not *may the sand remember you*, like a blessing. *The sand remembers*, like a threat.

I limped up the slope and lay down beside him, close, and pushed my long nose under his hand until his palm was over my skull.

It's all a dog has. We can't say *it wasn't your fault.* We can only stay there, and let you feel our hair lying flat.

He didn't look at me.

"Don't," he said.

I kept my head under his hand. *Feel my back,* I thought at him. *I'm not lying to you.*

"You were like that at Ghidd al-Zujaj too," he said. "Right up until you weren't."

I had nothing for that. One small, merciful lie in fifteen years, told with my whole body and not a single word, and it had cost me every true thing I'd tell him afterwards. Remember that, if you ever think a lie is cheap.

He was quiet a while. Then, without looking at me: "Where were you?"

"That night," he said. "After I walked away. You weren't with him. You weren't with me. Where were you?"

I lifted my head out from under his hand.

I'm not going to tell you what I nearly did, or where I was. Not yet. It's my story, so I get to choose. That's the only power a storyteller has: to make you wait in the dark.

I put my head down on my paws.

He nodded slowly, as though I'd answered. Then he lay down with his back to me and the broken sword in his arms, and I lay beside him until Suhail went down behind the dunes and the stars went pale, and the burned place in my side whispered its three words to itself, and neither of us made a sound at all.

---

There was a twist of waxed cloth at the bottom of his bag. Three dates rolled in honey, the last of the ones he'd carried all the way from Hilat al-Sarab, gone sticky and dark.

On the fourth evening he took them out and held them in his palm for a long time.

Then he did something I didn't understand until later. He put one to his lips, just touched it there, and closed his eyes, and I watched his face go hunting for something. Searching, the way a tongue searches for a missing tooth.

He was trying to remember what honey tasted like. The ash-blade had taken it in the storm, and it had come back, mostly. *Mostly*. He was checking.

I don't know what he found. He wrapped them up again without eating any. Maybe he was saving them. Or else he was afraid to taste them and find nothing there at all.

---

The fourth night was the one I'd been afraid of.

He stopped at the bottom of a long slope instead of the top. In fifteen years he had always slept high. Now he walked down into the cold trough between two dunes, where the wind poured sand down the slip-face in hissing curtains, like something being poured over a body.

And he lay down.

On his back, sword on his chest, arms folded over it, looking up at the stars. He let the sand come.

It came softly. A trickle over his feet. A drift against his side. The dunes were moving the way they move every night, and he had lain down in the path of one, and he was very still, and he was watching the Pleiades with an expression I'd seen before on other faces, a long time ago. Relief. That's the word. It's the most frightening expression a living face can make.

I barked. Salukis hardly bark; we were bred to run down gazelle without a sound. I barked anyway, sharp and ugly, right in his face, and shoved my nose under his jaw.

He didn't answer.

The sand was over his ankles now. His knees. It was spilling into the folds of his shirt, into the hollow of his throat.

The dead go into the sand the same day. That's what desert people do. He'd scraped a grave for Hazza' too shallow to keep the wind out. So he was giving the sand the next best thing.

I got my teeth in his sleeve and pulled with everything I had, which that night was less than a puppy has. My burned side split and smoked and I yelped, and his arm didn't move.

I could have let it out. You're thinking it. I was thinking it. My shadow could have flung that dune aside in three strokes.

He'd seen that shadow once, and his light had nearly burned it out of the world. To wake with that thing standing over him in the dark might be what finished him.

So I stood there in the sliding sand, shaking, a thin burned dog barking at a boy who wouldn't listen, while the desert buried him an inch at a time.

And the light came.

---

It wasn't his light. That's what I knew first.

His light starts at the mark and pours outward. This came from the slope beside him, from nowhere at all. It was soft and gold, and it didn't burn. It glowed the way a lamp glows in the doorway of a tent when someone is waiting up for you.

And it was cold. That's what nobody puts in the songs. The sand around it frosted. I watched the frost spread out from it in fine white feathers, and my breath came out of me in a cloud, and every hair on my body stood up at once, and then, one by one, lay down again.

I didn't see anyone in it. I only saw the light on Theyab's face, and his lips move.

Hazza' was sitting on the slope beside him, cross-legged, the way he sat by the fire when he was about to say something annoying. Light came through the edges of him, like a cloth hung in front of the sun.

He was grey. Theyab told me that, and then he wouldn't tell me any more about it for a long time. Grey, like the hollow that morning. And the golden cracks were still in him, running across his face and down his throat, and they were glowing again. Brighter than they'd been. As if the thing that had been trying to get out of them had nearly made it.

His hand was bare. No ring. Theyab noticed that. He couldn't stop noticing it.

"You look terrible," said Hazza'. "That's weird."

"You're lying in a hole," said Hazza'. "Letting a dune sit on you. Do you know how stupid that looks from where I am? Very. It looks very stupid. Even the dog thinks so. Look at him."

"I left you," Theyab whispered.

Hazza' stopped smiling.

"Yes," he said. "You did."

"I left you," Theyab said again, "and you died."

"I know. I was there." Hazza' leaned forward. He laid his hand on Theyab's shoulder, and it was *warm*. Theyab swore to me it was warm, warm as a living hand, warm as the sun on a cold winter morning, though the frost was spreading out from him across the sand the whole time. "Get up anyway."

"Who did it?" Theyab said. "Hazza'. Who—"

Hazza's face went still. He opened his mouth, and for a moment nothing came out of it but the smell of cold ash, and his eyes went somewhere past Theyab's shoulder, into the dark, and stayed there. As if someone were standing there. As if he'd seen them before.

"I can't," he said, and for the first time he sounded young, and frightened, and seventeen. "I don't think I'm allowed. I think there's a lot I'm not allowed." His grip tightened. "Get up, Theyab."

"I can't."

"Yes, you can. You're just choosing not to. It's different." His eyes were very bright, and very pale, and they didn't blink. Not once, the whole time. "Never give up, Theyab. You promised."

Theyab shut his eyes.

He had. Under the full moon, on the last night, Hazza' had said *Promise me you'll never give up*, and he had said *I promise*. And he could not lie. Not even lying in the sand with a dune on his chest.

He had promised. So it was true. So he had to make it true.

When he opened his eyes, the slope was dark and empty, and there was only a dog standing knee-deep in the sand beside him, ears up, every hair along his back lying flat and smooth as combed silk.

I hadn't seen anything. I'll swear to that.

But I hadn't growled once, the whole time that light was on the slope. And I growl at mirages. I growl at anything pretending to be what it isn't.

And the frost was still there on the slope, in the shape of someone sitting cross-legged. It stayed there until the sun came up and took it.

---

He got up.

He dug himself out with his hands, the way he'd once dug himself out of collapsed rooms in the old ruins, and climbed the slope on hands and knees, dragging the sword.

Then he looked at me. Actually looked, for the first time in four days.

"Did you see him?" he said.

I looked back at him. I didn't wag. I didn't lower my head. I just looked, and he read it, the way the bearers always half read me.

"No," he said slowly. "But you didn't growl."

I lay down at his feet with my chin on his ankle. My back was flat.

He looked at it a long moment. "He was warm," he said. "He was warm, Ghareeb. And everything around him was freezing." He swallowed. "Is that how it's supposed to be?"

I've walked with eleven bearers. I've seen a great many things come back to the people who loved them. Some of them were what they looked like.

I didn't know. I couldn't have told him if I had. I put my head down and closed my good eye, and he let it be an answer.

He looked away over the dunes, but he didn't turn his back on me. That night he slept with me curled against his side, one hand on the sword and one on my ribs, and twice I felt him wake and look at the slope where the frost had been.

---

This next part is where I went wrong.

In the morning he walked on, and I couldn't keep up.

The dune had cost me more than I'd thought. By mid-morning I'd folded up in the thin shade of a calotropis with my legs not listening, and he was four dunes ahead. I told myself I'd catch up when the heat broke.

Which is why I was a long way off, lying on a ridge, when the lamps came.

You don't see lamps lit in daylight. That's what made me lift my head. A string of camels coming over the eastern dunes, with brass lamps hung from their saddles still burning in the full morning sun, the way the lamps burn on the walls of Ubar. Water skins so fat they sloshed.

They came straight to him. Not near him. *To* him, as if they'd known exactly which dune a tired boy would be standing on.

The lead camel knelt. A man climbed down.

He was old, his beard silver and combed long over a bisht the deep blue of the sky just after sunset. His face was soft and kind and lined, someone's grandfather. His voice was low and gentle, the kind that makes you lean in.

And his eyes were grey.

Faded grey, flat and pale, like cloth left in the sun for a hundred years. Like the eyes of the emptied, before the white sets in.

The hair along my spine stood straight up, and the burned place in my side stopped whispering. All at once. Just went silent, the way the crickets go silent when something walks past in the dark.

Behind the old man, another figure climbed down: the biggest man I had ever seen. Shaved head under a plain cloth. Arms like the trunks of date palms. A face that didn't look as though it had ever made an expression it meant.

"Take the boy's bag," the old man said softly. "Can't you see he can barely stand?"

The giant lifted the bag gently from Theyab's shoulder and carried it back to the baggage camel and tied it there, among the water skins.

Remember that. I didn't. I was watching faces. I should have been watching the bag. The bag with Old Bakhit's bell in it.

---

"You are the boy who glows," the old man said.

Theyab didn't answer. His hand had gone to the hilt of the broken sword on his back.

"My name is Muhaisen," said the old man. "I am the wazir of Ubar. I serve Sheikh Mutlaq." A pause. "You'll have heard my name. From him."

He had. Hazza' had told him the whole story by the fire: the stable boy Suhail he'd hidden and tried to cure, the broken sword, and the soft old voice of the wazir calling it treason, calling it jinn-work and sorcery, until his father believed it.

"Yes," said Muhaisen. "I named him traitor." He bowed his silver head. "I was wrong. I've spent a month crossing this desert to tell him so. And I came too late."

He reached into his bisht and held something out on his open palm, and I saw Theyab's whole body go still.

A ring. Heavy gold, worn smooth, the tower-seal of Ubar cut into its face.

Hazza's signet ring. The one he never took off.

"He gave me this," Muhaisen said gently.

On the ridge, my hackles lifted.

I *think* they lifted. I'm nearly sure. But my fur had been standing since I'd seen those eyes, and a growl that's already in you can't rise any higher. I was a long way off, and in pain, and afraid. All of that is true. None of it is an excuse. I've had a very long time to try to make it one.

"When?" said Theyab. His voice cracked on it.

"On the night of the full moon," said Muhaisen. "I found him at last. By his little fire. We spoke. He forgave me, I think, or began to. And he gave me his ring and said, *If anything happens to me, find the boy who glows. Look after him.*" The old man's voice shook, very slightly, in exactly the right place. "I didn't understand what he meant. Not until morning."

There was no bell. Old Bakhit's bell was in a patched bag tied to a camel twenty paces away, among the water skins, and it made no sound. I've wondered since whether a bell can be smothered the way a voice can.

Theyab reached out, slowly, the way you reach for something that might vanish. Muhaisen let him take the ring. It was warm from the old man's robe. Theyab closed his fingers over it, and his face crumpled.

"Did you kill him?" Muhaisen asked quietly. Almost kindly. "They're saying you did. They're saying it in every majlis from here to the coast."

Theyab's eyes were streaming. He could have said *no*, and it would have been true.

But the boy could not lie, and in his heart, the truth was something else.

"I left him," he said.

Muhaisen nodded slowly. "I know," he said. "So did I, once, a long time ago. Someone I loved." His grey eyes were wet. "Everyone leaves someone, child. It's what we do afterwards that matters."

He held out his hand. After a moment, Theyab gave the ring back. Muhaisen tucked it inside his robe, carefully, close to his heart.

"It should go to his father," he said. "And his brother. I'll see that it does. But you need food, and shade, and sleep. There's an oasis town two days east, Al-Nakhlatain. I have a house there. Come with me. You'll be my guest, and a guest is sacred for three days, and longer if I say so." A pause, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck. "And when you're stronger, I'll tell you about your father."

Theyab's head came up.

"I knew him," said Muhaisen. "Long ago. He carried what you carry. I can tell you who he was. If you want to know."

---

By the time I got there, Theyab was already climbing onto a camel.

I came down the slope in a sort of controlled fall, my side smoking. Muhaisen's soft grey eyes found me, and he smiled.

"Ah," he said. "The old saluki. Of course."

There was no surprise in it at all. He looked at the burned place on my side, the dark showing through, and then, slowly, down at my shadow lying a hand's width too long on the sand. He looked at it the way you'd look at a door you already had the key to.

I stood at the camel's feet with every hair on my back on end and my lips drawn off my teeth, and I growled. Not loud. But long, and low, the growl every shepherd knows means *there is a wolf on the far side of this fold.* I looked up at the boy: one hand on the halter rope, the other pressed flat over his mark.

I could have done a great many things. Bitten the old man's ankle. Stood in front of the camel until someone had to kill me to get it past. I've had a long time to make a list.

I growled. That was all.

Theyab looked down at me. He looked tired beyond anything a boy of fifteen should be. "You growl at everyone," he said.

That was true too. Mostly.

"And you were quiet as a stone once," he said, more quietly. "At the carvings. Remember?"

He turned the camel's head east and followed the old man's lamps over the dune.

I stood there in the sand a long moment. Then I followed. What else was I going to do? I'd been following him for fifteen years.

The giant walked at the back of the line. He didn't look round once. But at the top of the dune he stopped, and he waited, very patiently, until I'd passed him, so that he was behind me all the way.

I didn't know then what it would cost, that one small, cowardly, honest growl, and all the things I didn't do after it. I know now. I'll tell you. Not yet.

The burned place in my side didn't whisper again all that day. Not once.

It was listening.

And above us the moon was thinning, night by night, like a face turning slowly away, and I had started counting again.

*End of Chapter Thirteen*
