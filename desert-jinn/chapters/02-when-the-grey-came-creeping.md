# Chapter Two
## When the Grey Came Creeping

> *The grey does not bite. The grey forgets for you. And then it wears what is left.*
> — words cut into the stone lip of an old well in the sands, most of them worn away

---

Nobody in Hilat al-Sarab believed him.

Nobody there had believed anything Theyab said in fifteen years. Never mind that he couldn't lie. They'd never bothered to notice.

He ran into the village before dawn with sand in his hair and banged on the wooden door of the sheikh's house until a lamp was lit. It was the only house in the village with a wind-tower and a majlis, and Theyab had never been inside it. He stood on the step and told the sheikh what he had seen. A line of grey on the horizon, low and flat, and the stars going out above it one by one.

He didn't tell him about the voice in the wind. There was no way to say it that didn't sound like a jinn had got into him, and everyone already thought one had.

Sheikh Mazyad, a heavy man with a beard like a thornbush hennaed orange at the ends, listened with his eyes half closed. Then he said, "Sea fog. Off the sabkha."

"The sabkha's four days away," said Theyab.

"Dust, then. A storm out of the south."

"It wasn't moving like a storm. It was—" Theyab searched for the word. "Listening."

Mazyad looked at him the way you'd look at a goat that had started reciting poetry. Then something crossed his face, quick and ugly, and he glanced past Theyab's shoulder toward the east. I smelled it come off him. Not a lie exactly. Fear, which is the lie's older brother. "Go back to your tent, Laqeet," he said, and shut the door. I heard the bar drop across it on the other side.

I was lying in the shade of the wall across the lane. I could have howled the village awake. I could have barked in the square until every child woke screaming.

But I have rules. The bearer has to see it first. He has to be the one who knows. The times I've broken that rule, things went worse. Or so I tell myself. I've had a great deal of time to practise.

So I did nothing. I'm not proud of that. It's becoming a theme.

---

The grey came the next day.

Not all at once. The Ifrit's curse doesn't charge like a raiding party. It *seeps*. It arrives the way cold arrives in a tent on a winter night in the sands: first at the edges, then along the floor-mats, until one moment you realise you can't remember the last time you were warm. Or what warm was. Or why you'd want it.

It started with the colour.

Umm Hanan noticed first, though no one asked her. She came out of her house before the sun with a basin of dough on her hip, and stopped. The door of the house beside hers had been painted indigo by a man who had died twenty years ago, painted the deep blue that keeps off the eye. It wasn't blue any more. It wasn't any colour at all. It was the flat dead grey of old ash in a cold hearth.

Every morning since the boats came home without her men, Umm Hanan had said their names under her breath as she kneaded the dough. Her husband. Her two sons. Three names, in the same order, slapped into the bread like salt. That morning she said her husband's name. She said her eldest son's name. She reached for the third and found only a smooth place in her mind, like a tooth-socket the tongue keeps returning to.

She stood in the lane with her hands in the dough for a long time. Then she went back inside and didn't come out again until evening.

By noon, the whole eastern edge of the village had gone grey.

The red cloth over Ghanim's stall was grey. The date palms in the grove were grey, the clusters of ripening dates hanging from them like bunches of small stones. A goat wandered out of the grey part of the village and stood in the square, and its brown coat was the colour of cold porridge, and its eyes were the white of a boiled egg. It stood with its head at a slightly wrong angle, as if it were listening to something underneath the ground, and when a child threw a date-stone at it, it didn't flinch. It simply turned its white eyes, very slowly, toward the child, and kept them there.

The child's mother carried him inside. The goat watched the door all day.

And it was quiet. In the grey part of the village there was no sound at all. No wind in the palm fronds. No doves. No creak of doors.

People began to gather in the square. They talked in low, nervous voices. An old woman said she'd heard stories from her grandmother's grandmother about a thing that came up out of the deep sands, an Ifrit's breath that made people forget, and that you knew it was close when you couldn't say the names of your dead. Sheikh Mazyad told her to stop talking nonsense, too loudly. I noticed that he did not try to say the names of his own.

Theyab stood at the very edge of the crowd, where he always stood, close enough to hear and far enough to run.

His hand was pressed flat against his chest.

The feather mark had been burning since the dune. A slow, low heat, like a coal banked under ash, and it got hotter whenever he looked east. Every time he closed his eyes he smelled honey and hot metal. *My birthmark is warm and I keep smelling something burning* wasn't the kind of thing that made people trust a boy they already thought was jinn-born.

"It's moving," someone said.

Everyone turned.

At the eastern end of the square, the grey had reached the first house. It climbed the mud wall the way damp climbs a cloth. It slid across a child's toy left in the dirt, a little camel knotted out of palm frond, dyed yellow with turmeric. The toy had been yellow. It wasn't any more. It had been lying on its side. When the grey had passed, it was standing upright, facing the crowd.

Nobody had touched it.

"Everyone inside," said Sheikh Mazyad. "Shut your doors. Put out your lamps. Keep the children away from the windows."

As if a door could stop it.

---

The sun went down red and swollen over the western dunes. Night came on. And with the night came the emptied.

They were people. That's what you must understand first. Every one of them had been a person once, a herder or a date-farmer or somebody's son, until the Ifrit's curse found them and ate their names. It takes the names first, then the memories, then all the small invisible ropes that tie one person to another, the way a tent is tied down against the wind. What's left still walks. It still has a face. But the eyes are white and blank as boiled eggs, and the skin is the colour of ash, and the face doesn't do anything.

Except, sometimes, smile.

That's the worst of it. Not the eyes. The smile. Somewhere in what's left there's a memory of what faces are *for*, and every so often one of the emptied will try. The corners of the mouth go up, too far and too slowly, like a tent flap being lifted by someone who has never seen a tent. The eyes don't change. Nothing behind them changes. It's a smile the way a footprint is a foot.

And they talk. Not to you. They say the last thing they said before the grey took them, over and over, in their own voices, worn smooth, like a bead rolled between fingers until its pattern is gone. A trader might walk for a hundred years saying *"Two annas, and that's my last price."* A mother might say *"Come in now, it's getting dark."* It isn't a message. It's what's left when the person is gone: one sentence, rattling around in the empty house like a date-stone in a dry gourd.

They are drawn to light, like moths that never, ever stop.

They came out of the grey just after the last of the sunset went. First one, walking slowly up the eastern lane. Then three. Then a dozen, and more behind them, a grey crowd moving between the houses with no sound but the murmur of their dozens of last sentences, all overlapping, like a market heard through a mud wall.

They had no smell. That was how I knew them before I saw them. Forty bodies walking up a lane and not one of them smelling of sweat or camel or smoke.

Theyab saw them from the top of the dune behind his tent. He saw the shapes drifting through the dark lanes, stopping at doors, laying their palms flat against the wood and standing there, as though listening for a heartbeat. He saw them turn, all together, toward the one house that had forgotten to put out its lamp.

He was running before he'd decided to.

---

I went with him. Of course I went with him. I'd been going with him for fifteen years.

*This is a bad idea*, I thought at him, loping alongside him down the slope with my ears flat. *I want that on record. For the funeral. A short speech, I thought: he was warned.*

He didn't look at me. His whole face was fixed on the village, his knife already in his hand. The rag-hilted khanjar, rusted along the spine, its edge sharpened on a flat stone from the wadi.

A knife. Against an Ifrit's curse.

I ran faster, and listened to the voices coming up the lane to meet us, and tried very hard not to recognise any of them.

---

The house with the lamp belonged to the well-keeper.

Nadim's house.

Theyab came round the corner of the lane and stopped so suddenly that I nearly went into the backs of his legs.

The emptied had surrounded it. Twenty of them, perhaps more, standing in a silent half-circle around the door, their white eyes all fixed on the yellow light leaking from the shutters. They swayed together, the way the long grass sways on a dune after rain. From inside, Theyab could hear someone crying. A small child, by the sound. And a man's voice, low and frightened, saying *hush, hush, hush*.

And out in the lane, under the murmuring, three or four of the grey shapes had begun to say it too.

*"Hush. Hush. Hush."*

Not mocking. Just learning it. The way the wind had learned Theyab's name.

In front of the door, between the emptied and the house, stood Nadim.

He had the long iron spade they used to clear blown sand from the mouth of the well. He held it like a sword, shaking so badly the blade rattled.

"Get back," he was saying. His voice cracked in half on every word. "Get *back*. I mean it. Get back."

The emptied didn't get back.

One of them stepped forward. Slow. Unhurried. It had been a woman once, a grey woman in a grey dress, with grey silver bangles still on her grey wrists, and the black mask that the Bedouin women wear, the burqa'a, hanging grey across the bridge of a grey nose. Her lips were moving.

*"Mind the step, my eyes,"* she said. *"Mind the step, my eyes. Mind the step, my eyes."*

She said it kindly. She'd said it to some child once, at some tent's edge or some doorway, and then the grey had come up behind her, and her life had stopped on that one sentence like a camel kneeling in the sand that will not get up again.

She reached out a hand toward Nadim's face. And she smiled.

Nadim swung the spade.

It connected with a dull, hollow thud, like hitting a sack of wet sand. The woman's head went sideways, much too far sideways, and stayed there. She didn't fall. She didn't stop smiling. She straightened, slowly, her head coming back up with a soft grinding sound, and reached out again, and this time her fingers brushed Nadim's cheek.

*"Mind the step, my eyes."*

Nadim went rigid.

I watched it happen. The colour drained out of the side of his face where she'd touched it, as if someone had wiped away henna with a wet thumb and left the clay underneath. His eyes went wide and lost. The spade slid out of his hands and fell in the sand.

"I—" Nadim said. He looked down at his hands. "I was… I was doing something. What was I…"

He had forgotten. Not everything. Not yet. A touch from the emptied steals a little at a time: a memory here, a feeling there. I could see him searching for what he'd lost, patting at his own mind the way you pat your sash for a coin that isn't there. He had forgotten *why* he was standing there. He had forgotten there was a child crying behind the door who needed him.

The child cried harder. Nadim didn't turn around. He'd forgotten there was anyone to turn around for.

The grey woman reached for him again.

And Theyab moved.

---

He didn't know anything. He was fifteen and terrified, and the boy who'd thrown stones at him since he was five was being erased in front of him one memory at a time. Theyab simply could not stand there and watch.

So he ran into the middle of twenty of the emptied with a rusty knife.

He slashed at the grey woman's arm. The blade passed through her sleeve and hit something that wasn't quite flesh, cold and dense like the packed mud of a house wall. It left a thin line. No blood. Inside the line, for a moment, there was nothing at all. Not darkness. Nothing. Then it closed.

She didn't even look at him.

But the others did.

All of them, at once. Twenty grey faces turned slowly from the lamp in the window toward the boy in the middle of the lane, and twenty mouths stopped murmuring in the same instant, and the silence that came down was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

Then, one by one, they began to smile at him.

They were drawn to light. I've told you that.

And somewhere under Theyab's shirt, the feather mark had started to glow.

---

He felt it before he saw it. A heat in his chest that wasn't fear, though there was plenty of fear too. It was a *burning*. It started at the centre of the mark and spread outward along his ribs and up his throat and down into his arms, like swallowing a coal from the coffee hearth and feeling it catch.

The emptied came toward him. Their voices started again, all at once, all different, all close. *"Two annas and that's my last price." "Come in now, it's getting dark." "Mind the step, my eyes." "Who left the goat pen open?" "Hush. Hush."*

He backed up until his shoulders hit the wall of the house. The knife shook in front of him, and the burning in his chest was getting worse—

And the light came.

Not gold. Not at first.

It flickered out of his skin, out of the cracks of his clenched fists, and it was *grey*. A thin, dim, sickly grey, like light seen through a dirty water-skin. It sputtered. And the emptied didn't slow. They came faster, the way a falcon comes faster at a bustard that has started to limp. Some of them tilted their heads at it, almost tenderly, as if the grey light were a word in their own language.

That's what fear does to the Light. It makes it smell like home to them. I should have warned him. I couldn't have. I should have anyway.

The grey woman reached for Theyab's face. Her fingers were an inch from his eye. He could smell her: dust, and cold stone, and very faintly, underneath, the rose-oil she must have worn once on feast days.

Theyab shut his eyes.

And behind the door, the child cried out. One word, high and thin and broken.

"*Nadim!*"

Something in Theyab's chest *turned over*.

I saw his eyes snap open, and I saw him look past the emptied to Nadim. Nadim, standing lost and grey-cheeked in the lane, staring at his own hands. A boy who had forgotten someone he loved.

Theyab knew exactly what that was like. He'd spent his whole life being the someone nobody remembered.

He stopped thinking about himself.

And the light went *gold*.

---

It came out of him like the sun coming up over the dunes.

One moment the lane was dark and grey and full of smiling shapes. The next it was blazing. Golden light poured out of Theyab's hands, out of his chest, out of the cracks between his fingers, out of his eyes. It filled the lane from wall to wall. It lit every face, every grey robe and every white eye, and threw their shadows huge and black against the mud walls. For one instant I saw the shadows clearly, and they were not the shapes of the people casting them. They had too many joints. They were all leaning toward him.

The emptied *recoiled*.

They didn't scream. They can't. But they flinched back from the light the way your hand flinches from a hot stone, all of them at once, stumbling and lurching away into the dark. The grey woman with the bangles staggered backward. And for one heartbeat, I saw her eyes change. The white faded, and underneath was brown, ordinary brown, the eyes of a woman who had once worn silver bangles because she liked the sound they made when she ground the coffee.

She looked at Theyab. Her mouth opened. For a moment I thought she would say something new, something that was hers.

"*Where are my*—" she said.

Then the grey closed back over her like water over a dropped stone, and the smile came back, slow and wrong, and she said, *"Mind the step, my eyes,"* and was gone with the others into the night.

I have wondered for a long time what the end of that sentence was. Children, probably. It usually is.

---

The light faded.

It drew back into Theyab the way water drains into sand.

He slid down the wall and sat in the dirt.

His hands were shaking. There were thin golden lines on his palms, like the cracks in a glazed water-jar, faintly glowing. They hurt the way a burn hurts the next day. He stared at them as if they belonged to someone else.

And then it happened. The thing that would happen every time he used the Light, for the rest of his life.

He saw fire.

A full white moon, enormous, filling half the sky. A dune higher than any dune he had ever seen. And heat, inside him, rising up his throat like something climbing out. He could feel his own skin tightening. He could feel the fine hairs on his arms curl and shrivel. He could taste honey, and under the honey, smoke.

It lasted less than a breath. Then he was sitting in the dirt of Hilat al-Sarab, gasping, his hands slapping at his own arms to put out a fire that wasn't there.

He thought it was a trick of a tired mind. He would learn otherwise. I lay down beside him with my flank against his leg, and I counted, as I always do, and the number was still twelve.

---

The door of the well-keeper's house opened.

Saqi stood there, an oil lamp in one hand and a butchering knife in the other, his face white. Behind him, peeking around his legs, was Nadim's little brother Shibl, a boy of seven, his face streaked with tears.

Around the lane, other doors were opening. A light bright as noon had come through the shutters and brought the neighbours out.

They looked at the lane. At the empty sand where twenty of the emptied had stood, now marked with footprints that all faced the same way. At Nadim, still standing there, blinking slowly, one side of his face grey as ash.

And at Theyab, sitting against the wall with golden cracks glowing in his palms.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Nadim turned his head and looked down at Theyab. His eyes were clearing. What the grey woman had taken was seeping back. I saw him remembering the spade, the door, his brother crying. I saw him remembering the light.

He opened his mouth.

I like to think, even now, that it might have been *thank you*.

But he never got the chance, because that was when I did the thing I had told myself, for fifteen years, I would never do in front of them.

---

I looked at forty frightened faces staring at the boy against the wall, and I smelled what was rising off them, and it wasn't gratitude. It was the beginning of stones. I wanted them looking at something else.

So I climbed onto the well-keeper's garden wall, where the lamp could reach me, and I sat back on my haunches, and I lifted my muzzle at the black sky where the stars were going out, and I howled.

A saluki does not howl like other dogs. We bay, high and broken. But that is not what came out of me.

What came out of me was a man's voice.

An old man's voice, cracked and deep, rising and falling in the long wordless keening the Bedouin women make over a grave, except it was a man making it, and it was coming out of a dog. It went up the lane and over the roofs and out into the grey, and the grey didn't swallow it. It was the only sound in the world the grey didn't swallow. It went on far longer than any animal has breath for. Somewhere in it there were almost words, old ones. I don't always know what lives in the silver half of me.

The camels in the pens behind the houses screamed. Every goat in the village went silent at once.

When I stopped, nobody in the lane was breathing.

Then I jumped down off the wall and trotted, quite calmly, past Theyab, past Nadim, past the line of footprints, to the end of the lane where the grey began. I walked into it. I felt it close over me, cold and smooth, the way it always does, like being licked by something with no tongue. I walked ten paces in, and turned around, and walked ten paces out, and sat down in front of the crowd and scratched behind my ear.

I was still sand-coloured. My one amber eye was still amber. The grey had not taken so much as a hair.

That's the thing about the grey and me. It knows what I am, better than I do. It doesn't eat its own.

Somebody screamed.

I think it was Ghanim. It was a very high scream for such a large man.

"*Jinn*," a woman whispered. "There's a jinn in the dog."

"There's a jinn in both of them," said someone else, and I smelled it come off the whole crowd at once, the thing I'd been trying to stop, only worse now. I'd poured oil on it. Good work, Ghareeb. Fifteen years of silence, and the first time you open your mouth you hand them a reason.

And then everyone was shouting at once, and mothers were dragging children back indoors, and Sheikh Mazyad came barrelling down the lane in his nightshirt bellowing for order. Through all of it, Theyab sat in the dirt against the wall and stared at me with his glowing hands in his lap.

I came and lay down beside him again. Our shoulders touched.

"You," he said. His voice was barely a whisper. "What are you?"

I looked at him with both my eyes, the amber and the silver.

"You knew," he said. "All this time. You always knew what was coming. Didn't you."

I couldn't tell him anything. I never have been able to tell any of them anything. But I could have howled sooner.

I looked at the golden cracks in his palms, and at the mark on his chest, still faintly warm under his shirt. I looked at his face. It was very young, and very frightened.

I have sat beside eleven children like this, at the moment they understood their life was not going to be the life they'd planned. The Falconer asked me, very politely, whether he could go home now.

It doesn't get easier. You'd think it would.

I put my head on his knee. I wanted him to have had a little longer. That's all. Just a little longer, before it started.

He put his hand on my neck, the way he'd done in Ghanim's stall. He didn't say anything else. But his hand stayed there, and it was shaking, and he didn't take it away.

---

Somewhere at the edge of the village, out where the grey was thickest, I heard a sound that brought every hair on my body up at once.

It was a child's voice. Small and confused, calling out into the empty night.

"Yumma?" it said. "Nadim? Where… where is everyone? Who…"

A pause.

"Who am I?"

And then, from all around it, very softly, from the grey shapes standing out there in the dark, a dozen voices that were learning said it back.

*"Who am I?"*

*"Who am I?"*

*"Who am I?"*

Beside me, Nadim had gone completely still.

Because his little brother, the one who had been crying behind the door, wasn't behind the door any more. In all the shouting, in all the screaming about jinn and dogs, he had slipped out past his father's legs to find his big brother.

And he had walked straight into the grey, the way he'd just watched a dog do.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
