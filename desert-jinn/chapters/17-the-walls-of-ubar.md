# Chapter Seventeen
## The Walls of Ubar

> *Behind every wall a lamp; behind every lamp a fear; beneath every street a knock. Do not answer it.*
> — a saying of the lamp-keepers of Ubar

---

Ubar isn't on any map.

Every child on this coast has heard of it. The city of tall towers that grew too proud and was swallowed by the sand in a single night. The old men tell it at the fire with their coffee going cold, as a story about pride.

They have it wrong in one particular. Ubar wasn't swallowed. It was *kept*. It stands where it always stood, deep in the red sands south of Liwa, where the dunes rise like mountains and nothing lives that doesn't have to. You don't find it. It lets you in. The dead find it easily enough: for as long as anyone remembers, the dead of the whole desert have been carried there on slow camels, wrapped in white, by guides who don't speak about the road.

We had a broken sword, a letter, a compass that pointed backwards, and a dog. None of us knew the way.

---

On the twelfth day south the wind began to carry a smell I didn't like. Lamp smoke, old and oily, ten thousand wicks burning at once, and under it something sweet and closed, like dates left too long in a shut jar.

I put my nose up and whined.

"You smell it," said Sharood. "Close is as far as I've ever got. You can smell Ubar. You never see it."

Then I smelled something else. Camels. Myrrh, the kind they pack the dead in. And a man.

He came over the next ridge on foot, leading three camels, and across each saddle lay a long white bundle. A young man, thin, sun-black, his face wrapped against the sand. He stopped when he saw us. He looked at Theyab a long time, and pulled the cloth down.

The left side of his face was paler than the right, a faint ashy patch from temple to jaw where the grey had licked him once and never quite let go. I knew that smell. I'd lain across his lap once, in the wreck of a caravan, after the zawba'a went through it.

"You," said Jasir.

Theyab didn't move.

"You gave me your water," Jasir said. "The last of it. I was dying in the sand and you put your hands on my face, and there was light, and then you put your waterskin in my mouth, and I called you—" His jaw worked. "I called you a monster. In front of everyone that was left."

"I remember," said Theyab.

"So do I. Every day." Jasir looked at the bundles. "Abu Kharbash is dead. Nobody hires a man from a caravan the whirlwind ate. Bad luck sticks. So now I guide the dead to Ubar. The dead don't care about luck." He looked at the broken sword. "Every coffee house from Buraimi to the coast says you killed the Sheikh's son. There's a thousand thalers on you."

"I didn't kill him."

I sat by Theyab's leg with my hackles as flat as a wet cat's. Jasir looked at me, and at the boy.

"Your water tasted of goat," he said at last, and touched the old waterskin hanging from his saddle, a knot of faded red wool still tied round its neck. "It was the best thing I ever drank." He turned his lead camel. "You can't use the gates. Come with the dead. The dead go in by the old way."

---

The old way was a *falaj*: one of the ancient water channels cut through the rock underground, with the men who dug it a thousand years dead. Ubar's runs under the sand from a spring nobody has seen, and the dead go in along it.

From the top of the last dune, before we went down, I saw the city.

It lay in a bowl in the sand as if a great hand had pressed it there. Walls of dark stone, eight towers taller than any fort on the coast, and every tower lit from foot to crown with lamps, so many that the smoke hung over the city like a bruise. The walls didn't reflect the last of the sun. They drank it. And all around, pressed against the edge of the lamplight, sat the grey. Patient as a dog outside a butcher's door. I know how that dog feels. I didn't like seeing it from the outside.

Sharood stood by the pale camel, not saying things. It ran in the family.

"I'll stay out here," he said. "The Nameless won't come through the gates like honest men. I'll see them first. And Theyab. Come back out to me. After. Swear it."

The boy was quiet a long time. He *couldn't* swear it. He'd started waking at night beating at his own chest, at flames that weren't there. If he'd sworn, the mark would have burned him like a coal.

"I'll try," he said.

My hackles lay flat. It was completely true. It was nothing like enough.

Sharood rode away along the hollow between the dunes, and the wind smoothed out his tracks behind him, the way it does for things the desert means to keep.

---

Jasir walked us in himself, down a ramp of worn stone into the dark, between posts carved so long ago the carving had worn to a suggestion: a hand, an eye, something that might have been a lamp.

The water was knee-deep on the men and considerably more on me. Salukis are made for running down gazelles across open sand in the sunlight. No saluki in the history of the world was made for wading through the dark under a city of the dead. I waded anyway, with my tail held up out of it on principle.

Theyab's mark glowed through his wet shirt and gave us just enough light. That was a pity, because then we could see the walls.

The falaj ran through the tombs.

The rock on either side was cut into shelves going up into the dark and on into the dark, and on every shelf lay a long white shape, wrapped and still. Above each, a name painted in red ochre. Up in the city, Jasir whispered, there were painters who went over the names day and night. Down here the names were left to do what names in Ubar do.

"Which is what?" said Theyab.

"Go," said Jasir.

Most were nearly gone. The ghost of a letter. A smear like a thumbprint.

And the tombs breathed.

Not a draught. The air went *in*, all of it, down the length of the tunnel, slow and steady, pulling at the feathering on my ears. A long pause. Then it came back out, warm, smelling of old linen and myrrh and something sweet underneath, like fruit gone soft. The water rose a finger's width against my chest on the in-breath and sank on the out. The camels' eyes rolled white. Jasir talked to them in the low steady voice camel men use, and they went on.

Theyab read some of the names. A boy who's only just learned to read reads everything.

"*Son of*," he whispered. "*Little daughter of*— *Beloved wife of*—" As he said *beloved*, the last red letters slid down the stone like a drop of water and were gone, and the name went with them before he could read it. "Did I do that?"

"No," said Jasir. "They do that by themselves."

I sniffed where the name had been. It smelled of nothing at all. The jackal-smell. Something on the other side of the stone had drunk it.

Where the channel bent there was an alcove with eleven shelves in it. Small ones. Child-length. Empty. Above them no names, only eleven painted feathers, gone the colour of dried blood.

Theyab stood in the water a long time.

"Ghareeb," he said. "Who paints the names of the bearers?"

I couldn't have answered. I'll tell you, though, because you'll need it. Nobody does. Nobody ever has. The desert remembers the *bearers*. Not the children.

Then, from under us, under the water and the stone, came a knock.

Three knocks. Slow. Patient. The way you'd knock on the door of someone you knew was home.

The tombs drew in a long breath and held it.

The knocking didn't come again. After a while the air went out, warm and sweet, and Jasir walked on much faster, and Theyab didn't read any more names.

---

Where the falaj split in three, Jasir stopped.

"I take the dead up the left way, to the washing house," he whispered. "They search everyone who comes out of the ground at the top. I don't know any other way. I'm sorry. I'll go up slow. I'll talk a lot."

He touched Theyab's arm, once, where the golden cracks were, and pulled his hand back fast. Then he led his dead up into the dark, and we were alone with two black tunnels and the breathing.

That was when I smelled the boy.

He was sitting up on a tomb shelf among the wrapped dead, so still I'd taken him for one of them. Fourteen or so, in what had been a stable lad's tunic. Thin as a lamp-wick. His skin was the grey of the emptied, but not all of it: patches of brown were left on his hands and one cheek, like the last colour on a cloth left out in the sun. His eyes were white, except for a thin ring of dark round the edge of each, like the rim of a well.

Under the grave-smell he smelled of camels and straw. He'd been down here a long time and the stable hadn't quite washed off him.

"Prince Hazza' says hide," he said, in a flat, pleasant voice. "Prince Hazza' says hide."

Theyab went white under the gold.

"Suhail," he said.

Hazza' had told him the story at Bir al-Ghina'. The stable boy the grey had taken. The boy he'd hidden in the fodder loft over the stables and tried to cure with honey and talking and stubbornness. The guards. The sword that broke defending him. And Wazir Muhaisen, after, smoothing his robes: *The boy has been taken away. For everyone's safety.*

Taken away. Down. Into the tombs, alive, where nobody would go looking. Where the knocking was.

Theyab climbed onto the shelf and put his hands on the boy's face. I told him with my teeth that it was a poor idea. He didn't listen.

The Light came, gold, and the grey didn't go. It flinched back a finger's width from the boy's mouth and stopped there. Too much of him was eaten, too long ago.

Theyab came back gasping, and I knew from his face what he'd seen: a stable full of hay and sunlight, a laughing young man with dark eyes pushing a frightened boy behind the feed sacks, *Here, quickly, hide, I won't let them.* And his own death, a breath longer than last time.

But the dark rings in Suhail's eyes were wider.

He looked at the hilt over Theyab's shoulder.

"He hid me," he said. Not flat. A boy's voice, rusty, surprised. "He hid me."

Then the grey came back over his mouth, and he climbed down into the water and walked away down the right-hand tunnel. We followed. What else was there?

He took us by ways no one living had used in a hundred years. Through a gap behind a fallen tomb-door. Along a ledge above black water.

That ledge is the part I still dream about.

The tunnel opened without warning into a cavern so wide Theyab's light couldn't find the far side of it. Pale rock, limestone, all holes and folds, like the inside of a bone. High overhead, in the middle of the roof, hung a disc of dim orange light the width of a threshing floor: the bottom of Ubar's great well, where the city let down its buckets, with lamps burning round its rim far above and their smoke leaning in. Ropes hung down through that light, swaying. Some had buckets on them. Some had only frayed ends.

Under it the water lay black and still, and so deep the light gave up on it.

Half of one of the old towers was down there. Long ago, before the oldest lamp-keeper's grandmother, the ground under the city had given way, and a tower of the wall had slid down into the cavern with everyone in it. Its stones lay heaped in the black water like the bones of something that had been eaten, carved faces and lamp niches and all, with the water lapping in their mouths.

And on a shelf of pale rock at the edge of the heap, just above the waterline, lay the vessel.

You've heard me tell of it. At Ghidd al-Zujaj, under the carvings, I told you how it was, or how it was not. Here it was.

Brass, gone green and black. As big as the great water jars the women carry to the falaj, round-bellied, long-necked, stamped all over with old marks worn almost smooth, so that the light from far above ran over them like water over fish-scales. It had split open from neck to belly, the brass peeled back in curling petals the way a pomegranate splits when it's left too long on the tree. Not from outside. From in.

Beside it, where it had rolled and come to rest, lay the stopper. The seal was still on it, cracked straight across.

I knew whose hands had cracked it. I'd known that boy's whistle, once, across a wadi at dusk.

Theyab stopped on the ledge. He didn't know what he was looking at. He didn't need to. His mark had gone dim and cold under his wet shirt, the way a coal goes when you drop it on damp ground.

"Ghareeb," he whispered. "What was in that?"

The water below the ledge moved. Not a ripple. The whole black face of it lifted a finger's width, the way the tombs drew breath, and settled.

And out of the split brass, faint as a cup set down in another room, something spoke, in no voice at all.

*Whoever frees me—*

Suhail began to hum the two notes of the Nameless under his breath, without seeming to know he was doing it, and walked on along the ledge. I pushed Theyab after him with my whole head. I didn't let him look back. I didn't look back myself.

I didn't need to. I've heard that sentence finished before, more ways than one. None of the endings are good. I'll tell you them when the lamps go out.

Up a channel so narrow Theyab went sideways and I went on my belly, until the air was warm and smelled, impossibly, of bread.

At the end were slippery steps and a wooden hatch.

Suhail stopped at the bottom. For one moment the dark rings were very wide.

"He used to go up there," he said. "For the sweet things. He always brought me one."

Then the white came back, and he turned down into the dark saying *hide, hide*, and the tombs breathed his voice in.

"I'll come back for you," Theyab said into the dark.

The bell on his belt gave the faintest shiver. He heard it. He didn't say it again.

He pushed the hatch up, and through the gap came bread, onions, cardamom, saffron and hot date syrup. He climbed out into a storeroom of rice sacks and hanging dried lemons, and found himself face to face with a cleaver.

---

The woman holding it was the largest person I had seen in some centuries.

Nearly as tall as Sharood and twice as wide, in a black abaya dusted with flour, the gold burqa of the old coast women across her nose and brows, and forearms like a well-digger's. She held the cleaver at the height of a head coming out of the hatch, the way you would if you'd been waiting.

"Back down," she said. Her voice could have stripped the gilt off a coffee pot. "I heard the knocking. You've a count of three to show me you're breathing on your own—"

Then she saw the hilt of the sword. The cleaver dropped a hand's width.

"Where," she said, in a completely different voice, "did you get that?"

Theyab stood dripping on her clean floor and told her the truth, because it was the only thing he had ever known how to do.

"It was Hazza's. He was my friend. I didn't kill him."

I sat down on a sack of rice and let her look at me. Every hair flat. Not a sound.

"He growls when someone lies," said Theyab, a little apologetically.

"Then he'd growl himself hoarse in this fort," she said. "And he's wet on my rice." Then her hand went up under the burqa to her mouth, and her eyes went wide, and then wet. The cleaver rang on the stone.

"I knew it," she whispered. "I *knew* it. I told them, and nobody listens to the cook."

She was Umm Shamma. She'd run the fort's kitchens for thirty years and half-raised the Sheikh's two sons, because the Sheikh was busy being a sheikh and somebody had to make sure the boys ate. She sat Theyab on a crate and put rice and lentils and a plate of her honey cakes in front of him, little fried rounds of dough shining with sidr honey. She put a bowl of goat's milk on the floor for me. I want that recorded.

"That boy," she said, pointing a floury finger at the hilt as if Hazza' were inside it, "stole honey cakes from my kitchen for ten years. Came up out of that hatch smelling of the dead every time, and thought I didn't know. Half of them went out to the stables, to that poor stable boy. I knew that too." Her chin trembled. "A boy like that doesn't kill anyone. And neither do you. Eat."

Theyab ate slowly, the way he'd eaten Umm Hanan's bundles, as if somebody might take it away.

"Suhail's still down there," he said. "He said Hazza' always brought him one."

Umm Shamma turned to stir a pot that didn't need stirring, and her big shoulders shook. When she turned back she was already tying up a cloth of bread and dates, and she set it by the hatch.

"I'll go down tonight," she said. "I'm not frightened of the dead. It's the living in this fort I'm frightened of."

My hackles stayed flat.

---

She hid us behind the oil jars in the back storeroom, great clay things taller than Theyab.

"Don't knock them. That's three months' light for this wing. People here have been killed for less oil than you could carry in your mouth."

At the inner gates, she said, the oil-sellers measured it out with brass spoons, a thumb at a time, to silent grey-faced queues. A thaler for a cupful. In Ubar they don't swear by their fathers' graves. They swear by their oil. Because if the last lamp in the city ever goes out, the dark outside the walls will walk in, and what knocks under the streets will come up.

The Sheikh did not eat, she told us, as if that were the worst of it. Since Hazza's death the only man he listened to was his wazir.

"Muhaisen," said Theyab. She spat into the cooking fire.

"Grey eyes. Soft voice. He's brought the Sheikh Hazza's ring. Said he took it from the Prince-Killer's own hand. Proof, he called it." She looked at Theyab sideways. "The Sheikh wears it on a chain now. Over his heart. And they painted Hazza's name on the wall of the fort court, under the traitors, and the painters aren't allowed to touch it up. So every day there's less of him. And the little one, Zaid, sits in front of it every sunset with a pot of ochre he stole from the painters and goes over the letters with his finger."

Then she asked what I'd been dreading.

"How did he die? Our Hazza'. Did it hurt him?"

Theyab didn't know, and it had been cutting him every night for a month.

"I wasn't there," he said. "I should have been."

My hackles lay flat. It was true. I looked at the floor.

Umm Shamma looked at me. She'd spent thirty years knowing which kitchen boy had broken a jar by the set of his shoulders. She looked at the set of mine. She didn't ask. You can't ask a dog.

I was grateful. I was ashamed of being grateful.

I'll tell you about that night soon, little one. Let me be a coward a little longer.

---

Zaid came down after midnight, bare feet on the back stairs, lifting the lid off the honey-cake jar exactly the way someone else had taught him.

Theyab stood up behind the jars. His mark glowed.

The boy froze. He was ten, small for it, with his brother's dark eyes and none of his swagger, and red ochre dried under every fingernail.

"That's Hazza's," he whispered.

"Yes."

"They said you killed him."

"I didn't."

I padded out and stood in front of him, every hair flat, and put my nose into his ochre-stained palm. He didn't pull it away.

"He knows when people lie," said Theyab. "Hazza' said he was the rudest dog in the Empty Quarter. He said he once ate a scorpion to impress him and was sick for a whole day, and Ghareeb watched and wagged."

That was generous about the wagging. I had thumped my tail twice, that was all. The mark didn't burn, so I suppose it counts. I let it go. Zaid made a small cracked sound, and it took me a moment to understand it was a laugh.

Then he sat down on the floor and cried, and Theyab sat beside him, shoulders touching, the way you share warmth at a fire, and I lay down across both their feet.

"His name's going," Zaid said into his knees. "I painted it three times today and by evening the last letter was gone again. Is that what happens? If nobody remembers you hard enough?"

Under the floor, very faintly, something knocked three times. The boy didn't even flinch. He'd grown up with it.

Theyab took out the letter. Zaid read it by the light of the mark, and I watched his face change halfway down.

"The Night of Lamps is tomorrow," he said. "The moon will be full. My father sits in the majlis in the morning. Anyone can come. I could take you in."

---

The majlis at the heart of the fort had no windows. It was lit only by lamps, hundreds of them, hanging on long chains from palm-trunk beams so high and black with soot that the lamps looked like stars in a sky with no moon. Every sound went up into the shadows and came back softer and slightly wrong, a word you hadn't said, a cough a beat late, as though the dark up there were listening and practising.

Along the walls the men of Ubar sat on cushions with their coffee cups, each with a little lamp of his own burning on the carpet beside him. At the far end, on a black stone bench spread with a camel-hair rug, sat the saddest man I have ever seen.

Sheikh Mutlaq had been tall and broad once. You could see the shape of it, the way you see the shape of a house after the roof has gone. A heavy gold ring hung on a thin chain at his neck, and every few breaths his hand drifted up to touch it.

One step below him sat Wazir Muhaisen. Silver beard, smooth face, robes the colour of sand, rosewater. His faded grey eyes found Theyab the instant the boy stepped out from behind Zaid.

He smiled. A small smile. The smile of a man who sets a trap and comes back in the morning to find the fox in it, exactly where he'd pictured it.

"The Prince-Killer!" someone shouted, and then everyone was shouting. Rifles came up, old Martinis and a matchlock older than the fort. A merchant shrieked *Monster!* and I saw the gold on Theyab's skin flicker grey.

I stood in front of him with my lips back. A saluki with his teeth out is not a small thing, even an old one.

"*Stop!*" Zaid had planted himself in front of us both with his arms out, shaking. "Father, he's come to your majlis. He's under your roof. You have to *listen*."

The Sheikh looked at his son a long time. Then he lifted one tired hand, and the rifles went down.

Theyab read the letter aloud. Every word. His voice didn't shake. Hazza' had made him practise until it didn't.

When he finished, I could hear the lamps hissing, and far under the floor, the slow breathing of the tombs.

"That is Wazir Muhaisen's seal," said Theyab. "Hazza' took this from one of the Nameless and hid it in his sword. They killed him before he could bring it home."

And Muhaisen sighed, gently, like a teacher whose favourite pupil has made a small mistake.

"My Sheikh will remember I reported my seal stolen on the night of the young sheikh's exile, and had a new one cut in Muscat. Every man in this majlis heard me." He spread his soft hands. "A forgery in a broken sword, carried by the boy who killed him, on the eve of the Night of Lamps. It is clever. And my Sheikh already wears the proof of what this boy took from Hazza's body."

Long before Hazza' found that letter, Muhaisen had made sure that if it was ever found, it would look like a lie.

My hackles went up from my neck to my tail. I growled, low and long, the way I'd growl at a viper in a tent.

"Did you kill Hazza'?" said Theyab. It came out of him the way the Light did, without permission.

Muhaisen looked down at him, soft and sad. "I loved that boy," he said, "as if he were my own son."

From Theyab's belt, Old Bakhit's little brass truth bell rang.

In that hall it sounded like a stone dropped down a well. And up in the dark above the lamps the hall rang it back, faint and wrong, a dozen tiny bells that weren't there. My growl went on under it.

"A Bedouin trinket," said Muhaisen, without looking. "Bells ring when boys shake them, my Sheikh. And dogs growl at strangers."

"He didn't shake it," said Zaid.

"I didn't kill your son," Theyab said to the Sheikh, very low. "I left him alone that night. We argued, and I walked away, and when I came back he was dead. That's the worst thing I've ever done. But I didn't kill him."

My growl stopped as if cut with a knife. Every hair on me lay down.

Some of the men along the walls had hunted with salukis all their lives. I saw them look at me, and understand, the way men understand their own dogs. I saw them look at the wazir.

The Sheikh wavered, like a dune shifting at night. Believing us meant believing he'd exiled his son for a lie and worn his ring over his heart because the man who killed him had handed it over. No father can look at that straight on. Not all at once.

"Hold the boy," Sheikh Mutlaq said at last. "In the east tower. He is under my roof; no one touches him. After the Night of Lamps I will hear this again. From both of you." He looked at Muhaisen. "Both."

"Of course, my Sheikh," said Muhaisen. "Shall we cancel the Night of Lamps? For safety?"

The Sheikh closed his eyes. "No. The people need their lamps."

As the guards took Theyab's arms, I saw the wazir's face when he thought no one was looking.

He was still smiling.

---

The tower room was small and round, with one window facing east. They took the sword and the bell. Nobody had worked out how you arrest a saluki. I lay down across the doorway, and the guard looked at me and decided to leave it at that.

The sun went down. All over the city, bolts slid home one after another like a great slow clock. Then the whispering began from every rooftop: the people of Ubar reading the names of their dead off the walls before the paint could fade further. *Son of. Daughter of. Beloved.* And under it, all across the city, so faint you'd think it was your own heart, the knocking. Three. Pause. Three. From under every street at once.

"They're answering," Theyab said at the window. "Something down there's answering the names."

My hackles didn't move. He saw them not move.

A soft sound at the door. Not a guard. Guards stamp.

Muhaisen's face appeared at the little barred window in the wood. In the dark his eyes looked almost white, like the emptied.

"Thank you," he said gently, "for coming. I've wondered for a month how to bring you inside these walls on the Night of Lamps, under the full moon. And you came by yourself, up through the dead, with a letter in your hand." He sounded almost fond. "The desert really does write in the sand. It wants you here as much as I do. Ask yourself why, bearer."

Then, softer still: "Tomorrow night, the lamps go out."

I lay with my nose a hand's width from his slippers and every hair on my body flat. Not a growl. Theyab looked down at me, and I saw him understand. Everything Muhaisen had said was true. That was what made it so terrible.

The slippers went away down the stair.

Theyab went to the window, and I put my forepaws on the sill beside him. Below, the city lay under its smoke, ten thousand small flames holding back the dark, each one bought a thumb at a time. And on the roof of the tower opposite, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure in a grey robe crouched with two grey blades across his knees. His mask was a smooth white face with a crack running down the middle, brow to chin.

He was looking up at our window.

And very faintly, under the whispering of the names and the knocking beneath them, so faint I might have been the only creature in the world with ears good enough to hear it, he was humming.

---

*End of Chapter Seventeen*
