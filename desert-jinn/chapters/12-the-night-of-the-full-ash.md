# Chapter Twelve
## The Night of the Full Moon

> *Under the badr, every promise is weighed. And what is found wanting is taken.*
> — an old saying of the oasis people, spoken over water at weddings and at graves

---

I made no sound at all before the full moon. Not a whine, not a growl, not a sigh through the nose.

I limped behind the two of them with my coat scorched brown down my left side and my silver eye swollen shut. The burn wept a thin black fluid that smoked when it touched the sand. I didn't eat much. At night I slept away from the fire, and every night Theyab looked over at me before he lay down, and every night I turned my face away.

He tried to say sorry. Once he left me the best part of a roasted sandgrouse and sat with his back to me so I could eat unwatched. I ate it after he'd fallen asleep. I want that on the record.

I wasn't angry with him. You need to understand that, because he didn't. I wasn't angry at all.

I was ashamed. He had seen what stands behind me. He had heard the voices that live in me. And there is no word for how that feels, in any language I've learned, except that it's the opposite of being remembered.

---

Hazza' was different too, after the storm.

He still joked, still complained about his feet and the general unfairness of sand. He still drew letters every evening, and Theyab could read whole short lines now, right to left, his finger following the way Hazza' had shown him. One night Hazza' wrote three words in big letters beside the fire, *Theyab and Hazza': monsters*, and made him read them aloud, and they both laughed, and for a moment the dark beyond the firelight felt a long way off.

Remember those words. I'm sorry. I have to ask you to remember them.

But Hazza' had started touching the pommel of his sword, resting his thumb on the brass cap the way Theyab touched his feather mark. And when he thought no one was looking, he'd stand on a dune and stare west toward Ubar, the way you stare at a house where you know someone is waiting for you in the dark.

"We're changing direction," he announced on the fourth morning. "West. I need to go home."

Theyab stared at him. "You were exiled."

"I know. I was there."

"They'll kill you."

"They'll *try*." Hazza's grin came and went too fast, like a lamp swinging past a window. "I need to see Zaid. I need to make sure he's all right."

My hackles stayed flat. That was true. Every word.

But Theyab had learned to watch the spaces between words, too. He looked at Hazza' a long moment. He knew there was something more. And because Theyab had never in his life asked anyone for something they didn't want to give, he didn't ask.

I knew what it was. I'd watched it go into the hilt. I couldn't have told him if I'd wanted to, and I didn't try.

---

Every night, the moon got fuller.

Theyab stopped looking at it. Then he started looking at it all the time. He'd lie on his back by the fire with his hand on his chest, staring up at that white face as it swelled a little more each evening, and I knew what he was seeing. The dune. The fire. The sound of somebody shouting a name at the bottom of the world.

He'd started waking in the night and patting his arms and hair the way you pat out sparks. Once I saw him hold his hand an inch above the coals and keep it there, his face blank, as if he were trying to learn the feeling in advance. Hazza' knocked his wrist away and said nothing, and Theyab said nothing, and they didn't speak of it.

He knew Tal Moreeb was nowhere near. But every full moon would bring it one month closer, and this was the first one since he'd read the word *ash* at the end of eight lines on a carved rock.

On the twenty-ninth night, it rose full.

It came up out of the eastern dunes huge and white and close, exactly as in his vision. The desert went silver, and nothing moved in it, not a jerboa, not a breath of wind. The desert was holding still the way a hare does when the falcon's shadow passes over.

It was the brightest night Theyab had ever seen, and he sat by the fire and couldn't stop shaking.

One month. Thin moon to badr, and the dark between. That's how long they'd had.

I knew it. I'd counted. Neither of them knew.

---

It started, as the worst things do, with someone trying to help.

"Come to Ubar with me," Hazza' said.

He'd been quiet a long time, sitting across the fire turning his signet ring round and round on his finger. Now he leaned forward, and his face in the firelight was serious in a way Theyab had hardly ever seen it.

"I mean it. Under the city there are the tombs, and down among the tombs there's the old library. Every book anyone in Ubar ever wrote. Every story. Every name. Hundreds of years of them, Theyab, rooms and rooms, all the way down into the dark. If there's a third way written anywhere in this desert, it's there. You can read now. Not fast. But we could look together."

"Hazza'—"

"I can get you in. I know a way through the kitchens. Umm Shamma would hide an army if I asked her nicely and brought back the honey jars I stole."

"I can't go to Ubar."

"Why not?"

"Because it's not *there*." Theyab's voice cracked. "It's not in a book. You read them. On the rock. You read them out loud to me. Eight of them burned, and three of them ran, and the ones who ran—" He pointed east, at nothing, at the empty silver desert. "Look what happened. One of them *is* the Ifrit's now. Its mouth. That's the third way, Hazza'. That's the only other way there is. You become the thing in the storm."

"You don't *know* that."

"I've *felt* it! Every time I use the Light I'm standing on that dune and I can feel the fire coming up my legs, I can *smell* it, I wake up and I can still smell it—" He stopped. He pressed his hand flat over his chest. "It isn't a story anymore. It's going to happen. It's *written*."

"Then we unwrite it."

"You can't unwrite the *sand*!"

The words went out across the silver dunes and the desert swallowed them whole and gave nothing back.

Hazza' looked away first. He looked down at his hands. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet.

"Promise me something," he said.

Theyab didn't answer.

"Promise me you'll never give up." Hazza' looked up. His eyes were very bright. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see in your visions. Whatever the rocks say. You keep going. You don't just *lie down* and let it take you. Promise me."

Theyab looked at him across the fire, under the full moon.

He could not lie. You know that. So when he said it, he said it knowing that the feather on his chest would have burned like a coal if he hadn't meant it with everything he had.

"I promise," said Theyab.

It didn't burn.

And I, lying in the dark with my face turned away, felt the hair along my back lie down flat and smooth as a stroked cat's. I lifted my head. I couldn't help it. A true promise does that to me, the way a lie does the opposite.

And somewhere out in the silver dark, a long way off, something answered. Not a jackal. Not a voice. A single soft sound, like a heavy book being closed.

Neither of them heard it. I did. I've never known what it was. I've been afraid for a very long time that I do.

---

If they'd stopped there, this would be a different story.

They didn't stop there.

"Good," said Hazza'. He let out a long breath, almost a laugh. "Good. Then come to Ubar."

"That's not—"

"You just promised."

"I promised not to give up. Going to the dune isn't giving up." Theyab's voice was shaking. "Don't you see? It's the *opposite*. The ones who gave up are the ones who ran. If I keep going, Hazza', *that's* where I'm going. That's what not giving up *is*, for me. It's walking into the fire with my eyes open."

Hazza' stood up so fast he nearly kicked over the coffee pot.

"No," he said. "No. I'm not doing this again. I'm not standing by while someone I—" He bit it off. "I'm not letting them take you. I'll drag you to Ubar by your ankles. I'll lock you in the tombs. I'll find the third way myself and read it to you through the door. I am *not* losing anybody else."

"You can't save me."

"Watch me."

And then Theyab said it.

He didn't plan it. I know that, because I know him. He was frightened, so frightened, and tired past the point where tiredness has a bottom, and the moon hung full and white above him like an eye that wouldn't close. Hazza' was standing over him promising things nobody could promise. And it hurt so much to be loved like that, when he knew exactly how it ended, that something in him reached for the sharpest true thing it could find, and threw it.

"You can't save me," he said. "You couldn't even save Suhail."

---

The fire cracked.

Hazza' didn't move. He didn't shout. His face didn't even change, at first. He stood there with the full moon behind him, looking down at Theyab, and something went out of his eyes. It was like watching a lamp go out on a high wall in Ubar, one of the ones that must never all go out, and then the next one along, and the next.

My hackles didn't stir. I wish they had. But it was true, every word, and nothing in the world cuts like the truth said at the wrong moment for the wrong reason.

Theyab saw his face. He saw what he'd done.

He got up. He didn't say sorry. He couldn't make his mouth do it. He turned round and walked away from the fire, out into the silver dark.

"Theyab," said Hazza' behind him.

Just his name. Once. Not loud.

Theyab didn't turn round.

I've thought about that moment more than any other in this story. More than the dune, more than the fire. A boy walking away across moonlit sand, and another boy standing alone by a small fire, saying a name once into the wind.

You know the old saying. *Never say your name to the wind.*

Nobody ever told Hazza' what happens when you say someone else's.

Theyab meant to come back. You should know that. He meant to walk until he'd stopped shaking, then come back and make it right. There'd be time in the morning. That's the oldest lie there is, and the feather never burns for it, because nobody knows it's a lie until afterwards.

He walked away and left Hazza' alone.

---

And where was I?

I'll tell you the first part.

I'd smelled jinn-jackals that evening, before the argument started. A long way off, downwind, but coming, drawn by the Light still glowing in the cracks on Theyab's arms. I'd been lying a little way from the fire with my scorched side to the cold sand, my nose working. When Theyab walked off into the dark, I got up and went the other way.

I went hunting.

A saluki runs down what runs. I let the jinn half out. It hurt worse than it ever had, the burned side tearing open as the shadow came up out of it, and the voices inside were louder that night, as if the full moon had pulled them up near the surface the way it pulls the tide over the coastal flats. But there were jackals in the dunes and the boy I loved was alone out there, and that is the only use I've ever found for the thing I carry.

I hunted them across the silver sand under the full moon, for most of the night. My shadow ran ahead of me, longer than the dunes.

That's all I'm going to tell you about where I was. Don't ask me any more. Not yet.

I said, *don't ask*.

---

Theyab didn't go far. He climbed a high dune a long way off and sat at the top, knees pulled up to his chest, the moon pouring white light down on him until he cast almost no shadow at all. He could still see the fire, very small, a single orange point down in the hollow where he'd left it.

He said sorry to it. Out loud, over and over, practising.

*I'm sorry. I didn't mean it. You were right to try. You were right to try to save him. You were right to try to save me.*

Some time after the middle of the night, he looked down, and the little orange point had gone out.

He told me later he'd thought nothing of it. Fires burn down. He said it twice, and the second time his mark burned, faintly, and he had to start over. *I told myself it was nothing.* That one was true.

Near dawn, with the moon sinking into the western dunes and the sky going the grey of old bone, he fell asleep sitting up.

He didn't dream. No visions, no warnings. He felt nothing at all. Not a whisper in the sand. Not a single bell.

The sky was very quiet that night. I've never forgiven it for that. It's a long list, the things I haven't forgiven the sky for. Most of them are quiet nights.

---

He woke when the sun touched his face. He scrambled up and ran down the dune, sliding and stumbling, the words ready in his mouth. *I'm sorry. You were right to try.*

He came over the last rise and stopped.

There were camels in the hollow. Five of them, kneeling and grumbling, with slabs of grey salt from the coastal sabkha roped to their sides. And there were men, five travellers in dusty white thobes, standing in a ring around the cold black circle of the fire.

Five grown Bedouin and not one of them talking. One had his hand over his mouth.

They were looking down at something on the sand.

Theyab walked toward them. He didn't feel his legs moving. The morning was very bright and very still, and the only sound was a saker falcon on one man's wrist, screaming and screaming under its hood, the way animals scream at something they can smell and can't see.

Hazza' lay on his back beside the dead fire, one arm flung out, his broken sword in the sand by his open hand.

He was grey.

Not the grey of the emptied. Something worse. His skin had gone the colour of old ash, every part of it, his lips, his eyelids, the beds of his nails. And running all through the grey, across his face and down his throat and along both arms, were fine bright lines of *gold*. Thin glowing cracks, like the glaze on a broken coffee cup. They were still glowing. Faintly. Fading even as Theyab watched, one by one, the way the last sparks go in a fire nobody is feeding.

His eyes were open, looking up at the morning sky. They had gone pale. Not white. Pale, like a window someone has breathed on.

His mouth was a little open too, as though he'd been about to say something. As though someone had come up to the fire in the dark, and he'd looked up, and started to say a name.

The sand around him was smooth. Perfectly smooth, the way the wind leaves it, for ten paces in every direction. No struggle. No tracks. Not even a beetle's. As if the desert had tidied up after itself.

And beside the fire, untouched, not a grain out of place, the words he'd written in the sand that evening, before the argument, and never rubbed out. Theyab hadn't noticed they were still there. He noticed now.

*Theyab and Hazza': monsters.*

Except the first word had been smoothed away. Just that one. The rest were perfect.

---

One of the travellers saw Theyab first.

An older man with a beard gone yellow at the edges. He backed away a step, then another.

They looked at Theyab. They looked at his arms, at the golden cracks still glowing faintly from wrist to shoulder.

"That's him," said the young man with the falcon. His voice was high and thin. "The sheikh's son. The exiled one. The son of Mutlaq of Ubar. Look at the jaw, the scar. They said at Buraimi he was out here selling himself for a sword—"

"And *that*," said the old man, staring at Theyab, "is the glowing boy."

"From the caravan," the old man said. "They were talking about him at the last well. The one the sand-devil didn't eat. A man walked out of the sand alive and he's telling everyone from here to the coast." He was backing toward his camel. "*Monster*, he said. And here he is."

"I didn't," said Theyab.

"I didn't do this."

The feather mark didn't burn. He felt it not burn. It was true, every word. And it didn't matter at all, because these men had never heard of a boy who couldn't lie, and if they had, they wouldn't have believed it.

There were other true things, too. They came up in him one after another, like water rising in a well that's about to drown you.

*I left him.*

*I said the worst thing I could think of, and I left him alone in the dark, and I watched his fire go out and I told myself it was nothing.*

"I left him," said Theyab. "I walked away. I left him alone."

The feather didn't burn for that either.

The travellers stared at him. And then one of them, I never learned which, said it: the word that would follow Theyab across the whole desert, carried on falcons' legs and in traders' mouths and around every coffee hearth from Liwa to the Hajar, faster than any racing camel could run.

"*Prince-Killer.*"

They ran.

They had the camels up and away in moments, salt slabs swinging, the falcon still screaming. East, toward the oases. Toward everyone.

*Prince-Killer. Prince-Killer. The glowing boy killed the sheikh's son.*

---

Theyab knelt in the sand beside Hazza'.

He didn't touch him at first. He knelt there with his hands open on his knees and the gold fading in Hazza's face, crack by crack, until there were only a few lines left glowing at the corners of his eyes, like the last of something trying to get out.

Then, very slowly, he reached out to close Hazza's eyes.

They didn't want to close. The lids were stiff and cold as wax and slid back open, a little, both times. The third time he held his fingers there and counted, the way you count through a pain, and when he let go they stayed. Mostly. A thin pale line of eye still showed beneath each lid, watching nothing.

He wanted to heal him. Of course he did. His hands were already burning. He laid them on Hazza's chest and called the Light, and it came, gold and desperate, more gold than it had ever come for anyone. It poured into Hazza' and went looking.

It found nothing. Not a dark place, not a hollow like one of the emptied, where something had been scooped out. *Nothing.* As if there had never been anyone in there. The Light went down and down and touched no bottom, and came back to him cold.

He'd never seen it fail before.

And then, because it always did now, the vision came. Not of Hazza'. There was no past or future in Hazza' to see. Only his own: the full moon, the dune, the fire climbing. But this time, for one breath, he wasn't alone in it. Somebody was standing on the sand below him, just at the edge of the light, very still, looking up. He couldn't see the face.

Then it was gone, and he was kneeling in the morning with his hands on a cold chest.

He took Hazza's hand. It was cold. The fingers were grey and cracked with gold, and on the right hand, on the finger where the heavy signet ring had always been, there was nothing. Only a band of paler skin where it had sat for years. The ring he never took off.

He looked at his own arms, at the cracks. Then at the cracks on Hazza's face. The same gold. He'd been asleep. He'd been far away. He'd felt nothing. But the Light had burst out of him once already when he didn't mean it to, and nearly burned a dog out of the world.

He thought of the little orange point going out.

*Did I do this?*

He would carry that question for a long time. I'm sorry to tell you that. I could have taken it from him, if I'd had a tongue for words, or the courage to find another way. I'm sorry to tell you that, too.

---

The broken sword was lying in the sand by Hazza's open hand.

Theyab picked it up.

It was lighter than he'd expected. Half a blade, dark leather on the grip, a brass pommel that didn't quite match. It was cold. Everything in that hollow was cold, though the sun was well up by then. He held it across his knees the way Hazza' used to hold it by the fire at night.

He didn't know what was inside it. Not yet.

The desert people bury their dead the same day, before the sun is high. Everyone knows that. Even a scavenger boy nobody ever invited to a funeral knows that. So when he could make his arms work, Theyab dug. With his hands, and with the broken sword, a shallow scrape in the hollow beside the fire, the way the poorest are buried, no shroud but the cloak Hazza' already wore. It wasn't deep enough. It couldn't be. The sand kept running back in.

He laid Hazza' in it and pushed the sand over him with his forearms. Then he sat beside it with the sword in his lap while the sun climbed and the shadows of the dunes went short.

Then the wind came, and began, gently, the way it does, to finish the work. A little over the place where the face was. A little more. A ridge of fine sand drifting across the hollow, smoothing it, until you couldn't have said where the grave began.

Theyab watched it happen and didn't stop it. He didn't cry. Not then. That came later.

He just sat, and held on.

---

I was already on the dune above the hollow when he came over the rise that morning. I'd been there before the travellers came.

Don't ask me how long.

I lay at the top of the slope, a long way off, with my scorched coat and my one good eye, and I didn't go any closer. My hackles lay flat. My tail lay in the sand behind me. Not a growl in me. Not a sound. Nobody down there was saying anything that needed me.

Theyab didn't look up. I'm not sure he knew I was there.

I could have gone down to him and let him hold me instead of a cold broken sword. I could have done what every hunter has seen a good saluki do: walked, and kept looking back over my shoulder until he followed. I could have shown him a track. One track. With a face at the end of it.

I didn't do anything.

I'm very good at that. I've told you before. It's becoming a theme.

It's the one I'm least proud of. It's the one that still wakes me, when I sleep, which isn't often. In the dream I'm always on that dune, and the fire is always going out, and I am always, always a moment too far away.

That's all. That's all you get. Turn the page.

---

*End of Chapter Twelve*
