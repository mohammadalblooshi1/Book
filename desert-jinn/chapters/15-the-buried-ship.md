# Chapter Fifteen
## The Buried Ship

> *A ship in the desert is a coffin waiting for the sea. What does it hold while it waits?*
> — a riddle the caravan children tell, which has no answer, and which they are told not to tell after dark

---

I'm going to tell you this part the way he told it to me.

He told it once, in the caves on Jebel Hafeet, by a fire that kept going out, in a flat, quiet voice I never heard him use again. He looked at his hands the whole time. I spent most of this part under a dead ship with sand in my mouth.

---

It took me a night and a day to find it.

I followed the camels. And under the camel-smell, the cold, empty smell of ash-blades. And another, the closer I got: burning cloth, and hair, and something sweet and scorched beneath them, like honey left too long on the coals.

I carried Old Bakhit's bell in my teeth the whole way. A saluki has a soft mouth. I didn't let it ring once.

At dusk on the second day, under a moon thinned almost to nothing, a white rind, like the edge of an eye rolled back, I came over a long ridge and saw it.

A ship.

Below me lay a sabkha, a salt flat, dead and white and cracked into plates, stretching off toward a sea that hadn't been there in a thousand years. You find shells in the sand out there, a day's ride from any shore. And at the edge of that dead white flat, between two great dunes, half swallowed, lay a dhow.

A vast old hull, longer than all of Hilat al-Sarab, sunk on its side like a drowned animal, its tall stern rearing out of the sand, its ribs pale through the broken planks. The whole thing leaned toward the sabkha, as if it were still trying to crawl back to the sea.

And inside it, through every crack and gap, lamps burned.

I crept down the slope in the dark, round to the side away from the sabkha, where the sand had drifted up against the buried belly of the ship. I put the bell down in a hollow and covered it.

And I dug.

It's what my kind does. I dug down along the curve of the hull under the sand, where the wood was soft and rotten and the salt had eaten it like worms eat a date, and I didn't stop until I could put my ear to the planks and hear, very close, a boy breathing.

---

He woke up chained.

A great beam ran down the middle of the hold, and from an iron ring in it ran a grey chain to a cuff on each of his wrists. When the light in his chest tried to rise, the chain drank it, with a faint sound like a child sucking at a cut finger.

The planks were covered in writing.

Names. Hundreds of them, scratched into the old wood with nails and buckles and fingernails, as high as a man could reach. Every one had been scraped over afterwards until only a pale smear was left, like a word rubbed out of the sand. He couldn't read a single one.

In the far corner, tossed in a heap like rubbish, were his bag and Hazza's broken sword.

---

They brought him up on the first night, for the burning.

The upper deck lay tilted under the stars, one edge running down into the sand like a ramp. The Nameless stood in a circle on it. Fifty of them, perhaps more, grey robes, smooth white masks, silent as the emptied. In the middle stood an iron brazier, and in it a fire burned.

To one side, in a line, stood the children.

There were eleven. The youngest perhaps five, the oldest perhaps twelve. Little grey shifts. Shaved heads. They stood very straight and still, the way children stand when they've learned that moving gets you hurt.

They weren't crying. Not one. Not a sniff. Frightened children cry. These had been frightened so long that crying had been worn out of them, the way the stones of an old well-head get worn into the shape of hands.

Behind them, on a row of pegs along the rail, hung eleven small white masks. Waiting.

The Nameless brought Theyab into the circle and forced him to his knees.

Then three young men stepped forward. They wore grey, but no masks. Each held a strip of white cotton, and on each, in charcoal, a word was written.

A name.

The circle began to chant: the wordless hum from the storm, fifty throats rising and falling like one breath. Then it flattened into words, fifty voices in one:

"*Names are chains. Memory is pain. Forget, and be free.*"

The first young man held his strip of cloth over the brazier. He said the name on it, once, out loud. His own name. He said it the way you'd say goodbye to someone at the edge of the camp, before a long journey.

He let it fall.

The cloth caught and curled, and as it burned he tried to say his name again. Then his lips stopped halfway through the shape of it and stayed open, puzzled, like a man who has walked into a tent and forgotten why. The smell came off the brazier: scorched cotton, and that sweet burnt something underneath.

A brother stepped up behind each of them and pressed a white mask to his face, and Theyab saw its edges *settle*, softening against the skin like wax near a flame. One of the young men raised a hand and felt for the edge, and couldn't find one. He lowered his hand.

Then the children swept the ash into a sack. The smallest hummed the chant while she swept, perfectly.

Three men had walked into the circle. Three nobodies walked out.

Theyab told me it was the worst thing he ever saw. Worse than the choosing, he said, was how *relieved* they looked.

---

Then a figure stepped into the firelight, and the circle bowed.

Grey robe, white mask, like all the others. But when it spoke, Theyab knew the voice before the first word was done.

"Hello again, child."

He crouched in front of Theyab and held out a strip of white cotton and a stick of charcoal.

"Write it," he said.

Theyab looked at the cloth.

"You can now, can't you?" said the Voice softly. "He taught you. Write it, and give it to the fire, and all of this stops. The grief. The guilt. That walk under the full moon, every time you close your eyes. You saw their faces. Tell me they weren't at peace."

The charcoal hung in front of Theyab's face.

He thought of Hazza' drawing letters in the sand with a stick, right to left. *Dhal first. Like an old man's bent back, with a dot riding on it. Then ya, a little bowl with two dots underneath, like footprints. Alif, the tent pole. And ba, a boat with one dot under it, sinking. That's you. A sinking boat.* Laughing. Always laughing.

Theyab spat on the cloth.

The Voice looked down at the wet spot a moment. Then he folded the cloth carefully and tucked it into his robe, close to his heart, where he kept the ring.

"I must go back to Ubar," he said, rising. "The Night of Lamps is close." He turned to the circle. "Drain him slowly. Make him remember. When he is empty enough to forget his own name, he will give it to us himself. They always do."

---

They put him back on his chain in the hold, and the draining began.

They came in pairs, every few hours: one with a lamp, one with an ash-blade.

An ash-blade doesn't cut. It lies flat against you, cold as the bottom of a well, and pulls, and the Light comes out of you in thin gold threads and runs into the steel and goes grey.

And with each thread, a memory came.

Never a good one. The good ones they took. The bad ones they *played*. Theyab was six again, running with a stone in his back. Or kneeling with Shibl's hand in his while Nadim screamed *Monster!*

Or he was on the dune under the full moon.

Over and over: turning away, walking, watching the orange point of the fire go out. And each time, the memory changed a little. By the second day he couldn't tell which version was true. In the version he saw by then, he'd heard Hazza' scream.

He hadn't. I know that much. One day I'll tell you how.

I couldn't tell him. I lay under the hull an arm's length from him, hearing every sound he made and making none, digging a little further each night, waiting for the one moment that would work. That's what I told myself. True things can still be cowardly.

---

On the second night, they let the emptied in.

Dozens of them were penned in the lower hold, so close to my tunnel I could smell them through the wood. The Nameless *kept* them, the way a sheikh keeps hawks. And the emptied are drawn to the Light the way moths are drawn to a lamp, with the same ending.

They were all muttering their own last words, a soft dry babble like water in a falaj. *Mind the step. Mind the step.* *Is that you?* *Bring the goats in, the shamal's coming.* *Tell her I said—tell her I said—tell her—*

Then, all at once, they stopped.

Then they spoke.

Every mouth, together. One voice out of thirty throats, not a syllable apart. Not their voices. Not any voice they'd ever had.

"*Little light.*"

Theyab went rigid on his chain.

Ghadban. The shepherd boy who said no. And under him, behind him, wearing him the way the masks wore the faces underneath them, the thing that had been sealed beneath Ubar since before Ubar had a name.

"*Little nameless light,*" said the emptied. Thirty jaws moving as one. "*They threw stones at you. They called you monster. They drove you into the sand to die. And you'd burn for them? Why? No one remembers the bearers. Ask the dog. Ask him how many he's buried. Ask him if anyone sings their names at the coffee hearth.*"

"*Ask him why it's always children.*"

Theyab shut his eyes.

"*Ask him who it is that's fed,*" the voice went on, gentle, almost sorry, "*every time one of you burns. Ask him what's on the other side of the Light. He's seen it. He's been inside it. He knows.*"

Here is something I've never told anyone. Under the hull, I didn't growl. I lay there with my lips off my teeth, waiting for the growl, and it didn't come. Not for *ask him why it's always children*. Not for *ask him who is fed*.

"*Let it go,*" said thirty voices. "*Let me hold it for you. It's so heavy, little light. I know. I carried it too.*"

Then the voice went softer. Almost shy. All thirty faces turned down, toward the floor of the hold, toward the sand where I lay.

"*Hello, Ghareeb,*" it said. "*I know you're down there. You always did listen at the tent wall.*"

The burned place in my side said its three words louder. In his voice. It had always been his voice.

I won't tell you what that voice sounded like centuries ago, when it was a shepherd boy's in the Hajar, whistling me in from the wadi at dusk. That's mine. I'll only tell you that I didn't go to it, and that it was the hardest thing I have ever not done.

---

The children came at dawn, with water.

One of them stopped in front of Theyab and stared.

He was seven. His head was shaved, his shift too big. And his eyes were brown, clear brown, not faded at all. Not yet.

"You're the monster," said Shibl.

"Nadim said you weren't," Shibl whispered. "After. He said he was wrong." Something moved in his face, deep down, like a fish rising in murky water. His lip shook. Just once. "Is Nadim coming?"

Theyab opened his mouth. And closed it.

He wanted to say *yes. I promise.* But Theyab couldn't lie, and he didn't know.

"I'll try," he said. It was all he had. "I'll try. I swear I will."

Shibl nodded, as if *I'll try* from someone who couldn't lie was worth more than *I promise* from anyone else.

Then the fish sank, and his face smoothed over. He hummed two notes of the chant under his breath, without seeming to know he'd done it, and followed the others up the ladder.

---

That night, alone on the chain, Theyab reached for his name and couldn't find it.

The blades had been at him all day. He was so cold he couldn't feel his hands. And he thought, the way you think of something just to make sure it's still there, *My name is*—

And there was nothing.

A blank, smooth place, like the face of a mask. He could feel the edges of where his name should be, the way a tongue feels a lost tooth. He reached for the village, Umm Hanan's bread warm in his hands, and they were greying at the edges like cloth left in the sun.

Worse: it didn't hurt. It felt like setting something down. He understood, then, the young men's faces at the brazier.

*Write it,* the Voice had said. *They always do.*

His finger moved in the sand and salt-dust on the floor of the hold. Searching. He didn't know what for.

"That's not even a letter," said someone.

The hold was full of gold.

---

Hazza' was sitting on the beam above him, swinging his legs.

Under the planks, every hair on my back lay down at once.

"I spend a whole month teaching you," Hazza' said, "and you give up on the first letter. I'm insulted."

"*Dhal*," said Hazza'. He leaned down and pointed at the dust. His finger left no mark. "The old man's bent back, with his dot. Go on. Right to left. You always forget which way."

Theyab's finger moved. A bend. A dot.

"*Ya.* The little bowl, two dots underneath." Hazza' was smiling now. "*Alif.* The tent pole. And *ba*. The boat with one dot, sinking. Go on. Say it."

"*Theyab,*" whispered Theyab.

It came back all at once, like water into a dry well. A small boy alone at the edge of a village, deciding he'd be called a word that meant *wolves*, because the wolves were the only ones out there who'd never once thrown a stone.

"There you are," said Hazza' softly.

His hand came down on Theyab's shoulder. Warm. Warm as the sun, though the frost was creeping out across the boards from where he sat, over the scraped-out names.

"You promised," he said. "Never give up." Then, quieter, with a grin that was almost the old grin: "Besides. There are eleven children up there who are forgetting their names too. Somebody's going to have to teach them. I'd do it, but I'm a bit busy being dead."

Theyab grabbed at his sleeve. His fingers went through it into cold. "What's it *like*? Hazza'. Where you are. Is it—"

Hazza's smile stayed exactly where it was. But his eyes went past Theyab again, to the dark at the end of the hold, the way they had on the dune. As if someone were standing there. As if someone were always standing there, wherever he went now.

"Crowded," he said.

Then the gold went out, and Theyab was alone in the dark, with four letters drawn in the dust beside his hand.

He didn't wipe them away.

In the morning, when the lamp came down the ladder, there were eight letters in the dust, not four. Under his own name, in his own hand, right to left, pressed in deep with one finger, the dot on the *zay* exactly where it belonged:

Hazza'.

He didn't remember writing it. He never did.

---

The guard who came at midnight on the third night was alone, and he was humming.

Theyab knew the tune before he knew the man: the old song of the 'Anqa, the firebird that burns and rises from its own ash, the song he'd heard in his memory at Bir al-Ghina'.

The masked figure came down the ladder with a lamp and two ash-quenched blades crossed on his back, sat a little way off, and went on humming as if he didn't know he was doing it.

The Seventh. Always hunting him. Always, somehow, missing.

Theyab started to hum too.

It just came out of him: the next line, the one the masked man hadn't reached yet.

The humming stopped.

"Where did you learn it?" Theyab whispered. "Who taught it to you?"

No answer. I never once, in all that time, heard him speak.

He stood and drew one of the grey blades. *Drain him slowly.* He knelt by the chain and laid the blade flat against Theyab's arm.

And Theyab's hand closed around his wrist.

He wasn't thinking about himself. That's the only reason it worked. He was thinking of eleven children who had stopped crying, and a boy asking whether his brother was coming.

The light came up out of his chest like the sun coming over the dunes.

*Gold.*

It ran down his arm and into the ash-blade and *through* it, faster than the blade could drink. Up the masked man's wrist. Up his arm. Into him.

And they both saw it.

---

A goat-hair tent, at night. A shamal howling outside, the walls bowing in and out like something breathing.

A small boy, five years old, sitting on a woven rug, very serious. In his arms, held carefully, the way he'd been shown, a newborn wrapped in grey cloth woven all over with strange symbols. The baby had one tiny fist round the boy's finger and wouldn't let go.

Somewhere above them, a man's voice was singing. Low and warm. The firebird, burning and rising.

Then the tent wall tore open, and there were white masks in the storm.

---

The vision broke.

So did the mask.

I heard it from under the hull: a clean crack, like ice splitting on a winter water-skin. A line ran down the white face from brow to chin, with gold shining through. Along the crack the mask had grown *into* him, fine pale threads that bled, very slightly, grey.

And Theyab saw what he always saw: the full moon, Tal Moreeb, the fire. Clearer. He could feel the heat on his shins now, his skin tightening. And there were people in it. A man on his knees in the sand, begging. A figure in a cracked white mask, standing very still.

The Seventh tore free and scrambled backwards, one hand clamped over the crack, making a small broken wordless sound, like a child woken from a nightmare who can't find the tent flap.

Then his blade flashed once. I can't tell you whether he meant to do it. I don't think he knew himself. But the grey chain parted between Theyab's wrists like a cut thread.

And the Seventh fled. Up the ladder, through the hatch, out into the night. Humming again, I swear it, as he went. Broken, out of tune. But humming.

---

He was across the hold before the hatch stopped swinging, the broken sword in his hand and his bag over his shoulder.

He went up the ladder to the children.

They were in a narrow room in the bow, where the hull curved in toward the stem-post, awake. Eleven small faces turned toward the boy in the doorway.

None of them moved. Two were wearing their masks already.

"Up," he said. "All of you. Now. Hold hands. Don't let go."

He went to the nearest masked child and worked his fingers under the rim, gently, the way you'd work out a thorn from a camel's foot, saying anything, *you've got a name, you've got a name*, and it came away with a soft sound like a lip unsticking. Under it was a girl's face: blank, then puzzled, then all at once crumpling.

She cried.

Shibl moved first after that. He got up and took the smallest girl's hand. Then the next child. Then the next. The second mask came off easier.

That's when the shouting started.

Running feet. Grey lamps swinging. Masks poured toward the bow, humming louder and louder. Theyab backed the children into the corner with half a sword and no way out, and the light in his chest flickered grey.

And the floor under them sagged.

---

That was me.

Three nights under the hull with Ghadban's voice in my ears. Three nights of digging along the keel and up under the bow, where the salt had eaten the wood soft as old bread, until I could hear the smallest child's heart.

When I felt the light go gold above me, I knew.

I let it out.

It came up through the burn like smoke through a crack, and all the voices came with it, his the loudest, glad, *there you are, there you are*. My shadow went up from under me, and there was no room in the tunnel for it, so it went up *through* the sand and the planks, long and wrong, a dog's shape the size of a ship's mast, ember eyes, my mouth full of teeth I didn't remember growing.

And I set my shoulders against the underside of that rotten hull, a thin old saluki's shoulders, and I *pulled it down*.

Not lifted. You can't lift a buried ship. I've heard it told that way since, around coffee hearths from Liwa to Ibri, and it's a good story, and it's not what happened. What happened was worse. I dug my claws into the keel-planks above me and pulled, with a strength that came up out of the dark through my legs, and the whole buried section of the bow came away. Wood that hadn't moved since the sea left tore loose like wet cloth. Sand poured in after it in a roaring rush. The ribs cracked one after another, and the deck behind the children's room folded down onto itself with a sound like the whole desert sighing, and the masks running along it went down with it, humming.

And the floor of the children's room fell away into my tunnel, and my tunnel ran out under the hull to the open dune.

I held the rest. The corner of the bow that hadn't fallen yet, the sagging stem-post, the planks over the children's heads, all of it leaning down onto me, every scraped-out name in that ship, on a dog's back.

Through the hole in the broken floor, Theyab looked down at me.

He'd seen this shadow once before, and burned it.

He wasn't afraid now. I saw his face. I'll never forget his face.

"*Ghareeb,*" he said.

That was all. My name. Not *monster*. My name.

And all the voices inside me went quiet. Every one. Even his.

"*Hold it,*" said Theyab.

I barked. Once. It came out of me like a rockslide, and it was not polite.

He laughed. In the middle of all that, he laughed. Then he was down through the hole with eleven children holding hands behind him, sliding down the tunnel past me in the dark, Shibl last, the smallest girl on Theyab's back, crying, still crying, all the way down and out onto the cold open sand under the stars.

When the last bare foot touched the dune outside, I let go.

The bow came down into the hole with a roar of splitting timber, and the ship settled deeper into its dune, groaning, and the sand closed over the broken end of it like water closing over a stone.

---

I don't remember my shadow coming back. I remember lying in the sand at the mouth of the tunnel, only a dog, with my side on fire and my legs not working.

I remember hands. His hands, picking me up. Holding me against his chest, right over the mark, where it was warm.

"I've got you," he said.

I wriggled until he let me down, and dragged myself three steps to a hollow, and dug, and came back with it in my soft mouth, and put it in his palm.

A small bronze bell, no bigger than a thumb.

He looked at it. He looked at me. And behind us, over the ridge, came the sound I'd been dreading: the howl of jinn-jackals, the roar and drum of racing camels, and under it all, the chant. No words. A hundred voices, none of them breathing.

Theyab tied the bell to his belt. He hoisted me over his shoulders, long legs dangling, the way a herdsman carries a lamb. He took Shibl's hand.

Behind him, eleven children were holding on to one another in the dark at the edge of a dead white sabkha. Some were crying. Some weren't. Two still hummed, under their breath, and didn't know they were doing it.

He'd come back for them. Nobody had asked him to. I remembered that, later, when it mattered.

And he ran.

---

*End of Chapter Fifteen*
