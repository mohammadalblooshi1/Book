# Chapter Sixteen
## The Rider Unveiled

> *Every rider in the dark is someone's father. Pray to the One who made the sands that he is yours.*
> — from a lullaby of the herders under Jebel Hafeet, sung only to children who will not sleep

---

Have you ever tried to cross the sands at night with eleven children who have forgotten how to cry?

Don't.

Crying children are loud, but loud is a mercy. Loud means they still want something. These ones ran in silence, a grey huddle of shaved heads, and when one of them fell he didn't call out. He lay with his face pressed into the sand until somebody bigger picked him up, and then he ran again, and his eyes never changed. Every so often, all together, they hummed. Two notes. No words. The chant from the dhow's hold. They didn't know they were doing it.

And Theyab ran in front of them with a broken sword in one hand and Shibl's hand in the other and a half-dead saluki slung across his shoulders the way a shepherd carries a lamb, my legs hanging down his chest, and he didn't stop once.

I could feel his heart through my ribs. It was going like something trying to get out.

Behind us, the jackals were getting closer.

---

You'll remember the jinn-jackals. Lean shapes the colour of nothing, with eyes like coals blown on, and no smell. That is the worst of it, for a dog. A real jackal stinks of carrion and old piss, and you know it's coming from three dunes off. A jinn-jackal smells of nothing at all, the way a cold room smells of nothing, and the first you know of it is the cold.

They track the bearer's Light the way a jackal tracks a wounded gazelle. Theyab's Light was leaking out of him through the golden cracks on his arms, and on that night, with the moon pared down to a fingernail, he must have shone like an open wound.

They were calling to each other. Six voices. Then nine. One of them wasn't howling. One of them, off to the left, was saying *Theyab* in a small wet voice, over and over, the way a child says a word it has only just learned. The wind had carried his name a long way since that night on the dune above Hilat al-Sarab. Things had been practising it.

We came over a ridge, and the far face fell away too steep to run. The children stopped in a huddle at the top. Then, all at once, they turned and looked back the way we had come. Calmly. Like people waiting for a guest they had been told would arrive.

That frightened me more than the jackals did.

Below us, in the trough we'd just crossed, eyes were opening in the dark. Pairs of them, red as coals, spreading out in a ring.

"Behind me," Theyab said to the children. His voice was very calm. "Stay together. Don't look at their eyes."

He set Shibl's hand into the hand of the oldest boy, who looked at it as if he didn't know whose it was. Then he slid me off his shoulders onto the sand, gently, and lifted the broken sword.

I got my front end up and growled at the dark. He looked down at me. "I know," he said. "Too many."

My hackles lay flat. It was true. He knew.

He stood between the dark and the children anyway, and the Light came up in him. Not grey. It came up gold, because he had stopped thinking about himself at all, and I have learned to be very afraid of that particular gold. It's the colour they go near the end.

The first jackal leapt.

---

He cut it out of the air.

Half a blade, the way Hazza' had taught him. The gold ran down his arm into the steel, and when the sword went through the jackal it came apart, and for a moment, before it became ash, I saw what was inside it. Faces. Small grey faces pressed against the inside of the shadow, like children against a window. Then sparks, and nothing.

The second knocked him flat. Its jaws were a finger's width from his face. Its breath frosted his eyelashes. He jammed the hilt between its teeth, light burst from his palm, and it shrieked in a voice that was almost a word and was gone.

Then there were four at once.

He fought. He was bleeding from his cheek, the golden cracks were spreading up his arms like a map of wadis after rain, and he was getting slower. I lay in the sand trying to make my legs remember how to be legs, and I watched him get slower.

The fourth jackal came over his shoulder, straight at the children.

The children didn't move. One of the little ones smiled at it.

And a spear came out of the dark.

---

It took the jackal in mid-leap and pinned it to the dune, and the thing burst into ash that fell on the children's upturned faces like grey snow.

Hoofbeats, soft, the padding thud of a camel at full stretch. A pale she-camel, almost white in the dark, like something that had been dead a while and hadn't been told. On her back a tall figure in grey wrappings, his face veiled to the eyes, leaning low from the saddle to wrench his spear free as he passed.

The Veiled Rider. Twice before he'd come out of the dark for Theyab, and never a word, never a face. This was the third time.

He went through the jackals like a scythe through dry grass. The spear went left and right, the pale camel spun on the spot, and jackals burst into ash wherever he went. Theyab fought beside him with gold flaring off the broken sword. The Nameless camel-riders came over the far ridge, took one look, and wheeled away, their white masks turning to watch us as they went, all at the same angle.

Then it was quiet. Not one of the children was crying. Theyab was on his knees, gasping, grey flakes settling in his hair.

The Rider couched his camel and slid down. He stood looking at the boy. Then, slowly, as though his hands belonged to someone else, he unwound the veil.

---

He was a man of perhaps forty with the face of someone left out in the shamal for a hundred years. Lean, burned dark, beard cropped short and shot with grey, cheeks fallen in. He had the look of a man who has slept alone in cold places for so long that he has started to forget what other people's voices sound like.

And his eyes were light. Too light for a man of the sands. Grey-gold, like a dune at first light.

Theyab's eyes.

I watched the understanding come into Theyab's face a piece at a time, like the sun coming up over a dune, and burn everything it touched.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

The man's mouth moved. Nothing came out. He tried again.

"I'm your father," he said.

The little brass bell on Theyab's belt hung perfectly still. And I, lying in the sand at his side, felt every hair along my spine lie down as smooth as oiled leather. Not a growl in me. The man looked at me as though I'd drawn a knife.

---

Theyab didn't move for a long time.

"Three times," he said at last.

"More than that." The man's voice was rough, like a door that hasn't been opened in years and doesn't want to be. "There were nights you never woke."

"You were *there*." Theyab's voice cracked down the middle. "All this time, watching, and you never—" His chest was heaving. "Why did you leave me? In the sand? Why did you *leave* me there?"

The man went down on his knees in front of him.

"To protect you," he said. "They were hunting us both."

The bell stayed silent. My hackles stayed down.

And that was when Theyab broke.

I'd seen him cry before. Not like this. This came from a place under the floor of him, like the tombs under a city. Fifteen years of it. Every stone. Every *nameless*. Every night in the patched tent at the edge of the village wondering who had thrown him away, and whether they had been right to.

He hit the man once in the chest with his fist. There wasn't any strength in it.

Then he fell forward, and his father caught him.

They knelt in the ash, the boy sobbing into the grey wrappings and the man holding him so tightly I thought something would crack, crying without a sound into his son's sun-bleached hair. Up on the ridge the eleven children stood in a row and watched with no expression at all, and hummed their two notes, and I dragged myself between them and that embrace without quite knowing why.

---

His name was Sharood.

I knew it already. I'd run beside him once, when he was young and had a feather on his chest and was so afraid of Tal Moreeb that he couldn't say the name of the dune aloud. He woke screaming most nights that year. He told me, the way bearers tell me things, lying with his face in my neck, that he could smell his own hair burning in his sleep. He was the eleventh. The one the carvings call *the Runner*. At Ghidd al-Zujaj the rock keeps him as a boy of sixteen, legs pumping, scratched into stone by a hand nobody remembers. It always keeps them as children. Have you noticed that yet? You will.

When he finally looked up from his son, his eyes found mine. The amber one first, then the silver.

"Still alive," he said. "Old dog."

I looked at him. The bearers all say they understand me. He had been a bearer. He understood me well enough. *Still running*, I said, in the only way I have, which is with my eyes and my silence, *Runner*.

He looked away first.

Neither of us did anything else. Anything else would have taken all night, and some of it would have been teeth. I'd known who the Rider was from the first spear in the dark, the night of the stones. I'd known his smell under the camel-smell. And I'd done nothing to let the boy know.

I'm noticing a pattern. I expect you are too. Hold on to it. It gets worse.

---

The caves were a night's walk north and east, where the sand ran out against Jebel Hafeet.

A long pale ridge of limestone heaved up out of the plain by Al Ain like the back of something sleeping, the colour of old bone. At its foot lie the old stone beehive tombs the herders say were built by people before people, each with its little black door. The children hummed a little louder going past them.

The dunes can't reach the mountain. That's why he went there. The sand rolls up to the rock every night and falls back, and it can't write on stone. I went down to the foot of the cliff once, while the others slept, and watched the sand come up against the rock in the dark. It scratched. Long, patient strokes. Like something trying to write with its fingernails.

The caves were high, reached by a goat path that switched back and forth across the cliff. Inside: a small fire of ghaf wood, goat hides, spears along the walls, and dozens of leather sacks hanging from pegs, each one full of the grey ash of the jinn-things he'd spent fifteen years hunting. When the fire popped, the sacks shifted on their pegs, all together, as if the ash inside had turned over in its sleep. I kept one ear on them all night.

The children lay down on the hides in a heap and closed their eyes at the same moment. Theyab counted them twice. Then he sat staring at his own left forearm.

There was writing on it. Scratched into the skin, faint and pink, in the crooked letters Hazza' had taught him in the sand: *DON'T FORGET HIS EYES.*

"I don't remember doing it," he said. He looked at it a long time. "Whose eyes?"

I put my head on his knee. It's what a dog has instead of an answer. I think the grey blades in the dhow had already taken the colour of them, and some part of him had felt it going and tried to hold on with a knife, and then forgotten that too.

Sharood sat down by the sleeping children and, very softly, without seeming to notice, began to sing. A low, warm voice. The old song the herders sing about the 'anqa, the fire-bird that burns and rises from its own ash.

The children's humming stopped. Every one of them, at once.

Theyab went completely still. It was the voice from Bir al-Ghina', the man in the water singing over a small boy's hand. He had been hearing it, somewhere under the floor of himself, his whole life.

---

Sharood told him everything that night. I'll keep it short. Some of it isn't mine to give away.

He had carried the Light before Theyab. He had been afraid. Not of the pain, though there is pain, and he described it, and Theyab listened with his hand pressed to his chest. He was afraid of being gone.

"And something else," he said, looking into the fire. "When you get close to Tal Moreeb, you start to hear it. Not the Ifrit, son. Something on the other side of the Light. It's hungry too, and it's been hungry a long time." He rubbed his face. "I was sixteen. I didn't want to be eaten. So I ran."

My hackles did nothing at all. They didn't rise and they didn't settle. They stood half up, like the fur of a dog who hears a sound from a direction that doesn't exist. Sharood looked at the ridge of my back, and then at my eyes, and neither of us moved.

Because he ran, the grey grew. Years later he came down from the mountain and married, and was happy for a while. Theyab asked about his mother.

"Another night," said Sharood, and his face closed like a tent flap with a wind leaning on it. "I swear it. Not tonight."

The bell didn't ring. He meant it. There wasn't time.

On the night Theyab was born, the Nameless came out of a sandstorm. They wanted the Light, and they knew it had passed into the newborn. Sharood fought. He got the baby out. But his first son, a boy of five, was dragged into the storm by grey hands, and the last thing Sharood heard was the boy's screaming stop. Not fade. Stop. As if someone had closed a lid.

He searched for weeks. He believed the boy was dead. And he knew that as long as the baby was with him, they would find them both. So he carried his son across the sands to a poor village of palm-frond huts at the edge of Liwa, laid him down in the middle of a storm, wrapped in grey cloth, and walked away without looking back, because if he'd looked back he would never have stopped.

"The storm covered my tracks," he said. "That was the desert, keeping you. I had to believe that." He watched the fire. "Every night since, I've wondered whether it was keeping you safe, or keeping you for later. The way you keep a lamb for the feast."

Theyab was quiet a long while.

"Your son," he said. "The one they took. What was his name?"

Sharood closed his eyes.

"Saqr," he said.

And Theyab told him.

---

The guard in the hold. The fire-bird song, hummed in the dark. The Light running up the grey blade. A five-year-old boy in a goat-hair tent, holding a newborn wrapped in grey. The mask cracking down the middle, one pale eye staring out of the crack.

"He's alive," Theyab said. "He's one of them. The Seventh. He's been hunting me since the start." He swallowed. "And he keeps letting me go. He doesn't know why."

Sharood didn't move. I watched the words go into him and keep going, the way a stone dropped into an old well keeps going after you've stopped hearing it.

Then he stood, walked to the mouth of the cave, and went down on his knees on the cold ledge above the plain.

I have heard a great many terrible sounds. The sound Sharood made on that ledge was worse than any of them, because it went on, and then it turned into something that wasn't a sound at all, just his whole body heaving in silence. Fifteen years. His son alive the whole time, his name burned, his face taken, taught to steal other people's children. And his father hiding in a mountain with sacks of ash, telling himself stories.

Theyab didn't go to him. Forgiveness doesn't work like that. Not quickly.

He turned to me instead.

"Did you know?"

I could have looked away. I could have got up and gone to sniff at the sacks, the way a dog does when the people get loud. I'd had a great deal of practice.

I didn't look away.

He knew what that meant. They always do.

Something went across his face that I am still, in the long nights, trying to forget.

"You knew he was my *brother*."

I held his eyes. Amber and silver.

He turned his back on me and didn't speak again that night. I lay outside the circle of the fire, where a dog is put when he has done something wrong, and it was the right place for me.

I'd given him the truth about that one. There was another truth I was still lying on, a heavier one, with blood under it. One out of two. You decide whether that counts.

---

In the morning we gave the children away.

In a fold of the plain at the foot of the mountain, where the warm springs come up out of the rock and the ground goes green for a stone's throw, the Al Bu Ghaf were camped, as they were every year when the nights began to cool. Their headman was broad and grey-bearded, with a voice like gravel in a pan. He gave us coffee first, because you give a guest coffee before you ask him anything, even a guest who comes down off the mountain with a dog and eleven shaved children. Then he looked at the shaved heads for a long time.

"The Nameless?" he said.

"Not anymore," said Theyab.

The children were standing in a row, the way they'd been taught, humming. The headman's wife came out of the nearest tent, saw them, and went back in.

"We'll keep them," he said at last. "Three days is a guest's right. These ones can have more than three days. Until someone comes for them, or the grey lifts." He lowered his voice. "We'll keep a lamp burning where they sleep. And we'll hobble their ankles at night, like young camels, the first while. The ones who come out of that ship, sometimes they walk."

Shibl held on to Theyab's waist a long time.

"Will you find Nadim?" he said into Theyab's shirt.

Theyab couldn't lie. He didn't know.

"If I see him, I'll tell him where you are. I'll tell him you were brave."

That was true. Shibl let go.

The smallest child, a boy of five, tugged Theyab's sleeve. "I don't know my name," he whispered. "They burned it. I watched. It smelled like hair."

Theyab knelt in front of him.

"Then you'll find it," he said. "Or you'll make a new one. I did."

The boy looked at him with no expression at all.

Theyab put two fingers in his mouth and whistled. Not loud. A herder's whistle, the high rising one the camel boys use, that cuts through wind. Every camel in the ring lifted its head.

"When I was little," Theyab said, "I didn't have anybody. So at night I used to whistle, out on the dunes. So I'd know I was still there." He took the boy's small cold hand and folded two fingers of it and put them to the boy's lips. "Like that. Blow."

A wet hiss. A thin squeak. Then, on the fifth try, a real whistle, small and high and wobbling, and the boy's eyes changed. Not much. A crack of something in them, like the first crack in a mask.

"If you're ever lost," said Theyab, "whistle. Somebody might come."

As we climbed back toward the caves I could hear him behind us, all the way to the first switchback, whistling. Thin and wobbling and stubborn, at the plain, at the sky. So he'd know he was still there.

My hackles never rose once. I lay that down in the place I keep such things, and didn't know yet what I was keeping it for.

---

That evening Sharood gave him two things.

The first was a pendant on a leather cord: a knot of old silver, black with age, a loop that turned back through itself and never ended.

"Every bearer has worn one," said Sharood. "I took it off the night I ran. It's yours."

Theyab put it on. It lay below the feather mark, and it was warm, the way a stone is warm when someone has been holding it. Nobody had been holding it.

The second was a small, battered compass in a dented brass case, the kind the pearling captains buy from the Indian traders on the coast.

"It doesn't point north," said Sharood. "It points toward whatever you're running from. For fifteen years it pointed at Tal Moreeb. I stopped opening it. I could feel it pointing through the case, like a finger in my back."

Theyab opened it. The needle spun, wobbled, and settled. Not toward the sands. Not toward Liwa, where the highest dune in the world waited for the full moon.

It pointed at the broken sword, lying on the hides beside the fire.

---

He'd carried that sword every step since the night of the *badr* and never once really looked at it, because looking at it meant looking at a body in the morning sand, grey and cracked with gold.

He turned it over now. Half a blade, notched and dull. A plain leather grip. A round brass pommel.

The pommel was loose. Gritty, as if it had been unscrewed and screwed back in a hurry, in the dark, by someone with shaking hands. He twisted it, and twisted again, and it came away, and the hilt was hollow.

Inside was a roll of paper, tied with thread.

A broken seal of grey wax, pressed with a little curling mark we both knew, because we had watched an old man press that same seal every evening in Al-Nakhlatain by the lamp, with the hooded falcon on its perch not moving.

Wazir Muhaisen's seal.

I smelled it before he'd unrolled it. Rosewater and grey ash. I growled, low, before I knew I was doing it, and my hackles went up so stiff they hurt.

Theyab read it himself. Slowly. Out loud, the way Hazza' had taught him, right to left, his finger under each word.

"*To the Brothers,*" he read. "*On the Night of Lamps, when every lamp in Ubar is lit, the Sheikh will die. Be ready on the stairs of the fort. When he falls, put out the lamps. Then the city. The eldest son is gone. The youngest will come to me. Speak no names.*"

His finger stopped at the bottom.

"*The Voice.*"

---

The fire cracked. On their pegs, the sacks of ash stirred. Down at the foot of the cliff, the sand went on scratching.

"He knew," Theyab said at last. "Hazza'. He was carrying this. Why didn't he tell me?"

"Perhaps he meant to," said Sharood. "Perhaps he ran out of time."

I put my nose on my paws and looked at the fire. Let me keep it a little longer. It's the only thing I have that's heavier than I am.

"Hazza' taught me to read," Theyab said.

It came out of him like something breaking open. As if every evening at Bir al-Ghina', every letter written backwards in the sand, every time Hazza' laughed and rubbed it out with his heel and made him try again, had been leading to this cave and this paper.

As if it had all been for something.

I wanted to believe that with him. It frightened me too. Because when everything in a life starts lining up to lead somewhere, little one, you should ask who drew the line.

Later I watched him unwrap Old Bakhit's broken golden feather. The break had nearly closed, mending a little more every day as he walked toward the end, like flesh closing over a thorn. I hated it.

He slid the letter back into the hilt and screwed the pommel tight. Then he stood.

"I'm going to Ubar," he said.

Sharood was on his feet. "Ubar isn't on any map. Nobody finds it who isn't dead or carrying the dead. And every gate it has is waiting for the Prince-Killer. You'll walk straight into Muhaisen's hands."

"Probably," said Theyab.

The bell didn't ring.

"The Sheikh has to see this. Hazza's little brother is in that fort. And Hazza' died carrying it." He picked up the sword. "I promised him I'd never give up."

Sharood looked at him a long moment. Then he put a hand on his son's shoulder.

Theyab let it stay. He didn't lean into it. He didn't call him father. But he didn't step away.

It's not nothing. In the sands you learn to count the things that are not nothing. You count them the way the people of Ubar count their lamp oil: by the drop, knowing exactly how little is left.

---

*End of Chapter Sixteen*
