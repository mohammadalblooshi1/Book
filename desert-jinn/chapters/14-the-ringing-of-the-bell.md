# Chapter Fourteen
## The Ringing of the Bell

> *The bell does not lie, but it can be too late. A bell rung too late is only a funeral.*
> — a saying of the old healers on the edge of the sands

---

The town was called Al-Nakhlatain, the Two Palms, and it was the kind of place you could love if nobody in it was trying to kill you.

It sat where the gravel plain runs up to the first red dunes, around a spring so old the stones of its well-head had been worn into the shape of hands. Two date palms stood over it, older than anyone's grandfather's grandfather, leaning toward each other like a pair of old men sharing a secret. There were thousands of palms in the gardens below, fed by a falaj that ran out from the spring in a stone channel. But only those two counted. The people said the water would last as long as the second palm stood, so nobody ever climbed them. There was a mud-brick watchtower, a market under palm-frond shades, a dyer's yard hung with indigo cloth, and a hundred flat-roofed houses. No walls. Just water, and people who'd decided to stay near it.

Muhaisen's house stood on the edge of the market. A carved teak door brought up from the coast, a courtyard with a lime tree, and a branch of the falaj running right through it in a plaster channel. In the desert, that is about as close to showing off as you can get.

"Dogs stay in the yard," said the steward at the gate, looking down at me.

Any Bedouin will tell you a saluki isn't a dog, not the way that word is meant. We sleep in the tents. I looked at the steward with my clouded silver eye, and let him look back at it for a while.

He went pale, the way people do the first time, and looked at Muhaisen.

Muhaisen smiled his soft smile. "The saluki may go where he likes," he said. "Outside."

So I slept on the roof.

---

They gave Theyab the top room. A real mattress with a real blanket, a basin of clean water, a window that looked out over the two palms. He stood in the doorway looking at it the way you'd look at a meal you suspected was a trap.

Then he took off the broken sword and laid it on the bed, and his bag beside it. And for the first time since the full moon, he slept through a whole night without the sword in his arms.

I watched him through the window from the roof's edge, my burned side pressed against the warm mud bricks.

I didn't sleep.

The servants in that house didn't speak. They moved through the rooms with lamps and basins and folded cloths, and they never said a word, not to Muhaisen, not to each other. When one passed below me in the courtyard I saw her lips moving under the edge of her face-covering, very slightly, the same small shape over and over, like someone saying a name to keep from losing it.

I told myself it was a prayer. I've told myself a lot of things.

---

You have to understand how good Muhaisen was at it.

Most liars are bad at it. They talk too much. They hurry. They sweat, and my kind can smell it. Muhaisen never hurried, and he never sweated. He wanted you to feel he had all the time in the world, the way you feel the weight of a warm camel-hair blanket.

On the first evening there was coffee, poured three times from a brass dallah with a beak like a bird's, the way you honour a guest. And there were dates on the tray. Dates rolled in honey, on a blue glazed plate.

Theyab stopped in the doorway and stared.

"Hazza' told me you liked them," said Muhaisen gently. "Sit. Eat."

The bell was upstairs, in the bag on the bed. I was on the roof with my ear to a crack in the shutter, and every hair along my back came up, and the growl came up into my throat and I had to swallow it like a stone. Nobody saw.

Theyab sat. He ate one date, slowly, eyes shut, and I watched him go searching for the sweetness the ash-blade had taken in the storm, and find it, and his face broke open with relief. He didn't touch the twist of waxed cloth in his pocket. He ate Muhaisen's dates instead, and his eyes filled, and Muhaisen pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in days.

That's how it's done. Not with chains. With honey, and coffee poured three times, and a real bed, and an old man who knows exactly when to look away.

---

Muhaisen wrote letters in the evenings.

He sat at a low table by the lamp with a reed pen and a little stone seal he wore on a cord around his neck. When each letter was done he folded it, dripped grey wax onto the fold, and pressed the seal in with his thumb. Every night a saker falcon flew out of the window with a letter tied to its leg, and just before it went, it turned its head and looked at Theyab with one black eye.

On the second evening Theyab sat across from him and watched the pen move.

"*Water,*" he said quietly. "That word. *Ma'.* Upside down from here. It says *water*."

The pen stopped.

Muhaisen looked up. For a moment, only a moment, something moved behind those faded grey eyes, quick and cold as a fish turning in the dark of a falaj.

"You can read," he said.

"A little. Hazza' taught me."

"Did he." Muhaisen set down the pen. "He was a patient teacher?"

Theyab almost smiled, the first I'd seen since the full moon. "No. He was terrible. He laughed every time I wrote a *kaf*. He said they looked like camels kneeling down the wrong way."

"Then you must be a very good student," said Muhaisen, and went back to his letter, and folded it, and sealed it.

I remember the seal. The grey wax, and the little curling mark it left. Some part of me must have known. The part of me that always knows things a moment too late.

---

On the third evening he talked about his son.

"I had a son once," said Muhaisen.

He said it to the lamp, not to Theyab.

"And a brother. A long time ago, before I came to the sheikh's court. There was a fever that year in the town where I was born, down on the coast. It came through like the shamal. My brother first. Then my boy." He turned his little coffee cup slowly in his long fingers. "He was six. He used to sit on my knee while I wrote, and try to catch the pen."

On the roof, my back lay flat as poured water. Every word was true.

That's the thing about truly dangerous liars. They hardly ever lie. They tell you the truth, and the truth is sad and real, and it opens you like a key in a lock. Then, once the door is open, they walk in and close it softly behind them.

"I'm sorry," said Theyab.

"So am I." Muhaisen smiled, tired and gentle. "I spent a long time trying to forget them. It doesn't work, you know. Not the way you'd hope." He looked at the lamp a moment longer. "Not unless you're willing to forget everything else as well."

Theyab was quiet. Then he said, "Tell me about my father."

"He carried what you carry. The Light. Before you. The carvings have a name for him. You've seen them, I think. At Ghidd al-Zujaj."

Theyab's hand went to his chest.

"*The One Who Ran*," he whispered.

"The One Who Ran. He was afraid of what the Light would ask of him. So he ran." A pause. "He's alive, Theyab."

"Where?" His voice cracked. "Where is he?"

"When you're stronger," said Muhaisen, and reached across the tray and laid a hand over Theyab's, very lightly. It was a dry hand, cool, and it stayed a moment too long. "I promise. When you're stronger, I'll take you to him."

My hackles twitched, then didn't. I could never tell, with him. That frightened me most of all.

He trusted him.

For the first time since Hazza', Theyab trusted someone.

---

That night he climbed up to the roof.

He sat down beside me with his knees pulled up, looking out over the two palms and the dark dunes beyond. The moon was a half now, and shrinking.

"He's alive," Theyab said at last. "My father."

I put my chin on his knee.

"He said he'd take me to him."

He looked down at me. "You don't believe him."

My back stayed exactly as it was. No hackles. No growl. Nothing, because I didn't *know*, and a dog can't say *I don't know* any more than he can say anything else.

"Ghareeb."

I got up. I went to the top of the stairs and looked back at him, and then down toward his room, and back at him, the way a hunting dog does when the hare's in a hole and the man's too slow to see it.

He frowned. "What?"

"I'm not playing games, Ghareeb." Sharper. "If you know something, *show* me. If he's lying, show me what about."

I could have stood under the old man's window and growled until the whole town came out with lamps. And then he'd have asked *why*, with his eyes, the way the bearers ask, and I'd have had to show him where I'd been on the night of the full moon, and what had been standing behind me. I wasn't ready. Not with him. Not even with you, yet. And I had no proof but a dog's growl, and my growl had gone silent once, at Ghidd al-Zujaj, when it should have spoken.

I lay back down.

Theyab looked at me a long time. Then he made a small, tired sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

"Where were you?" he said. "That night."

I put my head on my paws.

"That's what I thought," he said, and went back down the stairs.

---

I went down later, when the house was asleep.

Along the top of the courtyard wall, and in at Theyab's window. He was asleep on his face with one arm off the mattress.

His bag was on the floor by the bed. I put my nose in it. Dates and the old smell of Hilat al-Sarab, smoke and goats and poverty. And at the bottom, wrapped in a rag, a small brass bell no bigger than a thumb, on its cord of braided camel hair.

I took it in my mouth, carefully, so it didn't sound. Then I laid it on the mattress beside his face, an inch from his nose, where it would be the first thing he saw.

---

In the morning, he wore the bell.

He tied it to his belt by its camel-hair cord, under the fold of his shirt. A small brass bell no bigger than a thumb.

I went down into the market to find something to eat.

It was an ordinary oasis market at the end of the morning: dates, rope, goats complaining, a coffee-seller with his brass pots in the coals. By the old well under the two palms, an old man sat cross-legged on a mat with a tin cup, and the children were teasing him.

He was blind the way pearl divers go blind, his eyes cooked white by forty summers of opening them in salt water on the banks. His nose was flattened where the bone clip had pinched it shut every dive of his life, and his fingers were seamed with old white scars from the oyster shells.

"Tell the one about the ship," said a boy, and dropped a date in his cup.

So he told it, half sung, the way the old divers tell things. A pearling dhow from the coast, generations back, a big one with forty divers and as many pullers and a *nahham* to sing them down. Lying off the banks on a black night with no moon. And a voice coming up out of the water, calling one diver by his name, in his own mother's voice. Then another name. Then all of them.

"*Bu Darya*," the old man said, and the children went quiet. "The Father of the Sea. Big as a reef. A man's throat on him and a fish's belly, and no pity in either. He calls you by your name, and you answer, and over the side you go. And he takes you down to the dark of the banks and keeps your voice, to use on the next boat."

He sipped his coffee. "They found that dhow years after. Inland. A day's ride from any shore, lying on the salt where the sea went back. Not a man of her crew aboard. And you go near that sabkha at night, you'll hear them still. Calling to the pullers to haul them up. Calling and calling." He tapped the cup with one scarred finger. "Never answer a voice from the water, boys. Not even when the water's gone."

The children ran off shrieking, happy. The old man turned his white eyes to the shade where I was standing, which no blind man should have been able to do.

"Stay off the dead salt, saluki," he said. "It remembers what it ate."

I didn't know then that I'd be lying under that ship in two days' time. I'm not sure I'd have done anything differently if I had. That's the trouble with warnings. They only work on the people who were going to stay home anyway.

---

That's where I saw Nadim.

I almost didn't know him. The boy I'd last seen in Hilat al-Sarab had been broad and loud and well-fed, the well-keeper's son. This one was thin as a stick. Rags. Feet bound in strips of cloth gone black. He stood at the old well under the two palms with his hands cupped, begging for water, and nobody gave him any. They looked at his face and stepped round him.

There was a grey streak down one side of it. Not the old one, from the emptied thing in the lane. A new one, running from his temple into his hair, and the hair there had gone grey too, all the way to the roots, as if someone had laid a cold thumb on him and pressed. When he blinked, the eye on that side was a little slow to follow.

I went very still in the shade of a palm-frond awning.

Then the wind turned, and brought him to me.

Sweat. Fear. The black rot of feet bound too long in the same rags. And under all of it, worked into his hair and the threads of his shirt the way smoke works into the walls of a tent, a scent that had no business on a starving boy. Oud. Musk. And beneath them, faint and sweet, meat turning in the sun.

He'd been close to her. Not for one night on the road. Close for days. Close enough that she'd touched him, and kept touching him, the way you keep a hand on a camel you don't want to wander.

The children at the well wrinkled their noses at him and moved off without knowing why. A married man coming out of the coffee-seller's stopped, sniffed, and looked at the ragged boy with an expression I've seen on men's faces before, a kind of sick recognition, and walked quickly the other way.

He didn't see me. He was watching the house with the teak door the way a starving dog watches a butcher's stall. When the market got busy around noon he slipped off down a narrow lane between the dyer's yard and the garden wall.

I followed him. Every footprint he left was full of his fear.

The lane ended in a little dead square full of broken water jars, where the dyer's indigo cloths hung over the wall like curtains in a room where someone has died.

Someone was waiting there.

Grey robe. Smooth white mask.

I flattened myself behind a cracked jar and stopped breathing.

---

"Well?" said the mask. Its voice was flat and dry, like sand poured from a cup. It didn't seem to come from behind the mask. It seemed to come from all around the little square at once.

"He's here," said Nadim. His voice shook. "You were right. The house with the carved door. The top room, over the palms. He sleeps by the window. He's got a broken sword. He keeps it on the bed."

My back stayed flat. True. All of it.

"You're certain it's him."

"I'd know him anywhere." Nadim swallowed. "I've known him all my life."

"Good. Tonight, then."

"And Shibl?" Nadim took a step forward. "You *promised*. When she brought us to you in the desert, you said you'd keep him safe from the grey. You said if I found the glowing boy, you'd give my brother back."

"When we have the bearer," said the mask, "the child goes free."

Every hair on my body stood up at once.

A growl came up out of me before I could stop it, just a breath of it against the clay of the jar. I wanted to scream at him, *Look at me!* But he wasn't looking at me. He was looking at that blank white oval as if it were the only door left in the world.

"Everyone's gone grey," he said. His voice cracked in half. "Do you understand? All of them. My father sits by the well all day with the bucket rope in his hands, and he says—" He stopped. He made himself go on. "He says, *Draw it up slow, boys, there's plenty.* That's all. Over and over. That's what he was saying to us the morning it came. He doesn't know my name. He says it to me like I'm a stranger. Sheikh Mazyad walks round and round the square in the dark. And Ghanim—" He laughed, a terrible little sound. "Ghanim gave you his camels. Did you know that? Every thaler he'd ever hoarded, every rupee, the whole chest from under his floor. For a safe road out. He gave it all to you, and you took it, and then you took *him*." He pressed his hands over his face. "I took Shibl and I ran, and we walked for days, and then you came, and—" He stopped again. "He's seven. He's only seven."

"Tonight," said the mask.

It didn't turn to go. It just stood there with its blank face pointed at Nadim, perfectly still, until Nadim backed out of the square himself. And when I looked back, after Nadim had gone, the mask was still standing there, facing the empty place where he had been.

Then, very slowly, it turned its face toward the water jar I was hiding behind.

It didn't come over. It didn't need to. It only tilted its head, the way the emptied do, as if it had heard a sound it almost remembered. A growl, maybe. A dog it used to know.

---

Why would the Nameless need a starving boy to tell them where Theyab slept, when Muhaisen had chosen the room himself? I understood later. A man like Muhaisen never lets his own hands be seen. If the bearer lived long enough to wonder how the masks had found him, it would be Nadim's face he remembered. The boy who'd thrown the first stone. Not the kind old man with the dates.

It was a clever plan. It would have worked.

Except for a small brass bell, no bigger than a thumb, that a dog had carried in his mouth and laid by a sleeping boy's face.

---

I ran.

The teak door was shut.

I found a place where the garden wall was low, and jumped. I hit the top on my chest and scrabbled over, and came down hard on the burn. By the time I'd got up and found my way round to the courtyard, the sun was going down red behind the palms, and the whole yard was the colour of the inside of an eyelid.

The giant was standing by the falaj channel.

He turned his head and looked at me. That was all. He stood between me and the door, and when I tried to go round him, he was there. When I tried the other way, he was there too. He never seemed to move. He was simply always where I was going.

I was still standing there, shaking, my shadow trembling long across the plaster, deciding, when I heard the sound upstairs.

---

They were sitting at supper. That was the strange thing, Theyab told me later. Such an ordinary evening.

Then footsteps on the stairs. Many of them, soft and quick and perfectly in step.

And under them, faint, a hum. No tune. No words.

The door opened.

Grey robes. White masks. Five, then eight, filling the doorway and spilling into the room, and the lamps all leaned away from them, every flame bending the same way, as if a cold wind had come in with them. In their hands were blades, long and thin, the colour of old ash, and they drank the lamplight instead of reflecting it.

The smell came up through the floor to me in the courtyard. Burning cloth. That sweet scorched something. And under it, out of place, a thread of rose attar, the cheap heavy kind traders splash on to cover the smell of their camels.

I knew that attar. I'd smelled it every week for fifteen years, over a trading counter in Hilat al-Sarab, while a big man with a thumb on his brass scale told a scavenger boy his copper was worth half what it was.

Theyab was on his feet. The tray went over. His hand went to his belt for the knife, and the knife wasn't there. It was upstairs in his bag, with the broken sword, on the bed by the window, exactly where Nadim had said it would be.

And Muhaisen rose.

He rose smoothly from his cushion, the old man in blue, and stepped between Theyab and the masks. He put one arm out behind him, shielding the boy, the way a father shields a child.

"Stay behind me, child," he said. His voice was low and calm and full of love. "You are my guest. You're safe under my roof. No one will harm you while I live."

And the bell rang.

---

It wasn't loud. That's what I always remember. I heard it from the courtyard, through the shutters: one clear note, high and sweet as a drop of water falling into a well. A tiny sound.

It filled the whole room.

The masks stopped humming. All of them, at once.

Theyab went completely still.

Muhaisen turned his head. The soft face didn't change at all. But the eyes did. They moved down to the bell and rested there, and something in them went flat and cold and very, very tired, like a man who has been wearing a heavy cloak for a long time and has finally been allowed to put it down.

"Ah," he said quietly. "Bakhit."

Theyab's lips were white. "The ring," he whispered. "Did he give you the ring?"

"Of course he did," said Muhaisen.

The bell rang again. Louder. And it didn't stop shivering after, the way a bell does. It kept trembling on its cord, humming to itself, as if it had more to say and couldn't find the note.

Then it all came at once, each piece a small stone striking his back. The bag on the camel. The dates on the plate. *Hazza' told me you liked them.* The ring that had been warm from Muhaisen's robe. The guest-right, the coffee poured three times.

*He gave me this.*

"No," said Theyab. It came out of him like something tearing. "*No.*"

Muhaisen sighed. He lowered his arm.

"I should have searched you myself," he said, and stepped aside.

---

The masks came in.

Theyab fought. I want you to know that. No knife, no sword, no friend, and he fought anyway: fists, the brass tray, a lamp hurled in a burning arc that sent two masks reeling back with their robes alight. They didn't scream as they burned. They just patted at the flames, calmly, and came on.

One of them had a little brass trader's scale hanging from his belt on a cord, the pans swinging empty. He was big under the robe. He walked a little heavy on the left foot, the way a man does who's spent thirty years standing behind a counter. And as he came at Theyab, from behind the blank white face, in a voice I knew, he said:

"*Short weight, boy. Short weight. That's all it's worth.*"

Over and over. The last thing he'd said before they burned his name. Probably to someone else, at the end, bargaining for his road out with the last thing he had. Probably he'd thought he was winning.

Theyab didn't know him. He'd never once looked that man in the face; you don't, when you're the boy being cheated. But I knew. And I'll tell you what I felt, because I promised to tell you the truth: it wasn't glad. I'd have liked it to be. It was only cold.

And the light came.

But it came *grey*.

Thin and dim and sputtering, the colour of fear and fury. It crawled out of his fists like smoke and did nothing at all. A mask stepped in close and laid an ash-quenched blade flat against his forearm, almost tenderly.

Theyab screamed.

It didn't cut. There was no blood. But where the blade touched him, the grey light went *into* it, drawn out like water into dry sand, and something left him. A memory. He'd tell me later he never knew which. Only that it had been warm, and now there was a cold place with smooth edges where it used to be, and that he sometimes caught himself reaching for it, in the dark, the way you reach for a step that isn't there.

That was when I came through the shutters.

I don't remember how I got past the giant. Then I was in the room in a spray of broken wood, going for the nearest mask the way a saluki takes a gazelle, by the hind leg.

I got one ankle. That's all. It was cold. Cold as a cistern in winter, and soft, like something kept too long out of the light. Then a hand like the side of a house closed round my middle, right over the burn, and the giant lifted me, and I made a sound I'm not proud of, and he threw me.

I hit the wall. The world went white, then grey, then very far away.

---

The last thing Theyab saw, before the sack came down over his head, was the doorway.

Someone was standing in it. Behind the masks, in the shadow at the top of the stairs. A thin boy in rags, feet wrapped in cloth, a grey streak down his face.

Nadim.

Theyab told me afterwards that for a moment, over the burning cloth, the room smelled sweet. Heavy and sweet, like a wedding tent. He'd thought it came from the masks.

It didn't. I smelled it too, from the floor, through the white roaring in my head, and I knew exactly whose it was, and on whom. It came in through that doorway on a thin boy's rags and lay over the wrecked room like a hand: oud, and musk, and under them the meat in the sun.

They looked at each other across the wrecked room. The boy who'd thrown the first stone, and the boy it had hit.

"They've got Shibl," Nadim said. His voice was barely there. "I'm sorry. Theyab, I'm sorry."

He had never once, in fifteen years, called him by his name. I don't think he'd even known it, until the masks told him.

The bell on Theyab's belt hung silent. Every word was true.

Then the sack came down, and it smelled of burned cloth and something sweeter underneath, and the ash-blades came with it, and the room went dark.

---

I woke alone on the floor of a room that smelled of lamp oil and spilled coffee and lentils.

On the floor near the hearth lay a snapped length of camel-hair cord, and beside it, on its side, a small brass bell.

I picked it up in my teeth, gently, the way I'd carried it the night before, cord and all, and hid it in the soft of my mouth where no mask would think to look. It was still faintly warm. And very faintly, so faintly I might have imagined it, it was still trembling.

I didn't know where they'd taken him. I didn't know whether there'd be anything left of him when I found him. I only knew that the bell had rung, and rung too late, and that I had been standing in a courtyard when it did, with the truth in me, and no way to say it, and no courage to show it.

Nadim told me the rest of it many years later, sitting by a fire with his hand on my head the way the bearers used to. Only once. He was a grown man with grey in his beard by then, and Hessa was asleep, and he'd been staring into the coals a long time.

He said he'd carried Shibl out of Hilat al-Sarab on his back when the grey took their father, and walked until his feet bled through the rags, and on the fourth night, on the salt road, a woman had stepped out of the dark ahead of them. He said she smelled like a sheikh's wedding. He said she called his brother by his name before he'd spoken a word, and said it so gently that he wept, because nobody had said Shibl's name kindly in days. Not even him. She gave them water from a skin that never seemed to empty, and walked ahead of them all night, and every time he stumbled she turned her head and smiled, and he got up.

"Just before dawn we came to a fire," he said. "White masks round it. She stopped at the edge of the light and let me walk past her into it." His hand went still on my head. "I looked back. Once. To thank her. Her hands were wrong, Ghareeb. Long. Curved. Like the sickles you cut fodder with. And her face—" He stopped. "I don't know what her face was. I think she'd only been wearing it for me."

He never said whether she was one of them, a Nameless woman with oud on her robe and a trick of knowing names, or the thing the grandmothers warn their sons about, borrowed for the road. I never asked him. My hackles stayed flat the whole time he talked. Whatever she was, he told it true.

He said he never forgave himself for that night.

Neither did I.

---

*End of Chapter Fourteen*
