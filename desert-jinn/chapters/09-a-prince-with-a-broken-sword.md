# Chapter Nine
## A Prince with a Broken Sword

> *A broken blade still cuts, if the hand is true. So does a broken promise.*
> — words cut above the door of an old swordsmith's forge in Nizwa, the rest of the line chiselled away

---

Night one. I counted it at dawn, which is cheating, but I'm the one telling this and I'll count how I like. You'll find the one telling the story always does.

Theyab woke to the sound of someone fighting nobody.

He came up out of his cloak with his knife already in his fist, the way he'd woken every morning of his life, and found Hazza' out on the open sand between the palms, moving through the grey light before sunrise. The broken sword flashed and cut the air in long looping patterns, wrist and shoulder and hip all turning together, as if the whole thing were a dance he'd been taught before he could walk.

Which, as it turned out, it was.

Theyab had learned to fight from bullies and falling walls. He had never seen fighting that was *beautiful*.

"You're staring," said Hazza', without stopping.

"Sorry."

"Don't be. I'm magnificent." He finished the pattern with the broken point a finger's width from a palm trunk, held it there, then lowered it. "Taught by the best swordsman in my father's guard, and then by every guard who wanted to knock a sheikh's son on his backside and call it a lesson. I have never lost a fight."

From inside Theyab's bag, muffled by the rag he kept it wrapped in, came a small clear *ting*. Old Bakhit's little brass bell. He'd kept it wrapped since the caravan, where no one could see it glint and ask.

And at the same moment, from beside the cold fire, came a growl.

That was me. I never mean to. When a man lies within reach of my nose, the hackles come up from my neck to my tail in a stiff ridge, and a sound comes out of my chest like a stone being dragged. It is the one part of me I have never been able to train.

Hazza' looked at the bag. He looked at me, with my lip lifted. He looked at Theyab.

"Your dog," he said, "is growling at me."

"He does that when people lie," said Theyab. "And that's a truth bell. It rings when someone lies to me."

Hazza' put his sword away with great dignity.

"Twice," he said. "I've lost twice. Once to one of the guards when I was nine, and once to a camel."

No bell. My hackles lay down smooth, and I put my chin back on my paws. The corner of Theyab's mouth twitched.

"A *camel*?"

"It was a very large camel, and it was a racing bull in its season, and I don't want to talk about it."

---

There was something wrong about that sword, and Theyab noticed it while Hazza' was picking sand out of the guard.

The hilt was wrapped in leather gone black with hand-sweat, and the pommel was a round bronze cap that didn't quite match, as though made for a different sword.

"Why don't you mend it?" Theyab asked.

"Some things you don't mend."

It was true. My hackles didn't stir. It just wasn't an answer.

"The old smiths of my city made their hilts hollow," Hazza' said after a moment. He twisted the bronze pommel off. Inside the grip was a narrow empty space, the length of a finger. "You're meant to keep a blessing in here. A holy man writes it very small on a scrap of paper, and you fold it tight and carry it into every fight."

"What's in yours?"

Hazza' tipped the hilt toward him. Empty. Dark in there, like a little well.

"I was never much good at asking for things," he said, and screwed the pommel back on.

Theyab nodded slowly. "Me neither."

It was a small thing. Two boys admitting a small thing to each other by a well. But I've learned to watch small things. They're usually where the big ones hide.

---

He told his story that night, by the fire, with the dune humming over them like a crowd that had come to listen.

Theyab didn't ask. That matters. If he asked, he'd be asked back. But somewhere in the long hot day something had loosened between them, and when the stars came out, Hazza' simply started talking.

"I'm from Ubar," he said.

Theyab laughed. Then he saw Hazza''s face, and stopped.

Every child in the sands knows that name. Mothers in Hilat al-Sarab used it on children who wandered: *Come home before dark, or Ubar will come up out of the sand and take your name.* The city of a thousand pillars, swallowed by the desert for its pride.

"Ubar's a story," Theyab said.

"It's a story with very thick walls," said Hazza'. He smiled crookedly at the fire. "The City of the Dead and Darkness. The sand never swallowed it. It's down in a great hollow in the deep sands where no caravan goes, and you can ride within a day of it and never see a tower. The walls are towers, all joined together, very high. And there are lamps on every wall, thousands, burning day and night, and they must never all go out. There are men whose whole lives are walking the walls with oil jars." He poked the fire. "And under the city there are tombs, deeper than wells. On quiet nights, if you lie with your ear on the palace floor, you can hear a sort of knocking down there. Very slow. The old men say it's the stone settling."

"Is it?"

"I was the sheikh's son. Nobody told me the truth about anything." He shrugged. "And on the walls of every lane, someone has painted the names of the dead. Back to the beginning. The painters go round touching them up every year, because the names fade." He paused. "They fade faster than they used to. That's what the old painters say. Some fade in a single night. You go to bed and your grandfather's on the wall, and you wake up and there's just a pale patch where he was. No one knows why."

"A city that won't let anyone be forgotten," he said. "Mostly."

He turned the heavy gold ring. The tower and the lamp caught the firelight.

"My father is Sheikh Mutlaq," he said. "I'm his eldest son. Or I was. It's complicated."

I lay perfectly still, hackles flat. Theyab looked at me, the way he'd begun to whenever anyone spoke. Then at the young man who scraped jinn-ash into a jar for a living. His mouth fell open.

"You're the sheikh's *son*."

"Was. Probably still am. No one's quite sure. It depends who you ask, and whether they've got a khanjar in their hand at the time." Hazza' shrugged. "Close your mouth. You'll get sand in it."

---

There was a boy called Suhail.

He worked in the sheikh's stables, Hazza' said, looking after the riding camels and the sheikh's white racing she-camels and the three old mares that nobody rode any more. He was maybe twelve, with ears that stuck out like a jerboa's and a laugh like a donkey with hiccups, and he knew the name of every animal in the stables and told each one *good morning* every day.

"His mother named him for the star," Hazza' said. "Born the week Suhail rises. She said he'd bring the cool. I used to hide in the stables. When lessons got too much. When my father got too much. Suhail never told. He'd hand me a brush and point at a camel."

One night Suhail was sent outside the walls on an errand, past the last lamp. No one would ever say who sent him. He came back grey.

"Not all the way," Hazza' said. "But half of him was gone. He'd stand there turning his hands over as if they belonged to someone else." His voice had gone flat and careful, like a man walking on a roof that might give. "And he'd smile, sometimes. That was the worst. Not his smile. A different one. As if someone had shown him what a smile was once and he'd copied it wrong. And he'd say *good morning*. Just that. *Good morning, good morning*, at midnight, to the wall."

Theyab's hand had gone to his chest. Behind my eyes I heard a man with an orange beard calling *couch the camels in a ring* across an empty plain.

"The law of Ubar says the emptied are to be carried out past the last lamp and left for the sands. Before it spreads. So I hid him."

"Where?"

"An old fodder loft over the stables. I brought him food. I sat with him every night. I read him the names off the walls, all the old names, thinking if he heard enough names he might find his own again." Hazza' swallowed. "Once, after about a month, he almost smiled. His own smile. I swear by my mother's grave he did. *Almost*."

The fire cracked. No one said anything.

"The Wazir found out," Hazza' said. "Muhaisen. My father's counsellor. He's never once raised his voice in my hearing. He has grey eyes, pale grey, like someone left them out in the sun too long." He picked at the cloth on his shoulder. "And he stood in my father's majlis, in front of every elder in Ubar, and said very gently that the sheikh's son had been hiding a jinn-ridden thing in the palace and doing sorcery on it. *Treason*. He said it so kindly, Theyab. As if it grieved him."

Grey eyes. A soft voice. I've known a few men with eyes like that. None of them were ever grieved by anything.

"Was it true?" Theyab asked.

"I was hiding him," said Hazza'. "The rest was a lie."

I didn't growl. I didn't growl for either half, because he believed both, and so did I.

"They sent guards that night. Six of them. I stood in the loft door." He touched the hilt of the broken sword lying beside him. "One had an iron-bossed shield, and I hit it wrong. I was trying to get past it to the one who'd caught Suhail's arm. The blade went in half. I kept fighting with what was left." He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I'm very good with what's left. I've had practice."

"And Suhail?"

The dune hummed.

"Muhaisen had him *taken away*," Hazza' said. "That's all anyone would tell me. I asked the stable hands, and they said, *What boy?* As if he was never there." He looked at his hands. "I've been looking for most of a year. Sometimes I wake up and I can't remember his face either. Just the ears. And the *good morning*."

"And your father—"

"Wouldn't look at me." Hazza''s voice didn't change. That was the worst of it. "Muhaisen did all the talking. My father sat there with his hand over his eyes. And then I was outside the gate with a broken sword and a camel and a bag of dates, and that was that."

He picked up a stick and drew in the sand. A small figure. A stick boy.

"My brother Zaid ran after me," he said. "He's ten. He got all the way to the outer gate in his bare feet, crying so hard he couldn't talk. He just grabbed my *thawb* and wouldn't let go." His mouth twisted. "They had to carry him back. Muhaisen carried him. Very gently. Zaid stopped crying the moment Muhaisen picked him up. Just stopped, all at once, like a lamp blown out." He rubbed the stick boy away with his palm. "I can still hear him. I can still hear him *stop*."

Theyab looked at the smeared place in the sand a long time.

Then, very quietly, because a story is owed for a story and Theyab always paid what he owed, he told Hazza' about Hilat al-Sarab.

The stones. The scar under his shoulder blade. Nadim, and the lane full of the emptied, and little Shibl going grey and coming back. The faces afterwards. The wadi. The caravan, and Jasir drinking the last water and spitting out the word. Every time somebody had used it.

When he finished, Hazza' said nothing for a while.

"They'll call you a monster, Theyab," he said at last. "They called me one too." He looked up from the fire, and the crooked smile came back, a little shaky at the edges. "Let's be monsters together, then."

I got up, and walked round the fire, and lay down between them, touching both.

And out past the palms, beyond the firelight, I heard something move in the sand. Not a jackal. Something slower. Something that had been lying under the dune listening to two boys say their secrets out loud in the dark, and had liked it.

I lay between them and the dark all night with my ears turned outward. Neither of them asked why.

---

Night four. That was the night of the scorpions.

I want to say first that it was not my fault.

I had found a fat yellow one under a flat stone, as long as Hazza''s hand, nipped the sting off the way I'd done for a thousand years, and crunched it by the fire with my mouth open. Nothing ruins a bearer's appetite faster, and a bearer with no appetite isn't thinking about anything worse. Theyab made a small sick noise, which was what I'd been going for.

Hazza', however, leaned in.

"I wonder what it tastes like."

"*No*," said Theyab.

"Why not? He eats them. He looks fine." He was already on his feet. "And I've eaten locusts fried in butter in the souk at Nizwa. Everybody eats locusts."

"Locusts aren't scorpions."

"Locusts are scorpions with better manners."

He came back a short while later with his own scorpion pinned under the flat of his broken sword, looking deeply pleased with himself. He pulled the sting off the way he'd watched me do it. The legs were still going.

"Hazza'," said Theyab.

"Don't be a coward. I once ate a sheep's eye at a wedding feast in Ibri. It looked back at me the entire time."

He put the scorpion in his mouth and crunched.

His face did several interesting things in a row. Then he swallowed, nodded slowly, and said, "Huh. Actually, that's not so—"

He was sick for a full day.

---

I've watched a great many people be ill. Plague in the coast towns. The fever off the *sabkha* that takes whole villages in a season. Hazza' was not the worst. But he was the loudest. He lay in the shade with his arms flung out, turning interesting shades of green, rolling over now and then to be sick, and announcing to the sky that he was dying.

"Tell Zaid," he croaked, "that I died bravely. In battle. Against a beast of terrible size."

"It was the length of your hand," said Theyab.

"Tell him it was *this* big." He tried to spread his arms and gave up halfway. "Theyab. Theyab. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"I'm leaving you my sword. It's broken. Look after it. Don't let the dog near it. He'll bury it."

When he said that, something cold went down my back, like a door opening in an empty house. The desert has a terrible sense of humour, and it never forgets a line.

And Theyab laughed.

It wasn't a big laugh. It came out of him in a short surprised burst, like a sandgrouse startled off a waterhole. Then another, and then he had his hand over his mouth and his shoulders were shaking, and he couldn't stop.

Hazza' lifted his head off the sand. His face was green and his beard was full of grit.

"Is that—" he said. He looked at me, as if I could tell him. "Is he *laughing*? I'm dying and he's *laughing*?"

I thumped my tail on the sand. Twice. It is the closest a saluki comes to *yes, he is, and you deserve it*.

I'll tell you something now, and you can do what you like with it.

I had not heard Theyab laugh in eight years. Not since he was seven, and gave himself a name, and sat on the lip of Saqi's well saying it over and over under his breath until it made him giggle. After that, the village took the laughing out of him the way they took everything else. One stone at a time.

And here was this green, groaning, ridiculous sheikh's son, flat on his back in the sand at Bir al-Ghina', and he'd given it back without even trying.

I want you to hold on to that. I've told you how this ends, and there aren't many sounds like that one coming.

Theyab offered to help. He held out his hands, with the gold already stirring under the skin.

"Absolutely not," Hazza' moaned. "Every jackal for three days would come running, and I'd have to fight them lying down. And you'd go all strange in the eyes again. I'll be sick the honest way, thank you."

So Theyab sat with him all afternoon with water and a wet cloth, and every so often looked over at Hazza' groaning on the sand and started laughing all over again.

I didn't eat a scorpion in front of either of them for three days after that. I'd like that noted. It was a sacrifice. I know about those.

---

The lessons began on the sixth night.

It was Hazza''s idea. He'd been watching Theyab stare at the marks scratched into the stone lip of the oldest well, by some herdsman centuries ago, and finally he said, "You can't read, can you?"

Theyab's whole body went stiff. His hand went to his chest.

"No."

He said it to the fire. In Hilat al-Sarab almost no one could read, and Theyab had never once been allowed to sit with the boys at their lessons under the ghaf tree. And somewhere under his ribs he had already begun to dread the moment Hazza' would realise how far below him he was, and look at him differently, and go.

But Hazza' only smoothed a patch of sand flat with his palm and picked up a stick.

"Right," he said. "Come here."

He drew a shape. A little curved hook, like a bent finger, with a dot above it.

"We write from the right," he said. "Like this. That's *dhal*. It makes a *dh* sound, with your tongue between your teeth. Like the start of *dhi'b*. A wolf." To its left he drew three more. A little cup with two dots beneath. A tall straight stroke. A shallow boat with one dot underneath it. "*Ya*. *Alif*. *Ba*."

He sat back.

"That's your name," he said.

Theyab stared at it.

Four shapes in the sand, wobbly and crooked, because Hazza' wrote like a sheikh's son who'd always had a scribe to write for him. ذياب. *Theyab*. The only thing in the world that had ever been his, lying there outside him, where anyone could see it. Where anything could.

"Is it—" Theyab's voice cracked. "Is that like saying it? To the wind?"

"The wind can't read," said Hazza'.

I lifted my head off my paws and looked at the dunes. I'd seen the dunes write. I'd seen what they wrote back. I looked a long time, until Theyab followed my eyes, and went quiet.

He copied the four shapes, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. Then again. Then again. He was still at it long after Hazza' had fallen asleep, by firelight and then by starlight, until the patch of sand in front of him was covered edge to edge with his own name.

In the morning the wind had smoothed it all away.

Nearly all.

I found it while the boys still slept. Round the lip of the oldest well, far from the fire, where the old stone was soft enough to scratch. ذياب. Cut deep, over and over, in a ring all the way round the well-mouth, in his crooked new hand. The scratches were fresh. There was blood in some of them, from a split fingernail.

When he woke, the nail of his right forefinger was torn to the quick. He looked at it, puzzled, and sucked it, and didn't remember. I scraped sand into the letters until they were filled, and sat a long time with my nose pointed down the black throat of the well, wondering which of us it had been talking to in the night.

He wrote it again that evening, by the fire. Awake.

---

It went both ways. That's what I want you to understand about the two of them. Nobody was rescuing anybody. Every night Hazza' taught Theyab a few more letters, and every day Theyab taught Hazza' how not to die.

He showed him how to spread a cloth over a scrape in the sand before dawn so the dew would drip into a cup. Which roots held water, and which plants held poison, like the milky sap of the calotropis that blinds you. How to smell a shamal a day off, like an old coin. How to read a dune's long back and steep face, so even on a starless night your feet know which way the wind has blown all year. And more quietly: how to listen for the hiss of the sand to stop altogether, which means something under it is listening too.

"And when the dunes shift at night," Theyab said, "you don't follow the sand. The sand lies. You follow the stars. Al-Jadi up there in the north, that doesn't move. Al-Thurayya, the little bunch, like grapes. And that one." He pointed low into the south, to the great white star just clearing the dunes. "That's Suhail. The old men say when Suhail rises the worst heat breaks. Keep him on your—"

He stopped. Hazza' was looking at the star, and his face had gone very still.

"Suhail," Hazza' said.

"The stable boy," said Theyab softly. "I'm sorry. I didn't—"

"No." Hazza' kept looking at it. "No. I like that he's up there. I like that you navigate by him." He was quiet a long time. "Nobody ever taught me which one he was. I had six tutors. Not one of them taught me a single thing that would keep me alive."

"Nobody ever taught me either," said Theyab. "I just didn't die."

They looked at each other over the fire. Then Hazza' began to laugh, and after a moment so did Theyab.

*Yet*, I thought. *You just haven't died yet.* I kept it behind my teeth with all the rest. Behind my teeth is the only place I have.

---

On the tenth night, Hazza' reached into his saddlebag and brought out a small clay pot with a wax seal, and two handfuls of dates he'd shaken down from the palms.

"Sidr honey," he said, breaking the wax. "Wild bees in the Hajar. Cost me two jackals' worth of ash. Don't tell anyone, it ruins my reputation as a dangerous exile."

Theyab went very still.

"There was a cook in my father's kitchen," Hazza' went on, not noticing. "Umm Shamma. Enormous woman. Arms like a pearl-diver. She used to leave honey cakes cooling on the windowsill, and Zaid and I stole them every day for ten years, and she pretended every single day not to know, and shouted at the kitchen boys for it. I think she baked extra." He smiled down at the pot. "I think she knew exactly."

Theyab told him then about the cloth bundles left outside his patched tent by Umm Hanan, who never once spoke to him in the street and never once missed a week. About the dates rolled in honey he always saved for the hardest days, as if sweetness could be stored like water against a drought. How, on the day the village stoned him out, she'd pushed bread into his hands in front of everyone, and that bread had kept him alive across the sands.

Hazza' listened. Then he dipped a date in the pot and held it out.

"Then let's have it on an easy day," he said. "For once. See what that's like."

Theyab took it.

They ate the whole pot and argued about which star was brightest, and I lay between them with my legs stuck out stiff the way a saluki lies when it is shamelessly content, and I didn't growl once all night. It was the best night. I've measured every night since against it.

I counted that one. I counted all of them. The thin moon had gone and left the sky dark for a few nights, and now it was coming back, a little fuller every evening, like a cup being filled. Like a pyre being built, one stick at a time.

When Theyab told Hazza' that Old Bakhit had sent him to find Ghidd al-Zujaj, the glass ruins, Hazza' didn't even pretend to think about it.

"I'll come," he said. "I know roughly where it lies. My grandfather's falconers used to talk about it. And frankly, you need someone with you who can read the signs."

"I can read *some* letters now."

"You can read your own name, the dog's name and the word *jamal*." Hazza' grinned. "Camel. The most useful word in any language. I'm coming."

Not one hair on my back moved. He meant it entirely.

I looked up at the moon, filling. I couldn't have said anything if I'd wanted to. Out past the palms, under the great dune, the thing that liked to listen shifted in the sand, and settled, and was patient.

---

*End of Chapter Nine*
