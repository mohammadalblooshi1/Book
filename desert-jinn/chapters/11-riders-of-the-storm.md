# Chapter Eleven
## Riders of the Storm

> *Ride with the shamal, never across it. And if the wind says your name, do not answer.*
> — what every Bedouin father tells his son before the first sandstorm, and again after the last

---

The eighteenth night since the thin moon at Bir al-Ghina'. The moon was fat and white and getting fatter, and I'd stopped looking at it.

We'd left Ghidd al-Zujaj three days before, walking east toward the oases. Hazza' had decided that if the carvings didn't know a third way, somebody alive might. "Every old man between here and Buraimi has his own version of every story," he said. "One of them has to be the one where the boy lives."

Nobody answered. The rock had shown us eleven bearers cut into stone, and every one of them was a child. You don't argue with a wall like that. You just stop sleeping well.

Theyab walked. He found water. When Hazza' made jokes he laughed at them, a little too quickly, like a man paying a debt.

I kept my distance. I'd told one lie in that carved place, without a single word, and it had used up whatever it is in a dog that does the work of talking. Theyab hadn't mentioned it once. He still let me sleep against his side. Somehow that was worse.

On the morning of the eighteenth day, Theyab stopped on top of a dune and lifted his face to the air.

"Storm," he said.

Hazza' sniffed too. "I don't smell anything."

"Like an old thaler. Like a coin someone's been holding under his tongue." Theyab was looking north-west, where the sky along the edge of the world had gone the yellow of an old bruise, the kind that never quite heals. "Shamal. It's coming fast." He frowned. "And something else."

I'd smelled it for an hour and hoped I was wrong. Smoke without fire. Ash without heat. Burning cloth a long way off, and under it the sweet, flat smell of a room where somebody has been dead for days and nobody has opened the door.

Al-Ghasaq. The grey.

From the dunes behind us came a snarl like tearing cloth.

---

They came over the ridge to the west in a long grey line.

Jinn-jackals first: eight, ten, more, running low, with darkness streaming off their backs like smoke off a wet fire and their eyes burning like coals blown on. They don't pant. A hunting dog notices that. They run and run and never breathe, because there is nothing inside them that needs to.

Behind the jackals, on camels, came men.

Racing camels, the thin-legged kind the sheikhs breed for wagers, running with their heads high and their mouths open, roaring. The riders sat them light and forward, as if they weighed nothing at all.

The riders wore grey robes. Their faces were smooth white masks with nothing on them: no eyes, no mouth, no nose. A blank white oval, like the inside of a skull scraped clean. Every mask was pointed the same way, at the same boy, the way a falcon's head points at the lure.

Each one had a blade at his hip that didn't shine. It was grey, the flat, swallowed grey of a sky with nothing behind it. Steel quenched in the Ifrit's ash.

The Nameless.

Twelve of them. Theyab counted under his breath, and I watched the colour go out of him number by number.

They were humming. No words, no tune, rising and falling with the camels' strides, and it came across the sand without getting any quieter, the way a sound does in a dream.

"Friends of yours?" said Hazza'.

"They hunt me."

"Of course they do." Hazza' drew his broken sword. "Twelve of them, ten jackals, two of us and one bad-tempered dog. I like those odds."

From somewhere in Theyab's pack, muffled, came a small *ting*.

Old Bakhit's bell. It does not ring for silence. It rings for liars.

"I don't like those odds," Hazza' admitted. "Run."

---

Nobody outruns a racing camel on foot. Not even me. By the time the boys had stumbled down into the next hollow, the first rider was cresting the dune behind them, his camel sliding down the slip-face on its haunches in a hiss of sand.

Hazza' turned, planted his feet, and waited.

The rider came at him with the grey blade swinging low. Hazza' stepped *toward* it, which is not what anyone sensible does. He ducked under the swing, caught the rider's sleeve, and *pulled*, and the masked man went over the camel's shoulder into the sand. Hazza' was up behind the hump before the beast had finished roaring about it.

The man he'd thrown didn't get up straight away. He lay on his back in the sand with that blank white face turned to the sky, and from behind the mask, in a young man's voice, he said quite clearly:

"*Tell my mother I'll be home before the coffee's cold.*"

Then he got up, like a puppet lifted by the back of its robe, and walked toward Theyab.

"Theyab!" Hazza' wrenched the camel's head round by its halter rope. "*Come on!*"

A second camel had come down the slope. Its rider was turning to slash at Hazza's back. Theyab ran at it, ducked the long yellow teeth, grabbed the rider's ankle with both hands, and hauled. The masked man fell without a sound. Theyab scrambled up into the saddle in his place.

The camel went mad underneath him, roaring and spinning, trying to bite his knee. He grabbed the saddle horn and the rope together and clamped his legs. I took three strides and jumped. The Bedouin carry salukis on camelback across deep sand, so our feet are fresh for the hunt; I've ridden that way for longer than anyone has been writing it down. I landed behind him, scrabbled, and wedged myself against his back, and he didn't even yell at me.

Hazza' was already racing away. Theyab's camel saw its companion go and decided, as camels do, that wherever that was looked better than here.

It ran.

---

The dunes went past in a blur. Behind us came the Nameless, a grey tide pouring over every ridge, and under the drum of padded feet, steady and unhurried, that humming. Ahead and to our right, the sky had gone black, and the shamal was coming down on us like a wall falling over.

"*Right!*" Hazza' shouted. "We go right, around it—"

"*No!*" Theyab's voice cracked. "*With it! Ride with it!*"

"*What?*"

"*It's coming out of the north-west! Turn across it and it'll knock you flat! Go where it's going!*"

I'll give Hazza' this. He'd spent most of a month learning from Theyab how not to die, and he didn't waste a breath. He hauled his camel's head round until the storm was at its tail, south and east, the way the wind was running. Theyab followed.

And the storm swallowed us.

---

The world went brown. Then it went dark.

Sand everywhere, roaring like the sea off the pearling banks, so thick Theyab couldn't see his own hands. He pulled his ghutra across his face and rode blind. I pressed myself flat against his back, my nose buried in the cloth of his shirt, and felt his heart going like a trapped bird's.

And in the wind, there were voices.

The old men say it's only the sand singing. But I know the difference between the sand singing and the sand *talking*. Dozens of voices, faint and close at our ears, worn smooth with repeating:

*Is that you? Is someone there? I can't see. I can't see. Where did everyone go?*

And then, under all of them, close enough that I felt Theyab go rigid:

*Theyab.*

Not shouted. Said. Gently. The way someone says your name when they've found you after a long time looking.

Every hair along my spine stood up. When something lies, my hackles rise and a growl starts in my chest before I've decided anything at all, and that voice was a lie from end to end, *especially* the kindness in it. I growled into his back, low and long, so he'd feel it through his ribs.

He didn't answer. I felt him bite down on it.

Behind us, the Nameless tried to cut across.

I heard it happen. A camel bellowed as its rider turned it sideways to the wind, and the shamal caught it like a sail and rolled it, rider and saddle and all, away into the dark. Then another. Then a jinn-jackal howled, a howl like nothing I'd heard from them before, as the wind tore the shadow off it in strips and there turned out to be nothing underneath.

The riders didn't scream when they went. They just kept humming, right up until the storm took the sound away.

One rider was still with us, close behind, running in the lee of our camels. Theyab didn't see him until the grey blade came out of the brown, low and fast.

He twisted. It missed his leg and caught his forearm, a shallow cut along the old golden cracks.

But for one long heartbeat, Theyab couldn't remember what honey tasted like.

He knew he'd eaten it. But the sweetness itself was gone, cut out clean, and where it had been was a cold blank with smooth edges, like the place a tooth used to be.

Then it seeped back, slowly. Mostly. He gasped.

I don't think it all came back. It never does. The ash-quenched blades don't cut flesh so much as they cut *you*, and you heal the way a dune heals over a footprint: smooth on top, and something missing underneath that you'll never be able to point to.

Hazza's camel came round out of the storm, and the broken sword came down, and the rider behind us was gone. His mask spun away into the dark, still humming.

---

We came out of the worst of it into a place of stone.

Ruins, older than anything Theyab had ever dug in. A long road ran between rows of carved pillars, half sunk in sand, half fallen, like the ribs of something that had died on its back. There were faces cut into the pillars, and every one had had its eyes chiselled out. Carefully, all the way down the road.

The camels didn't like it.

Hazza's stopped so suddenly he went over its neck. Theyab's dropped to its knees and threw us both, and by the time Theyab had rolled to his feet both camels were gone into the brown, halter ropes flying. They'd rather the storm. Animals are wise about places.

"Well," said Hazza', spitting out sand. "That's gratitude."

Then the ground shook.

One of the great pillars beside him had been leaning for a thousand years. The shamal had been leaning on it for an hour. Now, with a long groan like the wall in the old ruins on the morning this story began, it gave up.

"*Hazza'!*"

He threw himself sideways. He almost made it.

The pillar came down across his legs with a sound like the end of the world. Hazza' didn't scream. He made a short, punched-out noise, as if every bit of air had been knocked out of him, and then he lay very still, face-down from the knees under a column of carved stone.

"I'm all right," he said into the sand. His voice was thin. "Nothing's broken. I think nothing's broken. It's just *very heavy*."

Theyab got his shoulder under the edge and heaved until the cracks on his arms flared, and the stone didn't so much as tremble. It weighed as much as a house. It weighed as much as a life.

I dug. It's what my kind does when something we love is under something. I tore at the sand beside Hazza's hip until my claws split, and the sand poured back in as fast as I threw it out.

From the storm behind us, out of the dark between the pillars, came a snarl like tearing cloth.

---

Six jackals. Four masked riders on foot, camels lost, grey blades drawn. They came out of the swirling brown slowly, because they could see there was no hurry. A boy who couldn't move a stone. A sheikh's son who couldn't move at all. A dog with bleeding feet.

The riders stopped in a line. They tilted their blank white faces, all four of them, to exactly the same angle at exactly the same moment.

"*Is that you?*" said one, in an old woman's voice.

"*Before the coffee's cold,*" said another.

"*Don't let it in,*" said the third. "*Don't let it in. Don't let it in.*"

The fourth said nothing. The fourth had a child's voice, I learned later, and had nothing left to say at all.

Theyab reached for the Light. It came grey and thin and sputtering, the way it had with the jackals at the well, because he wasn't thinking about anyone else now. Terror filled him to the brim and left no room for anything gold.

"Theyab," said Hazza' from the sand. "Go. *Go.* Ride the storm out. You know how. Leave me—"

"*No.*"

The first jackal sprang.

And I stopped being only a dog.

---

I'll tell you how it feels, because nobody has ever asked me. Nobody could.

It feels like drowning in reverse. Something cold and enormous that has been folded small inside me for a very long time begins to *unfold*, and it doesn't care what it breaks on the way out.

My silver eye goes first, the clouded one the Bedouin mutter about. It doesn't burn; it *opens*, wider than an eye should, and behind it there's no dog at all. Then the dark comes up out of the place where half of me used to be. It isn't a part of me. It's a part of *it*, a piece of the old curse I swallowed long ago, dragging someone out of it. It has always been patient.

My body didn't grow. That's the part that makes the men who've glimpsed it wake screaming years later. I stayed a saluki, lean and sand-coloured, ears flat to my skull, four bleeding feet planted in the sand.

It was my *shadow* that grew.

It poured out from under me the wrong way, toward the light instead of away from it, up the pillars, up the storm. Long, too long, a dog's shape stretched past anything a dog should be, ears like spearheads among the broken capitals, a jaw that opened and kept opening, row on row of teeth in it. It didn't lie on the ground the way a shadow does. It *stood*. The black that was there before anything was lit, pouring upward like smoke going back into a fire. And where its eyes should be, two coals.

And I could hear them. The ones inside the dark. Every one of the emptied the grey has ever eaten, all the last words of everyone who has ever been forgotten, rushing through me like wind through a ruined house. *Is someone there. Where did everyone go. Don't let it in.*

One voice under the others, quiet and familiar, saying a name I won't repeat.

The jackal that had sprung at Theyab met my shadow in mid-air and simply came apart, like fog walking into a wall.

I put my shoulder to the fallen pillar, a dog's shoulder, thin as a boy's wrist. I *pushed*, and the strength that came wasn't mine. It came up out of the dark through my legs, and the stone rose, the whole length of it, grinding off Hazza's legs the way you'd lift a stick off a sleeping child. I heard my own bones creak, and something under them that wasn't bone.

Hazza' dragged himself out on his elbows, and I heard him make a sound when he looked up past me and saw what was standing over him on the wall of the storm. It was not a brave sound. I don't blame him.

And then, because I was angry, and because the jinn part of me is always angry, I threw it.

It went end over end through the swirling sand and came down on the four masked men with a sound I won't describe to you. I'll only tell you that the humming stopped. All four voices at once, mid-note, the way a door cuts off a room.

The other jackals turned to run. I caught two. I didn't need my teeth. My shadow's mouth closed over them where they ran, and they went into the dark inside me like water into sand, and the dark was glad. That's the part I've never told anyone. *It was glad.*

Then it was quiet, except for the wind and my own breath, which was not a dog's breath. It frosted the sand at my feet.

I turned round. A thin old saluki standing in a ring of frost, and behind him and above him, rising off him like a tent pole holds up a tent, the shape of something with ember eyes and its long jaw still working on nothing. I looked at the boy I'd spent fifteen years protecting.

---

I've hidden that shape from every bearer I ever walked with. All eleven. Not one of them ever saw it.

I told myself it was to keep them from being afraid of me.

It was really to keep me from seeing their faces when they were.

Theyab's face, looking up at me in that storm, was the worst thing I've ever seen. And I have seen eleven children burn.

He didn't see me. Not the dog. All he saw was a jinn standing over Hazza' with its mouth full of teeth, the biggest thing of the grey he'd seen since the zawba'a took the caravan. And somewhere in all the voices pouring out of me, one said his name. I didn't mean it to. I don't know whose voice it used. All he felt was fear, pure and total, filling him like water filling a well, all the way to the top and over.

"*Get away from him!*"

The Light came out of him like the end of the world.

It wasn't gold. It wasn't grey. It was both, twisted together, pouring out of his hands and his chest and his eyes in a raging white torrent, gold shot through with grey like smoke through fire. He didn't aim it. He couldn't have. It *burst* out of him, and it hit my shadow full in the chest, and my shadow was me.

Light and jinn don't mix. Nothing in the world burns the dark the way that Light does.

I howled.

Salukis are a quiet kind. I had never howled like that in all my years. It came out of me broken and many-throated, every voice inside me screaming at once, and went ringing off the pillars and up into the storm. The Light tore into the jinn half of me like fire into dry grass, and for one moment I felt the dark in me *afraid*, which I had never felt and hope never to feel again. It burned my silver eye. It burned down into the cold place where the dark lived, looking for the bottom, and it *hurt*. More than the curse did, the first time it took me. More than anything.

I fell.

And as I fell, the shadow fell with me, running back under my feet like water out of a broken jar, and the voices went with it, the quiet familiar one last of all. By the time I hit the sand there was only a dog, scorched and smoking down one flank, my silver eye squeezed shut and weeping something that wasn't tears, and a shadow the ordinary size, lying the ordinary way.

The light went out of Theyab all at once. He stood there swaying. His arms were cracked gold from wrist to shoulder.

"Ghareeb?" he whispered.

---

The vision came the moment the Light went out, the way it always did now, like a debt being collected. Longer this time. Clearer. Closer.

The full moon, so near he could see the grey stains on its face like old thumbprints. The great dune of Tal Moreeb beneath his feet, its crest a knife-edge of sand. The fire climbing his arms, and this time he could *feel* it: not pain yet, but heat, rising, the way the air rises off a coffee-roasting pan just before you touch it. Somebody below him on the dune, shouting. And for the first time, the sand under his bare feet, cold as a grave, and how the cold and the heat met somewhere around his knees.

Then it let him go, and he was on his knees in the ruins, and he could still smell his own hair burning. He touched it. It wasn't. He touched it again anyway.

"Ghareeb," he said. "*Ghareeb.* I didn't know— I didn't— Ghareeb, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry—"

He crawled toward me with his hands out.

I turned my whole face to the sand, so he couldn't see the eye and I couldn't see his. If I'd had a voice for him, I'd have said: *don't look at me.*

I turned away from him for a long time after that.

---

The storm blew itself out by nightfall.

They made camp among the pillars, because Hazza' couldn't walk far; his legs were bruised black from the knee down, but by some mercy nothing was broken. Theyab built a fire from a dead calotropis and its dry pods, and turned the nearest stone face toward the dunes so its empty sockets wouldn't watch us. Then he gathered me into his lap, scorched side up, and curled himself around me, and I let him, because I had no strength left to get up and go.

He kept saying he was sorry into my fur until the words stopped sounding like words, and even asleep his hands kept checking that I was still there.

I lay in his arms with my good eye half open, and I watched Hazza'.

Hazza' didn't sleep either.

He waited until Theyab's breathing had gone slow, then limped to the fallen pillar to see if any of them were alive. Hazza' was like that, even with them.

One of them was. Barely.

He lay apart from the others, half under the stone. His mask had cracked down the middle, and Hazza' had to pull at it. It had grown to his face at the edges, the way bark grows over a nail driven into a ghaf tree. Under it was a face thin and grey and frightened. Not much older than Hazza'.

His lips moved. Even I couldn't hear. Hazza' bent down close. Whatever he said, it took all he had, and then his lips stopped, and his eyes stayed open on the moon.

Hazza' knelt there a long while. Then he reached into the dead man's robe and drew something out.

A letter. Folded small and sealed with grey wax.

He held it up to the moon and went still. He'd seen that seal before, at the bottom of the order that sent him out of Ubar with half a sword. The personal seal of the Wazir Muhaisen.

He broke it and read.

I'll tell you what it said, because you'll need to know, and because Theyab couldn't have read it then even if he'd seen it. It was signed *The Voice*. Soft, careful handwriting, every letter perfectly formed, like the hand of someone who has never once been in a hurry. It gave orders. On the Night of Lamps, when every lamp in Ubar was lit for the lost bearers, Sheikh Mutlaq was to die.

Hazza' read it twice. His hand was shaking.

Then he folded it very small. He twisted the brass pommel off the hilt of his broken sword and slid the letter into the hollow where, in the old swords of Ubar, a smith would roll up a written blessing for the man who carried it. He screwed the pommel back on, tight.

He came back to the fire and sat beside us with the sword across his knees. He looked at Theyab asleep, the gold still glowing faintly up both arms. He looked at me, and I didn't pretend to be asleep, and he didn't pretend he hadn't seen what had stood over him that afternoon.

He didn't wake Theyab. He didn't tell him.

I think he meant to. I think he wanted Theyab, for one more night, to carry only one terrible thing instead of two.

We were a fine pair, the sheikh's son and I. Two keepers of secrets, sitting up in the dark among blind stone faces, watching over the same sleeping boy.

Above us the moon hung nearly full, white and patient, like an eye that has decided not to blink.

---

*End of Chapter Eleven*
