# Chapter Three
## Monster

> *Beware the child who glows; the desert has plans for him, and the desert is always hungry.*
> — a saying of the grandmothers of Liwa, told to children who stay out after the sunset call of the doves

---

Nadim ran first.

I'll give him that. Whatever else he did, before and after, Nadim was the first one into the grey that night.

"*Shibl!*"

The grey swallowed the name. Sound doesn't carry in the Ifrit's curse. You shout, and the shout leaves your mouth and simply stops, like a stone dropped into wet date-paste. Nadim shouted his brother's name three times, and each time it died a hand's width from his lips.

And each time, somewhere deeper in, something said it back to him. Softly. Getting it a little more right each time.

Theyab was on his feet before he knew it. His legs were shaking. His palms still burned where the golden cracks ran across them. He looked at the flat dead place where the lane stopped being a lane, and took one step toward it.

I took his sleeve in my teeth.

Gently. I have a mouth made for carrying a hare back to the tent without bruising it. I held his sleeve and set my weight back on my haunches, and I looked up at him, and if you have ever been looked at by a saluki you know we can say a great deal with our eyes when we bother.

*No. You're half dead on your feet, and that is the curse itself. Not a story. You walk in there tonight and something else might walk out wearing you.*

He looked down at me. "He's seven," he said.

I let go of his sleeve.

He went in after Nadim, and I went in after him, because apparently that's what I am.

---

The grey feels like nothing.

It's the feeling of a tent after everyone you love has packed up and ridden away in the night without waking you, spread over the whole world. The colour goes first. Then the sound. Then the smells, and for me that is like going blind and deaf at once. Then, if you stay long enough, the reasons you came. It doesn't eat me. But every time I come out of it, I'm a little less sure which of my memories are mine.

Theyab walked with his hand flat against his chest, and the feather under his palm was the only warm thing for a hundred paces in any direction. Every few steps he whispered something, too low for the grey to steal.

*The sand remembers. The sand remembers.*

There were emptied in there. Standing between the grey houses like tent-pegs, faces turned up toward a sky with no stars left in it. As we passed, their heads came round, slowly, one after another, following the warmth of the mark. They smiled their wrong smiles and murmured their last sentences into the dead air. One of them was a boy no older than Shibl. He was saying, *"I'm not tired, I'm not tired, I'm not tired."*

Theyab didn't look at him. I was proud of that, and sick about it.

We found Nadim on his knees at the end of the lane, near the dead grey palms of the grove.

Shibl was sitting in front of him in the sand, cross-legged, the way small children sit when they've been told to wait and haven't been told for how long. Four of the emptied stood around him in a neat square, one at each corner, facing inward. Keeping him. The way you'd pen a lamb with four stakes and a rope.

He wasn't crying any more. That was what frightened me most. His face had gone the colour of old ash, and his eyes were open and calm and terribly white.

Nadim had him by the shoulders, shaking him gently, desperately.

"Shibl. Shibl, it's me. Look at me. *Look* at me."

Shibl looked at him. Politely, the way you'd look at a stranger who had stopped you in the souq at Buraimi.

"Who are you?" he said.

And around him, the four emptied said it too, in four different voices, at exactly the same moment. *"Who are you?"*

Nadim made a sound I've heard many times and still have no word for. It's the sound of something tearing that isn't cloth.

---

They got him out of the grey between them. Nadim carried him and Theyab walked behind, his glowing palms held up like lamps, and the grey drew back from them a little. The four emptied didn't follow. They stayed exactly where they were, standing around the empty square of sand, facing inward at nothing. I looked back once. They were still guarding it.

Saqi the well-keeper was waiting at the edge with a lamp. He took one look at his younger son's face and sat down in the dirt as if his legs had been cut from under him. Behind him people were gathering: Sheikh Mazyad, Ghanim with a camel-stick, neighbours clutching their shawls around their faces, men with old matchlocks they hadn't fired since the last raid and didn't know how to load in the dark.

Nadim laid his brother on the hard ground outside the house.

Then he turned and looked at Theyab.

It wasn't hatred. It was the look of a man at the bottom of a dry well, seeing a rope come down and hating that it was this particular rope.

"You did something," Nadim said hoarsely. "Before. You did something with the light and that woman's eyes went *brown*. I saw it."

Theyab said nothing.

"Do it again."

"I don't know how."

It was true. I stood at Theyab's side with every hair on my back lying flat, and I'd have given my silver eye for Nadim to know what that meant. He didn't. But he saw something in Theyab's face, and something in his own face crumpled.

"Please," said Nadim.

I don't think he'd ever said that word to Theyab before. It came out of him rusty and broken, like a well-wheel that hasn't turned in years.

"Please. He's my brother. *Please.*"

Theyab knelt.

---

I want to tell you he laid his hands on that child like the heroes in Bakhit's stories. He didn't. His hands were shaking so badly that when he set them on either side of Shibl's face, the boy's head rocked. He was thinking of the emptied, and the grey, and forty people watching him from behind their lamps and their rifles. He was thinking about the word *jinn*, and what they'd do with it. He was thinking about himself.

So the light came grey.

It leaked out of his palms thin and sickly, the colour of the thing he was trying to fight. It lay over Shibl's face like dirty water. And Shibl, under it, smiled. Slowly. Too wide. The smile of the emptied, on a seven-year-old's mouth.

Theyab snatched his hands back as if he'd been bitten.

"I can't," he whispered. "I can't, I'm making it worse, it won't—"

I couldn't tell him. I've never been able to tell any of them. So I did what a dog does. I shoved my long nose up under his chin, hard, so his head came up, and I turned my muzzle and pushed, and pushed, until his face was turned away from the crowd and the lamps and the rifles, and there was nothing in front of his eyes but the child.

*Not them. They're not the ones who need you. Him.*

He looked.

And I watched it happen for the second time that night. I watched him stop being a frightened boy surrounded by people who hated him, and become a boy looking at a child who had forgotten his own name. A child who was, in that moment, exactly as alone as Theyab had been every day of his life.

The light went gold.

---

It came up out of him gently this time, softer and warmer than the blaze in the lane. It flowed from his chest down his arms and into Shibl's face like honey poured into warm milk, and the grey began to lift.

And Theyab saw.

He saw a baby born in the killing heat of the summer, red-faced and furious, while a boy of eight pressed his nose to the doorframe. He saw that same boy carrying the baby on his shoulders to the top of the dune behind the well, pointing up at the night and naming the stars the way the Bedouin name them. *That one's Suhail. When he comes up, the heat breaks. Those are al-Thurayya, the sisters. That one's the camel, see, and the little one following it is the calf. That's you. The little one, right next to me.* He saw Nadim sneaking him extra bread, and sitting alone behind the house one night after their father had beaten him, crying silently into his knees where nobody could see.

Nobody but the small boy watching from the window.

Then the pictures turned and ran forward. Shibl taller, with his brother's shoulders, laughing across a fire. Shibl's hands, older, lifting an oil lamp. Blurred, like a reflection in the well when someone drops a stone. The future always is. But warm. Full of colour.

And then, behind all of it, the other vision.

It didn't wait its turn this time. It came up through Shibl's future like a hand coming up through water and took hold of him.

The moon, enormous above him, so close it filled half the sky, pale and pitted like a skull left out on the sands. Sand beneath his own bare feet, higher than any dune he'd ever climbed, falling away on every side into the dark. His own arms, opening; he couldn't stop them opening. Light running out of the feather on his chest, cracking him from the inside, and this time he felt it. Not a beautiful heat. A real one. His skin tightening across his ribs like a drum-hide drying in the sun. His lips splitting. The wet in his eyes boiling, and the world going white and staying white. The smell of honey, and hot metal, and hair, and now, under it, the smell of meat on a spit.

He tried to scream and found that the fire had already taken that.

Somewhere below, someone was shouting.

He couldn't hear what. He thought, wildly, through it, that it might be his name. Then he thought: *which one?*

---

Shibl gasped.

A huge, ragged breath, the breath of a pearl-diver coming up from the bottom with his lungs on fire. The colour flooded back into his face, brown and alive. His eyes cleared, white to grey to the ordinary dark brown of a well-keeper's son.

He blinked up at the sky.

Then he saw Theyab.

I understand why he screamed. Imagine you're seven, and you've just come back from the place where there is nothing, not even yourself. The first thing you see is a face bent over yours, and the face is burning. Golden cracks running up its neck and across its cheeks like a clay jar about to shatter in the kiln. Light pouring out of its eyes like tears. Its mouth open in a scream with no sound in it. And beside it, a long pale dog that walked into the grey and came out again, staring at you with one amber eye and one silver.

Shibl screamed for both of us. He twisted out from under Theyab's hands and scrambled backward across the sand until he hit his brother's legs, and he clung to them and kept screaming.

Theyab's light snapped out like a blown lamp.

He knelt there with his hands still raised, slapping at his own arms, his own face, putting out a fire that had already gone. Tears were running down his cheeks. Some were his and some belonged to the vision, and he couldn't have told you which.

And across Shibl's cheeks, where Theyab's fingers had been, lay two thin golden lines, faintly shining.

Nadim saw them.

He looked down at his brother, clinging and sobbing, with gold scored across his face like a brand. He looked at Theyab, cracked and glowing and weeping light. He looked at me, the dog that had howled with a dead man's voice.

He was fifteen. His brother had forgotten him and been given back, and the price was written in gold across that small face, and Nadim understood none of it. He'd been taught his whole life to fear the strange thing at the edge of the village. That's what fear does, in the end. It reaches for the oldest word it knows.

"*Monster!*"

It cracked out of him like a camel-whip.

"Get away from him! You *monster*, you jinni's filth, get away from my brother, get *away*—"

---

It went into Theyab the way it always did. Like a hook.

He flinched. Just a flinch, as if he'd been slapped. Then he said, quietly, the way he'd said it at the well, the way he'd said it his whole life:

"I'm not a monster."

My hackles lay flat as a sleeping cat. Nobody was looking at them.

They were looking at the light still flickering in the cracks of his skin. And somewhere out in the dark, at the edge of hearing, something howled. Not me. A long thin sound out in the dunes that rose and broke like a child crying itself out, and was answered by another, and another. Jackals, you'd think, if you'd never heard a jackal. I have heard ten thousand jackals. These were not jackals.

"He's calling them," said Ghanim. His camel-stick trembled in his fist. "You hear that? Him and the dog. They're *calling* them."

"The grey came when he did," said a woman behind him. "Two nights ago. I saw him. Up on the dune. He was saying something to the wind."

A murmur ran through the crowd: the ugly sound of frightened people discovering they all agreed.

Theyab's hand went to his chest.

Because she was right. He had whispered his name to the wind, and the wind had whispered it back, and the grey had come. Every denial that came to his tongue, the feather burned before it reached his lips.

So he said nothing. And to the people of Hilat al-Sarab, his silence sounded exactly like guilt.

"Sheikh," said Ghanim.

Sheikh Mazyad looked at Theyab a long moment. I'll say this for Mazyad: he didn't enjoy it. He had the hopeless face of a man who has decided to feed one thing to the dark so the dark will leave the rest alone. Villages have been making that bargain since before there were villages. It has never once worked.

"Get out, Laqeet," he said. "Take your jinn-dog and get out of Hilat al-Sarab, and don't come back. You have no guest-right here. You never had."

The first stone came from somewhere at the back of the crowd.

---

It hit Theyab's shoulder. Not hard. A test.

He didn't move. He was still on his knees, looking at Shibl, who had stopped screaming and was staring at him over his brother's leg with huge wet eyes.

The second stone hit his ribs, and the third his cheek, and after that I lost count.

I barked at him. I barked the hunting bark, the one that means *now, now, it's running, go*, and I seized his shirt and hauled, and when he still didn't move I bit his hand. Not hard. Hard enough.

He got up. He didn't run. He walked, backward at first, then turned and walked away through the crowd with stones coming at him from every side. The crowd parted, then closed behind him, the way a fist closes. A rock struck his back just below the left shoulder blade. I heard the sound it made. It hit within a finger's width of the old scar, the one he'd carried since he was six.

He staggered. He kept walking.

He never raised his hands. Not once. I want you to know that.

A man lunged for Theyab with a camel-stick raised over his head like a sword. I took him by the ankle. To the bone. He went down howling, and I let go and kept walking, and I'm not ashamed of it, and I could smell from the blood whose ankle it was. I won't tell you. He has grandchildren. Some of them may be at this fire.

---

At the edge of the village, where the last houses gave way to the gravel, there was a small mud house with a baking-plate and a clay oven behind it.

The door opened as Theyab passed.

Umm Hanan came out. She was very small, with a face folded like a dried fig, and the black burqa'a of an old woman across her nose, and her hands still white with flour at midnight because she could not sleep and did not know what else to do with them. She didn't look at the crowd.

She walked straight up to Theyab and pressed something into his hands.

Flatbread, still warm from the plate, a thick round of it, and a fistful of dates, all wrapped in a square of faded indigo cloth knotted at the top.

He knew that cloth. He stared down at it, then up at her, and I watched him understand. Fifteen years of bundles with no footprints leading to them.

"Eat, child," said Umm Hanan. Her voice was dry and thin and fierce. "The sand remembers kindness too."

I smelled no lie on her. I've never smelled a lie on her, in all the years. Only flour, and woodsmoke, and old grief, and under it something fierce and frightened that I'd call courage if I were a man and had words for things.

Then she went back inside and shut the door. I imagine she sat on her mat and shook for a long time. I imagine she tried to say the names of her dead, too, and found only two of them, and said them twice.

Theyab stood there holding the bread.

It was the first time in his life anyone in Hilat al-Sarab had touched him gently. He stood at the edge of the desert, bleeding from a dozen places, holding that bread against his chest as if it were a sack of silver thalers.

I pressed against his legs and leaned, the way a saluki leans, all our weight on you at once. *Come on. We can't stay.*

He walked out into the dunes. Behind us the lamps of Hilat al-Sarab went out one by one, as if the village had closed its eyes. Or as if something were walking from house to house, pinching the wicks.

I looked back once more. One lamp stayed lit. Small, low, in the window of the little mud house with the oven behind it.

---

The jackals found us before midnight.

I heard them first. Well, of course I did. A low sound under the wind, like breath drawn through teeth. Then a howl, close, from behind the dune to our left. Then another, ahead. Then one directly behind us, so near I felt it in the feathering of my tail.

And no smell. That's how I knew. A pack of jackals smells like a dead camel that has been lying in the sun for three days. These smelled like the bottom of a cold well.

"Ghareeb," Theyab whispered. He had never said my name like that before. He'd only heard Bakhit say it.

I pressed against his leg. *Keep walking. Don't run. Running makes them happy, and you don't want to see them happy.*

They came over the dune like smoke poured from a jug.

Jinn-jackals. Six of them, shaped like the golden jackals of the wadis, if a jackal were made of the darkness between the stars. Long and low, their bodies fraying into black mist at the legs and tail, too many ribs showing through the dark of their sides. Their eyes were coals, burning, the only colour in them. They spread out in a slow circle around us, and the sand didn't dent under their feet, and the air inside the circle went cold enough that Theyab's breath came out white, there in the hot months, on the edge of Liwa.

They track the Light. Every time a bearer heals, it rings out across the desert like a bell on a lead camel, and the jinn-jackals lift their heads and follow. Theyab had healed a child three hours ago. He might as well have lit a signal fire on Tal Moreeb and sung.

He pulled his rusty knife. His other hand went to his chest, and he tried, I watched him try, to call the light.

It came grey. A sputter in the cracks of his palms, and then nothing. He was too frightened, too full of stones and the word *monster*. He'd just watched himself burn. Nobody reaches eagerly for the thing that's going to kill them.

The jackals saw it falter, and their coal-eyes brightened.

The first one sprang.

Theyab slashed at it. The knife passed through its shoulder like a hand through smoke, and the jackal hit him full in the chest and knocked him flat. Its jaws opened above his face. There was no mouth inside. No teeth, no tongue, no throat. Only more dark, going down and down, much farther than the jackal was long, and a cold breathing up out of it like the air from the bottom of a dry well where something has been lying a long time.

And at the bottom of that dark, very small and far away, I heard a voice. A child's voice. *"Who am I?"*

I hit it from the side.

I'm a saluki. My grandmothers' grandmothers ran down gazelle on the gravel plains for men who wore swords. I can take a jackal by the throat at a full run and break its neck with one shake. I took this one by the throat.

My teeth closed on something cold and slippery that wasn't there, and the jinn-jackal twisted in my jaws like a snake, and shook me off with a flick of its head, and I went tumbling down the slope.

Rolling through the sand, I felt something stir in the silver half of me. Something huge and hungry uncoiling, stretching, tasting the cold in the air the way you'd taste a meal you'd eaten many times. It liked the jackals. It recognised them, the way you'd recognise cousins. My shadow, thrown long across the sand by the starlight, was for one moment longer than it should have been, and the wrong shape.

I didn't let it out. Not that night. That's a story for later, and you won't like it, and I like it even less.

As it turned out, I didn't need to.

Because that was when the spear came out of the dark.

---

One moment the jinn-jackal was crouched over Theyab's chest with its jaws spread. The next, there was a long shaft of pale wood standing straight up out of its back, and the jackal was coming apart.

It didn't bleed. Jinn things don't. It crumbled into a drift of black ash that fell over Theyab's face and chest like soot from a cooking pot, and it was cold, and it smelled, at last, of something: a lamp that has burned out and been left in a tomb.

Then the hoofbeats.

Not hoofbeats. A camel at the gallop doesn't drum. It thuds, soft and enormous, the great padded feet striking the sand, a rhythm I felt through my paws before I heard it. A camel came over the crest of the dune at a full run. It was pale, almost white in the starlight, a racing camel from the Omani strains, long-legged and narrow, like something carved from bone. On its back rode a figure wrapped from head to foot in grey cloth. There was no face to see. Only the wrappings, and a headcloth wound into a veil, and a dark slit where the eyes should be.

The rider leaned from the saddle, pulled the spear from the sand in one smooth movement, and drove it through the second jackal without slowing. The third leapt at the camel's flank. The rider took it out of the air.

The last three ran, their coal-eyes winking out one by one in the dark.

---

The pale camel halted at the top of the dune perhaps twenty paces away. It blew through its nose and shifted its feet.

The rider looked down at us.

Theyab was sitting up in the sand, black ash all over his chest, knife still clutched in his fist. In the dark, the golden cracks along his jaw were faintly glowing.

The rider didn't move or speak. But I could feel the weight of that look from where I lay, and it wasn't a hunter's look. It went on a long time. Longer than it should have. The spear-hand, I noticed, was not quite steady.

The wind was coming down off the dune toward me. I put my nose up into it.

I'm not going to tell you what I smelled. Not yet.

"Who are you?" Theyab called.

The wind answered him. Nothing else did.

Then the rider turned the camel and rode away north into the dark, the long spear held upright like a war-banner. Soon there was nothing left but three drifts of cold black ash and a line of broad camel tracks the dunes were already filling.

"Who was that?" said Theyab.

I licked the ash off my foreleg and said nothing. That part was easy. I always say nothing.

Who the rider was is a story for its proper time. If I tell it now you'll skip ahead, and then where will we be? Be patient. He was. Fifteen years, he was patient. Patience is just fear that has learned to sit still.

---

Theyab sat in the sand a long time after that, holding Umm Hanan's bread in his lap.

The golden cracks on his face and arms were fading slowly, the way colour goes out of a coal. His back was bleeding where the stone had hit. Every so often he turned his hands over, checking they were still skin.

He looked back toward Hilat al-Sarab. At its eastern edge the grey lay over the houses like a shroud over a man laid out for washing. Two lights still burned in that whole black huddle of mud and palm frond. One was the small lamp in the window of the house with the oven. The other was out at the far western edge, a single stubborn glow through the black goat-hair walls of a tent hung with herbs and bells.

Bakhit was awake.

Theyab looked at that light for a long time.

"I saw it," he said at last. He wasn't talking to me. He was saying it to the dark. "When I healed him. I saw myself. On a dune. I was burning, and I couldn't stop it, and I couldn't scream." His voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it. "All those stories. The Phoenix-Born. He *knew*."

I couldn't answer that. But I came and lay down against him, along the length of his leg, with my head on his knee, and every hair on my body lay flat and still in the cold.

He looked down at me. He'd been learning, these last few hours, what my hackles meant. He understood. I watched him understand.

Theyab stood up. He tucked the bread inside his shirt, against the feather mark, where it was warm. He picked up his knife.

Then he started walking back toward the light. Toward it. They always do. That's the whole trouble with them.

---

*End of Chapter Three*
