# Chapter Eleven
## Never Walk Into the Grey After a Child

> *The Grey keeps what it's given and counts what it keeps.*
> — words worn into the lip of an old well, half under the sand, south of Hilat al-Sarab

---

I heard him before anyone.

I was lying across the mouth of Theyab's tent with my nose on my paws and one ear turned east, the way I had lain every night since the Grey came, and in the deep cold hour after midnight, when even the palms stop talking to each other, a small voice came walking down the lanes.

*"Who am I?"*

It wasn't crying. That's what lifted me off the ground. It was asking the way a child asks the name of a star, interested, unhurried, as if the answer were just ahead.

*"Who am I?"*

And from the east, from the dead lanes where nothing had made a sound for nine days, the answer came back. Not one voice. Many. Thin and flat and pleasant, like a row of women at a well all agreeing about the weather.

*"Who am I?"*

I put my nose in Theyab's ear.

He came up out of sleep the way he always did, all at once, with his fist already closed. The hirz Hilal had tied on him three days before swung on its cord against his chest, a little silver case no longer than a date-stone. He put his hand on it and snatched the hand back.

"It's hot," he whispered.

Of course it was hot. Something from below was walking in Hilat al-Sarab, and the silver knew it. Hilal had said it would burn. He had not said what you were supposed to do then. Men who write amulets rarely do.

*"Who am I?"*

Theyab heard it that time. I watched it go into him. He knew the voice. Everyone in that village knew the voice; it had been asking for bread and stories and piggyback rides at every door for seven years.

"Shibl," he said.

---

We ran.

He went over the low wall behind the potter's house without touching it, down the alley where the goats were penned, past the sheikh's yard with its dead fire. I ran beside him with my ears flat. Everywhere doors were shut. Nobody had heard. Or everybody had heard, and the doors were shut anyway.

At the well-court we nearly collided with Nadim.

He came out of his father's house barefoot, without his headcloth, with his hair standing up wild and a lamp in one hand that he'd forgotten to light. Behind him, in the doorway, Saqi was trying to stand. He'd been Worn three nights ago, and the thing that wore him had left him like a robe left on a floor: empty, creased, not yet able to hold its own shape. He got as far as the doorframe and hung there, gripping it.

"His mat," Nadim said. He said it to me. I don't think he saw Theyab at all. "His mat's empty. I put the bead on him myself. I tied it twice. I tied it *twice*."

Then he saw who was standing beside the dog, and his face did something complicated.

He didn't have time for it. Neither of us did.

*"Who am I?"*

It came from the east, from the place where the lane past the well stopped being a lane.

---

You have to understand what the Grey looked like by then.

By that night it lay across a third of the village, a flat dead country with a border you could have drawn with a stick. On our side, the walls were mud-brown and the doors indigo and the palm fronds rattled. On its side, everything was the colour of ash a day after the fire. No wind moved there. The border didn't shift much by day. At night it breathed.

Where the lane crossed it, something lay in the sand.

Nadim saw it first and made a sound. He went down on his knees and picked it up, and then I saw it too: a blue glass bead on a twist of red thread. Hilal's bead. The one he'd hung on Shibl's neck with his own hands, the one that's supposed to catch the eye of anything that looks at a child with wanting.

It was split clean in two. Not smashed. Split, like a date opened with a thumb, both halves still hanging on the thread.

The grandmothers say a blue bead breaks when it has taken a look meant for the child, and they say it like a comfort. I have seen a great many split beads, little one. They mean something looked hard enough to break glass. They mean it's still looking.

Nadim stood up with the two halves in his fist.

He didn't say anything to either of us. He didn't light the lamp. He threw it down, and it broke, and the oil ran out into the sand, and he walked across the border into the Grey.

---

I'll give him that. Whatever else Nadim did, and you'll hear all of it, he was the first one into the Grey that night.

"*Shibl!*"

The Grey swallowed the name. Sound doesn't carry there. You shout, and the shout leaves your mouth and simply stops, like a stone dropped into wet date-paste. Nadim shouted his brother's name three times, walking fast into the dead lane, and each time it died a hand's width from his lips.

And each time, somewhere deeper in, something said it back. Softly. Getting it a little more right each time.

Theyab was at the border. He'd stopped with his toes on the line where the colour ended, and I saw why. His left hand was shut tight over his palm, the way he always kept it. His right hand was pressed flat to his chest, over the hirz and the place under it where the feather was. The silver was burning him. I could smell the skin.

He took one step.

I took his sleeve in my teeth.

Gently. I set my weight back on my haunches. *No. You haven't eaten since yesterday noon. Three nights ago you threw a grown man across a yard with light out of your own arms, and you haven't stopped shaking since. You walk in there half-empty and something else might walk out wearing you.*

He looked down at me.

"He's seven," he said.

There's no answer to that. In all the years and all the children, I've looked for one.

I let go of his sleeve.

He went in after Nadim, and I went in after him, because apparently that is what I am.

---

The Grey feels like nothing.

It's the feeling of waking in a tent after everyone you love has ridden away in the night without waking you, and lying there listening to the quiet, and understanding. Spread that over the whole world. The colour goes first. Then the sound. Then the smells. Then, if you stay too long, the reasons you came.

It doesn't eat me. I'm smoke-touched; the Grey and I are, let us say, of different households, but from the same street. Still, every time I come out of it, I'm a little less sure which of my memories are mine.

Theyab walked with his right hand flat on his chest, and the feather under his palm was the only warm thing for a hundred paces in any direction. I could feel it from where I trotted at his knee, the way you can feel a coal through a blanket. The hirz was hot too, a different heat, a sharp metal heat like a nail left in the sun. Two kinds of fire on one small chest. Every few steps his lips moved, too low for the Grey to steal.

*The sand remembers. The sand remembers.*

There were emptied in there. Of course there were.

They stood between the grey houses like tent-pegs, faces turned up to a sky with no stars left in it. As we passed, their heads came round, slowly, one after another, following the warmth of the mark on his chest. They smiled their wrong smiles. They spoke their last sentences into the dead air, over and over, in voices worn thin as old cloth.

I'll give you some of them. I gave you a list before. I'll give you another. Someone ought to keep them.

*"Bring the bucket, the bucket."*

*"I'll be back before the milking."*

*"Is that you? Is that you? Is that you?"*

*"Don't let the fire go out, I'm cold."*

*"Tell my brother I'm sorry about the knife."*

*"The goat's loose. The goat's loose."*

*"Here I am. Here I am."*

That last was the herdsman. The one who had answered his dead wife's voice from the eastern lanes, four nights before, and walked in to meet her, and walked out again like this. He stood at the corner by the old date-store with his arms open, the way a man stands when someone he loves is running toward him. He'd been standing like that for four nights. His arms didn't tremble. That's how you know they're empty. A living man's arms would have given out.

I knew his name. He used to throw me gristle, because he liked to see a saluki move. His name was

——.

There. You see. I try to say it and the place where it should be is smooth, like the skin over a healed burn. It wasn't always like that. When I first told this, years ago, to a different listener, I had it. It's gone now. The Grey keeps what it's given.

Next to him stood a girl I didn't know at all, saying *"I'm not tired, I'm not tired, I'm not tired,"* and I can't tell you if I never knew her, or if I knew her once and the Grey has eaten even the fact of knowing.

Theyab didn't look at them. I was proud of that, and sick about it.

---

We found Nadim on his knees at the end of the lane, near the grove where the palms stood dead and grey as ash-columns, where the Grey had been thickest since the first day.

Shibl was sitting in front of him in the sand, cross-legged, the way small children sit when they've been told to wait and haven't been told for how long. He was in his sleeping shirt. One foot was bare and one had a sandal on it. The broken thread of the bead hung at his neck with nothing on it.

Four of the emptied stood round him in a neat square, one at each corner, facing in. Keeping him. The way you pen a lamb at dusk with four stakes and a rope.

I knew those four. I won't try their names. You've seen what happens.

And there were marks in the sand round that square.

I saw them before Theyab did, because my nose is closer to the ground, and because I'd been looking for them since the noon Shibl walked toward the woman in the heat-shimmer, the noon Theyab carried him home and nobody believed how. Small round cups, two by two, pale and soft-edged the way everything is in the Grey. They came in from the dark side of the grove. They walked once round the four emptied and the child between them, slowly, all the way round, close, the way a herder walks round a pen at dusk to count what's in it. Then they went back out the way they had come.

Counted. And left. The way you leave a thing you're coming back for.

Theyab saw the prints. I heard his breath stop. He looked at them, then at Shibl sitting so neatly in the middle, and I knew what he was thinking because I was thinking it too. Every mother in Hilat al-Sarab had called her children in at noon for nine days, and every child had come, and the doors had shut, and something with hooves had walked the lanes and found nobody to take.

So it had come at midnight instead. It had come for the one child it had already touched. The one it had almost had, and lost. It doesn't like losing, that thing. None of them do.

Nadim had his brother by the shoulders, shaking him gently, desperately.

"Shibl. Shibl, it's me. Look at me. *Look* at me."

Shibl looked at him. Politely. The way you'd look at a stranger who had stopped you in the souq at Buraimi to ask the price of rope.

"Who am I?" he said.

And around him, the four emptied said it too, in four different voices, at exactly the same moment.

*"Who am I?"*

Nadim made a sound I have heard many times since and still have no word for. It's the sound of something tearing that isn't cloth.

---

Now I have to tell you the part that I've spent a long time not telling.

I looked up.

Not at the child. Not at Nadim. Past them, over the heads of the four emptied, into the grove, where the dead palms went back in rows toward the old wall. Something had pulled at me. You know the feeling, little one: you're in a room full of people and you turn your head without deciding to, and someone across the room is already looking at you.

There was heat between the palms.

There is never heat in the Grey. That's the first thing about it. It's cold the way a dead body is cold, all the way through, without a draught. But between two of those grey trunks, at the height of a woman's head, the air was moving. Rippling. The way it ripples over a stone lane at noon in the month when the dates ripen, when the whole world goes soft at the edges and you can see water where there's only sand. A noon shimmer. At midnight. In a place with no sun.

With my amber eye I saw nothing in it. Just the shimmer, and the palms behind it, bent and running like a reflection in a disturbed well.

So I looked with the other one.

I don't do it often. The silver eye is like a window in a house I live in, onto a courtyard I've never been allowed into, and mostly I keep my back to it. But I was frightened for the child, and I wanted to *know*, and so I turned and looked through it on purpose.

She was standing in the heat.

From the waist up, a woman. A tall one, taller than any woman in Hilat al-Sarab, in a black abaya that hung straight down as if there were no wind in the world, which in there there wasn't. Her face was a mother's face. I mean it was *made* of mothers, every face that has ever leaned in a doorway at noon and called a name into the glare, shifting softly from one to the next like sand sliding on a slope. Umm Hanan's, forty years younger. A face I didn't know. Saqi's dead wife's face, which I'd seen once, at the well, when Shibl was a baby on her hip. It held that one longest. I think it knew whose son was in the pen.

From the waist down, she was something else. The abaya didn't reach the ground. Under its hem there were legs, long and thin and bent backward at a joint where no woman has a joint, grey-furred, ending in small neat hooves that stood in the sand without denting it.

The Noon Mare.

Or one of the Hoofed, wearing her story the way you'd wear a borrowed robe to a wedding. I can't tell you which. Perhaps there's no difference. Perhaps a story told often enough, in enough frightened doorways, grows feet of its own and goes walking.

She was looking at the child.

And then, because I was looking at her, she turned her head and looked at me.

---

You're lying in the dark in a strange house. You know there's someone in the room with you. You've known it for a while, and you've kept very still, and you've told yourself that as long as you don't move, as long as you don't make a sound, as long as you don't look, it can't be sure you're there.

And then you look.

And it was waiting for exactly that.

Her mother-face went still on Saqi's dead wife and stayed there. Her eyes, which were too large and set a little too far apart, like a donkey's, met my silver one across forty paces of dead grove, and something passed between them that I can only call *recognition*. Not hers of me. The other way about would have been bad enough. This was worse. It was the look of someone who has been handed a lamp in a dark place and lifted it, and in the light of it, sees everything.

The whole Grey turned toward us.

I don't know how else to say it. Every emptied in that dead country, every one of the nine days' harvest standing in the lanes and the doorways and the courts behind us, turned its head at the same moment, the way a field of grain bends when the wind hits it, and faced the end of the lane where we were. As if someone had pointed. As if someone had stood at the top of the village with an arm out and said, *there.*

The four round Shibl turned first. They had been facing in. Now they faced out. At us.

Their last words stopped. The emptied always talk; it's the one thing left in them. For a long breath there was nothing at all in the Grey, not a word, not a whisper, and then all of them, every emptied in Hilat al-Sarab, said the same thing at once, in their thin borrowed voices, softly, from every direction:

*"There."*

---

They came.

Not fast. They came the way the tide comes in over the salt flats, an inch, an inch, from everywhere at once.

"Get him up," Theyab said. "Nadim. *Get him up.*"

Nadim didn't move. He was staring at the four emptied who had turned to face him. One of them, I'll tell you this much, was the well-keeper's cousin, who'd taught Nadim to swim in the falaj when he was small enough to be held up with one hand. He was holding out that hand now. Reaching for Nadim's face. Smiling.

I went for his legs.

You don't do anything to an emptied you'd regret if they woke up, because sometimes they wake up, and then they have to live in their bodies afterward. So I hit him behind the knees with my shoulder, hard, and he went down like a dropped tent, and lay in the sand still smiling and reaching for nothing.

Theyab had Shibl under the arms and was dragging him backward out of the square. The boy came like a sack of barley. He didn't fight and he didn't help. His head lolled. He was still asking his question, softly, to nobody.

The second of the four turned and put her hands on Theyab.

I saw him shudder all over, the way a horse shudders off a fly. Her hands were cold right through his shirt. She didn't grab, the emptied don't grab, she simply *held on*, the way the dead hold on in dreams, without strength and without any intention of letting go. Her mouth was at his ear.

*"Who are you?"*

"Let go," he said. "Let go, let go of me—"

He tried to call the Light. I saw him try. His chest flickered, and the cracks in his palms showed for an instant, gold, thin as the edge of a coin.

And then went grey. A sputter, a smear, the colour of the thing he was trying to fight. He was too frightened. It's a terrible thing to have a gift you're afraid of. You end up holding it so carefully you can't use it.

I took the woman's sleeve in my teeth and pulled. The cloth tore. She didn't notice. She had both arms round Theyab's chest now, over the burning hirz, and the silver was so hot I smelled it scorching her sleeve, and still she held on.

There were more coming. Out of the doorways. Out of the lanes. Down off the roofs, some of them, simply stepping off and landing in the sand and getting up again, unhurt, unbothered. The herdsman with his open arms. The girl who wasn't tired. Two small boys holding hands. A woman with a baby on her hip that wasn't moving, and wasn't crying, and was looking at me.

Nadim was still on his knees.

---

And then she spoke.

Not the emptied. Her. The thing in the noon shimmer between the palms. She hadn't moved; she hadn't needed to. She opened her borrowed mouth on Saqi's dead wife's face and called out, across the grove, in a voice that every child in that village would have known in their sleep: warm and tired and slightly cross, the voice a mother uses at the door at noon when she's called twice already.

"*Nadim.* Bring your brother in out of the sun."

He lifted his head.

He was fifteen. His mother had been dead six years. He'd been the one who held Shibl at the burial, a boy of nine holding a baby of one, because his father couldn't stand up. He'd been the one who carried Shibl on his shoulders to the top of the dune and named him the stars. And now from the dark, in the dead grove, in the only voice in the world he had never once in his life disobeyed, someone was telling him to bring his brother in.

He got up.

He started to walk toward her.

He had Shibl's sandal in one hand. I don't know when he'd picked it up. He walked toward the shimmer between the dead palms holding his little brother's sandal, and the emptied parted in front of him like water to let him through.

"*Nadim!*" Theyab shouted, and the Grey ate it, a hand's width from his lips. He shouted again. It went nowhere. He was half under two of the emptied by then, holding Shibl against himself with one arm and trying to tear the woman's hands off his chest with the other.

I couldn't reach Nadim. There were three of them between us. I could have gone through them. I could have broken them going through. I felt the other thing in me, the silver half, sit up and take interest at the thought, the way a dog lifts its head at the sound of a knife on a whetstone. It liked the idea. It liked the cold and the dead and the hooves in the sand; it found them familiar, the way you find a cousin's face familiar across a crowded market. My shadow, thrown out ahead of me by the light from Theyab's chest, was for a breath longer than it should have been, and the wrong shape.

I didn't let it out. Not that night.

I barked.

---

I want you to understand what that means. Sound doesn't carry in the Grey. Every shout that night had died a hand's width from the mouth that made it. I had been in that dead place more often than anyone alive, walking in and out to draw it off the village, and I had never once opened my mouth there, because I knew it would be useless.

I barked anyway. The hunting bark. The one that means *now, now, it's running, turn.* The one I'd been barking across sand since before there was a well in Hilat al-Sarab.

And it carried.

Perhaps because a dog's bark has no name in it, and the Grey feeds on names. Perhaps something else let it through, something I wouldn't like to think about. But it went out from me across that dead grove like a stone skipped across the falaj, and Nadim stopped walking.

He stopped two paces from the shimmer. Two paces. He was close enough that the heat of it moved his hair.

He turned round. His face was wet. He looked back at me as if he'd just woken in a strange place.

Then he looked at his hand, and the sandal in it, and he understood what he'd been about to do, and I saw him understand it, and I wouldn't wish that understanding on anyone.

Behind him, the mother-face slid off the thing in the shimmer like a cloth pulled off a cage.

---

What was under it, I won't tell you. Not because I'm protecting you. Because I didn't see it. I'd looked away, at Nadim, and when I looked back she was already going.

And this is the thing I've never told anyone, little one. This is the thing I came all this way round the long road to tell you.

She went *down*.

With my amber eye, I saw the shimmer fade and the hoofprints stop, the way prints stop at the edge of a rock. That's all. That's all anyone else would have seen. Prints in the sand, and then none.

With the silver eye I saw the sand open.

Not a hole. Not a pit. Steps. As if the ground of the grove had always been a flight of stairs going down, and someone had just pulled back a rug. Narrow steps, cut deep, worn in the middle the way the steps of an old fort are worn by a thousand years of feet. Her hooves went down them, one, two, one, two, small and neat, and with every step down the smell came up stronger, out of the dark below, and I knew that smell.

Burnt hair. Old frankincense, the kind they used to burn in the temples before there were any temples you'd know. And heat. Not the clean heat of the sun on a lane. Heat with no light in it at all, a heat that came up the steps like breath out of a mouth.

At the bottom, very far down, farther than those steps should have gone, I saw them. Rows. Figures. Walking. Walking in long patient lines through a dark that lit nothing, with their heads up and their arms at their sides, walking and walking and never arriving anywhere, the way you walk in dreams. Thousands of them. More. And some of them, near the top, at the end of the lines, were wearing the faces of people I had known in Hilat al-Sarab.

She stopped halfway down and looked back up the steps.

Not at Nadim. Not at the child. At me.

And she counted. I saw her lips move. One, two. As the hoofprints had walked once round the pen. *One, two.* Two of us. Or two of them. Or something else she was counting that I didn't understand.

Then the sand slid back over the steps like water closing over a dropped bucket, and there was nothing between the dead palms but dead palms.

---

It was the Underneath, little one, when the old people give it any name at all. I'd always known it was there; part of me has been closer to it than I'd care to say. But I had never seen it so plainly as that night, because I'd chosen to look.

I have wondered since whether she opened that door for me or I opened it for her. I'll tell you what I thought then. I thought: *she turned and looked at me, and then everything in the Grey turned and looked at us.*

I put that thought away, in a deep place in me that was getting full.

---

The emptied went slack.

The way a crowd goes slack after a wedding when the music stops. The woman holding Theyab let go and stood beside him, smiling at nothing. The two little boys sat down in the sand, still holding hands. The herdsman's arms stayed open. They always would.

Their voices came back, thin and pleasant and separate. *Bring the bucket, the bucket. Here I am. I'm not tired.* They'd forgotten us. They'd forgotten what they'd been told to look at.

Theyab staggered free. He fell to his knees with Shibl across his lap, and for a moment I thought he'd simply stay there. Then he put his hand on the boy's face.

Nadim was coming back. He was walking stiffly, the way you walk after you've nearly stepped off a roof in the dark, and he had the sandal clutched against his chest, and he wouldn't look at the place between the palms where the heat had been.

"Is he—" he said. "Is he all right, is he—"

Theyab didn't answer.

He had turned Shibl's face up toward his own. The light from his chest, thin and wavering, lay across the boy's cheeks.

I came close and put my nose to the child's mouth.

Nothing.

No small damp push of air against my whiskers, the one every living thing makes. Nothing at all. As if he'd been made of the Grey itself and set down there for us to find.

Theyab thumbed one of Shibl's eyelids up.

The eye was white. Not rolled back. White all through. White where the dark should be, white where the brown should be, a smooth unbroken white like the inside of a shell, like a bead with no blue in it.

The boy wasn't breathing.

And somewhere far down under the sand, under my four feet, I felt something very small going one, two, one, two, down a flight of stairs that went on forever, and I knew, the way you know a thing in the bones of your skull before your ears have caught up, that she hadn't left empty-handed after all.

---

*End of Chapter Eleven*
