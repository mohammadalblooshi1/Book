# Chapter Nine
## Always Let the Mutawwa' In

> *"A guest who comes reading is better than a son who comes armed."*
> — a saying of the majlis

---

He stopped at the gate of the sheikh's yard and leaned on his stick and looked at us all.

He took his time about it. He looked at the men with the matchlocks, who had stood up too fast. He looked at Sheikh Mazyad, who had come out still pulling his robe straight. He looked at Saqi tied sitting to the post with camel-rope, his broken arm strapped across his broken chest and his white eyes open and his smile gone.

He looked at Theyab, against the far wall, with his cracked arms held out from his body.

And then he looked at me. He looked at me longest. I did not like it.

"Peace on this house," he said.

His voice was dry, like a date that has hung on the palm all summer, wrinkled and sweet in the middle. He said the greeting the full, old way, and Mazyad answered it stumbling, like a man who has not said it properly since he was a boy.

"You are welcome," said Mazyad. "You are welcome, uncle. Our house is your house. Three days, and the Maker's blessing on every one of them."

"Good," said the old man. "Then let's start with coffee. I have walked since the middle of the night and I am too old to read on an empty stomach." He nodded at Saqi without looking at him again. "He'll keep. You've tied him to a post. Good. Posts are underrated. You'd be surprised how few people think of them."

Somebody laughed. Just one man, a short, cracked, frightened laugh, quickly stopped. But it was the first laugh in Hilat al-Sarab since the Grey came, and I saw the yard loosen round it, a little, like a knot when you get your thumbnail into it.

---

His name was Hilal. Mutawwa' Hilal, the women called him, though they whispered it as if he might not like to hear it said. He was perhaps sixty. He was so thin that his cloak hung off his shoulders like a tent off its poles, and the sun had burned him the colour of old leather, so dark that his beard looked white as salt against it. His eyes were small and deep and very bright, and they did not stop moving. He looked at everything as though he were reading it.

He drank three cups of coffee standing up, and shook the cup for no more, and asked for water.

A boy ran for the well.

"No," said Hilal. Not loudly. The boy stopped as though he had been caught by the collar. "Not from that well. Not yet. From a skin, if somebody has one. Something that came in from outside."

Ghanim had one. Ghanim always has one; he sells them. He brought it himself and poured, and I watched Hilal watch him pour, and I watched Hilal's small bright eyes go to Ghanim's hands, and his thumb on the neck of the skin, and stay there a moment, the way a man notes the price on something he has no intention of buying.

"How did you know to come, uncle?" said Mazyad. "We sent no one. Nobody would ride east."

"A camel-man on the Liwa track," said Hilal, and drank. "Tall fellow, three tired camels, a lip split from the sun. I met him at a well two nights back. He told me that in Hilat al-Sarab the well-keeper had given him water and dates and coffee for nothing, and smiled at him the whole time." He wiped his mouth. "He said he'd watered at that well for twenty years, and the well-keeper had never once given anybody anything for nothing. He said he'd never been so frightened in his life."

He handed the skin back to Ghanim.

"So I came," he said. "A man who gives his water away is either a saint or a coat. And I have never met a saint who kept a well."

---

He did the reading in the yard, in the full sun, where everyone could see.

Mazyad wanted it done inside, with the doors shut. Hilal would not have it. "Let them see," he said. "Let them see what it is, and what it isn't. It's the not seeing that makes them stupid."

So they saw. The whole village came. They stood on the walls and on the roofs and in the gate, as close as they dared and not one step closer. The women in their black at the back, with the children held against their skirts. The men in front. Nadim at the very edge, by the gate, holding Shibl against his legs with both hands, as if somebody might try to take the boy again.

Theyab stayed where he was, against the far wall, with the four men and their matchlocks sitting round him. Nobody had told him he could go. Nobody had told him he couldn't. In Hilat al-Sarab, that was the same thing.

Hilal sat down on the sand in front of the post, crosslegged, a little more than an arm's length from Saqi. He laid his stick across his knees. He set a clay bowl of water from Ghanim's skin on the ground in front of him, and took a pinch of salt from a twist of cloth in his sash and dropped it in. He shut his eyes.

And he began.

I will not give you the words. I could not if I wanted to. They are not mine, and they are not for a dog's mouth, and I have promised myself there are some things I will not carry. But I will tell you what it was like. You can have that.

It was like water.

Like a falaj running in the night, when you lie on a roof and hear the water going along its stone channel between the palms, low and steady, never hurrying and never stopping. His voice was not loud or beautiful. It was thin and old and hoarse from the road. But it had a rhythm in it like the rhythm of a heart, a long rise and a short fall, a rise and a fall, and the breath came in the same places every time, a small sharp in-breath like a man about to lift something heavy.

And with every rise, the yard grew cooler.

I felt it. Every creature there felt it. The flies left. A goat that had been bleating in a pen behind the house stopped bleating and lay down. The air in the sheikh's yard, which had been thick and close and sour with the smell of smokeless fire since the men had tied Saqi up, began to thin, the way the air in a tent thins when you lift the side panel.

Saqi's head came up.

---

It came up slowly. The white eyes were open. They went to Hilal, and fixed on him, and the face round them did nothing at all.

Then the back began to arch.

A man tied sitting to a post, a rope round his chest, and his back began to bend away from it, as if someone had put a hand under his spine and was pressing. His shoulders stayed against the wood and his hips stayed in the sand, and everything between them curved up and out like a bow being strung, further, further, until his belly was lifted toward the sky and his head hung back over the top of the post and the white eyes stared upside down at the people in the gate.

Somewhere at the back a woman began to pray aloud and was hushed.

The bones in his broken chest ground together. I heard them. Everyone heard them.

Hilal did not stop. He did not open his eyes. He did not speed up and he did not slow down. The rise and the fall went on, steady as water.

And Saqi laughed.

The mouth hung open upside down over the post and laughed, Saqi's big rough laugh that nobody had heard in years. Then a child's, giggling. Then an old woman's, wheezing. Then a whole majlis of men laughing at a joke, out of one upside-down mouth, on and on.

Then it stopped, and the voice that came out next was Saqi's own, perfectly ordinary.

"*Old man,*" it said. Reasonably. Warmly. "*You're tired. You've walked all night. Sit back. Rest. Drink from my well; the water's sweet.*"

Hilal read.

"*No?*" said the voice. "*Then let me tell you about the people you came to save. You'll like this.*"

The back came down. Slowly, the spine unbending, until Saqi was sitting against the post again with his head up. The white eyes went round the yard. They went from face to face, slowly, unhurried, the way a man walks down a row of camels at a market feeling their legs.

They stopped on Sheikh Mazyad.

"*Forty thalers,*" said Saqi's mouth. "*Forty thalers to the date-merchant in Buraimi, and next year's harvest promised against it. And promised again to the merchant in Liwa, the same dates, the same palms. You'll think of something by spring. You always do. And the grazing on the widows' land, Mazyad. You sold it and kept the price and told them the land was sour.*"

Mazyad's face went the colour of the sand.

Nobody in the yard looked at him. That was how I knew it was true. When a thing is a lie, people look at the man it is told about, to see him deny it. When it is true, they look at the ground.

My hackles stayed flat.

The white eyes moved on.

"*Ghanim,*" said Saqi's mouth, with great affection, the way you greet an old friend. "*Ghanim, Ghanim. Where to start. The thumb on the scale, every day for thirty years. The widow's bangles you took for a debt she never owed. The coin you took off a boy in the ruins, for safekeeping.*" The smile came back to the face. Just a little. "*And the last thing you sold. That wasn't dates, was it? That wasn't a skin. You sold a thing you saw, to a man with clay under his nails. What did he pay you for it, Ghanim? Was it enough?*"

Ghanim had gone the colour of the Grey. His mouth worked. Nothing came out of it.

I looked at Theyab. Theyab was not looking at Ghanim. He was looking at his own left fist, closed tight on his knee, and the knuckles of it were white.

---

Hilal read. He had not stopped. He had not so much as turned his head. The water ran on, low and steady, and the yard was cold now, cold as the shade of a cave, and the thing in Saqi was talking faster.

"*Why read over me?*" it said. "*Why me, old man? I'm a coat. You said it yourself. Read over the one who broke me.*"

The white eyes came round the yard. And found Theyab.

"*Read over him,*" said the thing. Not in Saqi's voice now. A woman's voice, the dead mother's voice, soft and pitiful. "*The monster. Ask him what he is. Ask him where he came from, that nobody saw him come. Ask him why his dog walks in the Grey. Ask what lights him up from inside like a lamp in a tent. Monster. Monster. Read over the monster, old man, and leave a poor broken coat alone.*"

It was the first time anyone had said it.

Remember that, little one. In all the eight years since the noon he woke crying in the lane, nobody in Hilat al-Sarab had said that word out loud. They had thought it. They had not dared. It was the thing in the well-keeper that said it first, in a dead woman's voice, in the sheikh's yard, in front of everyone.

I watched it go into them. I watched it settle. The way a seed settles into wet sand.

Hilal read.

"*And ask him,*" said the voice, softer still, almost a whisper, almost loving, "*about the line in his hand.*"

Hilal stopped.

Just for a breath. Just for the space between one rise and the next. But the water stopped running. In that whole long reading it was the only time it stopped, and every creature in that yard felt it stop, the way you feel a mill-wheel stop in the night by the silence it leaves.

His eyes opened.

He did not look at Saqi. He looked across the yard at Theyab, at the white-knuckled fist on his knee. And then, briefly, at me.

"*The boy with the line in his hand,*" said the thing, very pleased, as if it had found the place where the knife would go in. "*Ask him to open his fist, old man. Go on. We already know what's in it. We've known since—*"

Hilal began again.

Louder. That was the only time I ever heard him raise his voice in reading, then or after. Not much. But the water that had been running low and steady came up all at once like a falaj when they open the sluice at the head of the channel, and the rise and the fall went harder and faster, and the yard went so cold that my breath smoked.

Saqi's mouth opened and the voice stopped mid-word.

His whole body went rigid against the post. Every muscle in it at once. I saw the cords stand out in his neck. I saw his good hand claw at the sand. The rope round his chest went taut as a bowstring and the post behind him creaked in its hole.

And a sound came out of him.

It came out of his open mouth, but it was not from his throat. It was from somewhere lower than that, deeper, from the bottom of him. You have heard it, little one, if you have ever thrown a cup of water on the stones round a fire. That hiss. That sudden fierce spitting rush, as the water meets the heat and the heat fights it and loses. It went on for a long time. Much longer than any cup of water. It was the sound of a whole well poured onto a whole bed of coals. And with it, in the air above his open mouth, the yard shimmered, the way the air above a dune shimmers at noon. Something went up out of him into the sun, something with no colour and no shape, only a ripple in the light, and the smell of smokeless fire came off it so strong that people at the gate cried out and covered their faces.

From the well-court, three lanes away, came a sound like a heavy lid dropped onto a jar.

Then nothing.

The rope went slack. Saqi slumped against the post.

Hilal stopped reading. He sat with his eyes shut and his hands on his knees and breathed, long slow breaths, like a man who has carried a sack of grain up a ladder. Sweat was running down his face into his beard. In front of him, the water in the clay bowl had gone dark grey, the colour of ash stirred into rain.

---

Saqi's eyes were brown.

Ordinary brown. Bloodshot, swollen, the eyes of a man who has not slept in three nights, but brown, with a person behind them. He blinked. He looked down at his chest, at the rope round it, at his broken arm strapped across it. He looked at the bowl of grey water. He looked up at the faces on the walls.

And I saw him remember.

When the Smiling Hunger takes off a robe, little one, it leaves every memory behind, folded neatly in the man like clothes in a chest, for him to find. I watched Saqi find them one by one.

The bucket. The humming.

The camel-man and the coffee.

The bench by the well in the early sun, and the knots in his son's hair, and his fingers working them out.

His elder son's neck under his hand. The boy leaning into it.

The mat at midnight. The weight of the child.

The well.

He began to weep. Not the way men weep at a grave, quiet behind a headcloth. He wept like a child, mouth wide, his broken body shaking against the rope, and with every sob the broken ribs ground and he cried out and wept harder.

"I combed his hair," he said. It came out in pieces. "I combed his hair. I combed his hair, and it was— it wasn't— I combed his *hair*—"

He was looking for his sons.

He found them. Nadim was by the gate where he had been all morning, with Shibl held against his legs. Saqi looked at him across the yard, with his face wet and his mouth open, and lifted his good hand an inch off the sand toward him, the way you lift your hand toward someone across a market to make them see you.

Nadim looked at the ground.

He did not look up. Not once. He stood with his head down and his jaw clenched so hard I could see the muscle jump in it, and both his hands on his little brother's shoulders, and he looked at the sand between his feet as if there were something written in it. His father said his name. He did not look up. His father said it again, broken, *my eyes, my eyes*, and Nadim turned round and walked out of the gate with Shibl and did not come back.

I think, little one, that Nadim was not punishing his father. I think he could not look at him because of the hand. For three days a hand had lain on his neck the way he had waited his whole life for one to lie there, and he had leaned into it, and it had been the thing from the well. Now here was his father's real face, wet and sorry, and Nadim could not tell any more which one he had loved.

He never could, after. Some wounds don't close. They only crust over.

---

Hilal stood up. It took him a while. His knees cracked.

"Untie him," he said. "Gently. Get a bonesetter for that arm, if you have one, and if you don't, I'll do it myself after I've eaten. The ribs will heal on their own if he lies still. Make him lie still." He poured the grey water out of the bowl onto the sand, carefully, away from where anyone was standing, and the sand where it fell hissed faintly and went dark, and did not dry. "Don't let anybody step there for a day. And nobody draws from that well until I've read over it."

Then he turned round.

"Now," he said. "Who threw him?"

Nobody answered. Then everybody did, all at once, without speaking. Every head in the yard turned to the far wall where Theyab sat in his ring of matchlocks.

Ghanim found his voice. He had lost it for a while, and he found it now, and he used it the way he always does, loudly, to cover something. "The Laqeet," he said. "Him. We all saw. He did it with fire. Fire out of his hands. Look at him. Look at his arms."

Hilal walked across the yard.

The men with the matchlocks got out of his way. He came and stood in front of Theyab and looked down at him, leaning on his stick, and I stood up. I stood up between them with my broken ribs, and I did not growl, because there was nothing to growl at yet. But I stood.

Hilal looked at me. "Peace, old one," he said quietly. "Peace. I've only come to look."

Then he crouched down, with his knees cracking again, and held out his hand. Theyab did not move. Hilal waited. He waited a long time, longer than most men wait for anything, his hand held out palm up between them, until at last Theyab, without quite deciding to, put his right arm in it.

Not the left. The right.

Hilal turned the arm over and looked at the cracks.

They had faded since the night. In the full sun you would hardly have seen them: thin pale lines along the inside of the forearm, from the wrist to the elbow, like the lines on the inside of a shell. But when Hilal touched one with his thumb, it lit. A thread of gold, there and gone, the way a coal glows when you blow on it.

I smelled his face, if you understand me. I waited for the sour, cornered smell that had come off every face in the yard all morning. I waited to smell him decide that Theyab was something to be afraid of.

It did not come.

"Hm," said Hilal. "Does it hurt?"

"Yes," said Theyab.

"Good. The things that don't hurt are the ones to worry about." He laid the arm back down on Theyab's knee, quite gently, the way you lay down a thing you have borrowed. He stood up again. He looked at Mazyad.

"What is he?" said Mazyad. His voice was hoarse. "Uncle. You heard what it said. What is he?"

Hilal considered Theyab for a long moment. The bones of his face. The rag of a shirt. The ribs you could count through the tear in it.

"Hungry," said Hilal, "by the look of him. When did anyone in this village last feed this boy?"

Nobody answered that, either.

---

He sent the men with the matchlocks home, without raising his voice, and they were gone before they had thought about whether they wanted to go. He sent a child running to Umm Hanan's for bread, and when the child came back with it, it was still warm, and there were honey-dates folded into it.

He sat beside Theyab while he ate, and did not talk, and watched them carry Saqi indoors on a door taken off its hinges.

When the bread was gone, he reached into his cloak and took something out.

It was a hirz. A little silver case, no longer than a date-stone, battered and dented at one end, on a cord of black goat-hair. It was old. The silver had gone dark in the cracks of the pattern on it, and the cord was greasy from years of somebody's neck.

"There's writing inside it," said Hilal. "Don't open it. Don't take it off. Not to wash, not to sleep, not for anybody, however nicely they ask you. If they ask you nicely, keep it on twice as hard." He leaned over and tied it round Theyab's neck himself, with two knots and then a third. "When the ones from below come near you, it'll burn. You'll know. It won't stop them. Nothing stops them. But you'll know, and knowing is half of living through anything."

Theyab put his fingers on it. "Whose was it?"

"Mine," said Hilal. "I made it when I was about your age. I've made better ones since. That one's been lucky, though."

I put my nose against Theyab's hand where it lay on the hirz. I wanted to smell it. Silver, and old sweat, and a dry, faintly bitter smell like the dust on old paper.

The hirz grew warm under his fingers.

I felt it. Through my nose, through his hand. Not hot. Just warm, the way a stone grows warm in the first sun. Theyab felt it too. He looked down. He looked at me.

I took my nose away. I stepped back a pace.

It cooled.

Hilal had seen. Of course he had seen. His small bright eyes were on me, and they stayed on me, and he did not say anything at all. He only looked, the way he had looked when he first stood in the gate of the yard. Reading.

It was the boy's own heat, I told myself. He was frightened. He had just eaten. It was his blood under his fingers warming the silver. That was all.

I have told myself a great many things, little one.

---

Before he went back to the sheikh's majlis to eat and sleep, Hilal did two more things.

He went to Saqi's house. Nadim would not let him in, at first. Then Nadim did. I watched from the lane. Hilal came out again some time later with Shibl, holding his hand. He crouched down in front of the boy in the doorway, and took out of his sash a blue glass bead on a twist of red thread, and hung it round Shibl's neck with his own hands. "To catch the eye," he said, "of anything that looks at you with wanting. Wear it. Your brother will make sure you wear it."

When he had gone, I saw Nadim come out and kneel down and look at the knot Hilal had tied, and frown at it, and untie it and tie it again. Twice. Hard. Pulling the thread so tight that Shibl complained.

The second thing he did was come to the hollow at dusk.

Theyab was sitting outside his tent. Hilal came down the slope from the village with his stick, slowly, and stopped at the edge of the hollow, outside it, the way a guest stops outside a tent until he is asked in. Theyab did not know he was supposed to ask. Nobody had ever come.

After a while Hilal came in anyway and sat down on the sand.

They sat together and watched the light go off the dunes, red and then blue. In the east the Grey lay flat and dead, untouched by any colour at all.

"It knew about your hand," said Hilal at last.

Theyab said nothing.

"The thing in the well-keeper. It knew. It wanted me to ask." Hilal turned his stick in his fingers. "Things like that don't usually know anything worth knowing. They lie the way you breathe. But that one wasn't lying, was it?"

Theyab said nothing. His left hand was on his knee. Closed.

"May I see it?" said Hilal.

It was the first time anybody had ever asked.

Ghanim had seen it by cheating. The thing in the well had seen it by whatever ways those things see. Nobody in eight years had ever asked Theyab for anything, except to go away.

I watched his fingers begin, just barely, to open.

Then they closed. Hard. And his whole arm came in against his body, the fist hidden in the crook of his other elbow, the way a child hides a thing it has stolen.

"No," said Theyab.

Hilal nodded, as if that were a perfectly good answer. Perhaps it was.

"Hm," he said. "Keep it, then. For now." He got up, with his knees cracking. "But keep it well, son of no one. You are not the only one who's heard about it."

He went back up the slope toward the village in the blue dusk. He did not look back. I watched him all the way to the sheikh's door.

---

I did not sleep that night. My ribs would not let me, and neither would my eye.

I lay on the top of the dune above the hollow, where I could see the whole of Hilat al-Sarab in the starlight, and beyond it, the Grey: flat, cool, dead, a long low bank of nothing with a clean edge like the edge of a cloth. I watched it with both eyes. That is what an old dog does on a hill at night.

After a while I noticed that the edge was not quite clean any more.

There was a place where it bulged. Very slightly. A soft swelling in the line, like a sail filling, at the end of the eastern lane. Leaning toward the village.

I looked at it.

It leaned a little further.

I did not like that. I looked away, to the north, toward the date grove, the way you look away from a man who is staring at you in a market.

And when I looked back, the bulge was gone. The edge at the eastern lane was straight and clean again.

There was a new bulge. To the north. By the grove. Exactly where I had been looking.

I lay very still on the top of the dune.

I looked south, at the goat-herd's empty hut. I counted ten breaths. I looked back. The northern bulge had flattened. The edge was swelling, slow and soft, toward the goat-herd's hut. Toward the place I had looked.

I looked at the sheikh's house, where Hilal was sleeping. I did not mean to. It was the first place my eye went.

And the Grey leaned toward the sheikh's house.

Slowly. Gently. The whole long edge of it, from the grove to the southern huts, tilting very slightly in the starlight, the way a field of grass tilts when the wind comes over it. Toward the house where the man who had just driven the Smiling Hunger out of Hilat al-Sarab lay sleeping. Toward the exact place where my silver eye was resting.

As if somebody far below were looking up through it. And following.

I shut the silver eye.

I put my paw over it. I lay on the top of the dune with my face pressed into the cold sand and my paw over my eye like a puppy hiding from thunder, and I did not move, and I did not open it until the sky in the east had gone from black to the colour of ash to the colour of a pigeon's breast.

When I opened it, the Grey was where it had always been. Flat. Clean-edged. Dead.

I told myself I had dreamed it. An old dog dreams, little one. I have told you that.

But I did not look at the sheikh's house again for three days.

*End of Chapter Nine*
