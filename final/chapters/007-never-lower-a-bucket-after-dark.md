# Chapter Seven
## Never Lower a Bucket After Dark

> *"Name the Maker before you pour, or something under the ground will thank you for the drink."*
> — the women at the well of Hilat al-Sarab

---

Every well has someone living in it, little one.

The women of Hilat al-Sarab knew that, though none of them would have said it so plainly. They said the words before every bucket, and they poured the first splash of every day back into the shaft, and they never shouted down the well or spat in it or let a child drop stones into it to hear the splash. They did these things the way you cover your mouth when you yawn: because their mothers did, and because once, long ago, somebody's mother had found out why.

The one in the well at Hilat al-Sarab was old and quiet. I had smelled it for eight years without ever seeing it: a cool smell, wet stone and moss and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a water-jar that has held good water a long time. It was a Dweller, the kind that lives alongside people. It kept the water. It asked nothing except the words and the splash, and in return the water at the bottom of that shaft had never once gone bitter, not in the worst summer anyone could remember.

That night, after Saqi stopped humming and the well hummed back, the cool smell was gone.

---

I was on my feet before the third note.

Saqi was standing at the well with the rope still in his hands, perfectly still, the way a man stands when he has heard his name called in a crowd and is not sure he heard it. The humming came up the shaft, soft and round. Three notes, a little turn, three notes. His dead wife's grinding song, sung by something that had never ground grain in its life.

Then the rope went tight.

I heard it before I saw it. The rope creaked over the wooden roller with a sound it had never made under a bucket of water, a long, dry, straining groan, as if a grown man had taken hold of the other end and put his whole weight on it. Saqi's arms jerked straight. His heels slid on the stone.

He did not let go. That is the thing I will always remember about him. He was a hard man and a mean one, but he did not let go of his rope.

He hauled.

Hand over hand, the way he had hauled ten thousand buckets, his shoulders bunching under his shirt and his breath coming harder. The roller groaned. Something was coming up. I could hear it knocking against the shaft as it came: not the light knock of a bucket swinging, but a slow, heavy drag, like something being pulled up a stairway it was in no hurry to leave. And with every arm-length of rope, the smell came up ahead of it.

Not wet stone. Not moss.

Smokeless fire, little one. I have no better words for it. It is the smell of heat with nothing burning, of a forge with no coal in it, of the air above a dune at noon if that air could be bottled and kept in the dark for a thousand years. And under it, damp and cold, the smell of stone that has been wet since before the world had people in it.

I growled. I did not decide to. It came up out of the bottom of my chest and stayed there.

Saqi did not hear me. He brought the bucket over the lip of the well and set it on the stone.

It was water. Only water. A leather bucket of ordinary dark water, slopping a little, with the stars in it.

He looked into it.

I was close enough by then to see what he saw. I had come round the wall without meaning to, low, my belly nearly on the sand. I saw his face in the water, dark against the stars, a heavy face with a short grey beard and deep lines at the corners of the mouth from years of not smiling.

The face in the water smiled first.

Not much. A little lift at both corners. Saqi's own face, in the water, smiling up at him, a heartbeat before his face above the water did anything at all.

And then his face above the water smiled back.

He bent over the bucket. He put his two hands in it and cupped them and lifted the water to his mouth and drank. He did not name the Maker. He drank the way a man drinks who has crossed the sabkha in summer, deep and long, with water running down into his beard, and the stars in the bucket shivered and broke apart.

I saw it go into him.

I saw it with the silver eye. Something went out of the water and into the man the way water goes into dry sand, all at once, without a sound, leaving nothing on the surface. He straightened up. He wiped his beard with the back of his hand. He stood there in the starlight for a long moment, and the line of his shoulders changed. They had been hunched, the way they always were, braced against a world that owed him money. Now they settled. They went loose and easy, like the shoulders of a man coming home to a good supper.

Then he turned his head and looked straight at me, where I lay against the wall in the dark, where no man's eyes could have found me.

"Good dog," said Saqi gently.

Saqi had never called anything good in his life.

---

I will tell you what had happened, so that you understand it better than I did that night.

The Grey had been in Hilat al-Sarab for days by then, and the Grey does not stay on the top of things. It soaks down. It finds the wells and the roots and the graves, all the old places where the ground is thin, and it goes into them like damp into a wall. Wherever there was a Dweller in the dark, keeping something, minding something, the Grey came down on it and leaned on it and whispered at it in a thousand voices. Some of them held. Most of them did not.

The one in the well had been treated kindly for longer than the village had a name. But kindness is a wall you build fresh each morning, little one. It is not a wall you build once. The night of the voices, nobody at the well had said the words. Nobody had poured the first splash back. Everybody was too frightened, and the bucket stood untouched. And the night after, Saqi came to it humming a song he had heard coming out of the Grey, and lowered a bucket into the dark without a word, and the thing at the bottom, which was already leaning, simply fell.

It went over. It Turned, as the old book says. The good thing that kept the water became a door.

And what came up through the door was not the Dweller. It was one of the five that serve the Sealed King. I knew it the moment Saqi smiled at me, because it is the only one of them that smiles.

They call it the Smiling Hunger. It is the one that puts people on.

It does not eat you, the way the Grey eats names. It does not crush you in your sleep. It simply puts you on, the way you would put on a robe that somebody has left lying by the door, and it walks about in you, and it smiles with your face. It is a better smiler than any of us. It has had so much practice, and with so many faces.

When a person has been put on like that, the old people say *he is Worn*, and they say it quietly, in case the thing hears and comes looking for a new robe.

---

The next morning Saqi gave away water.

I want you to understand how strange that was. Saqi charged for water the way the sun charges for shade. He charged travellers a copper a cup. He charged the herders by the head of goat. He had once made a widow pay for a bucket in dates because she had no coin, and then complained that the dates were dry. The well was his, and his father's before him, and every drop that came up in Hilat al-Sarab was a drop that somebody owed him.

That morning a camel-man came in off the dunes from the west, a tall thin Bedouin with three tired camels and a lip cracked open from the sun, and asked at the well, the way travellers always ask, what the price was.

Saqi filled his skins for him. All four. And watered the camels. And then he went into his own house and came back out with a bowl of dates and a little cup of coffee and pressed them into the man's hands, and stood smiling while he ate.

"The well belongs to whoever is thirsty," Saqi said. "Go with the Maker's care."

My hackles went up all along my spine, from skull to tail.

The camel-man saw it. He looked at me, and at Saqi's smile, and then at the smile again. He was a desert man, and desert men know a great deal that village men have forgotten. He did not finish the coffee. He set the cup down very carefully on the lip of the well, and thanked Saqi in the formal way, the full way, naming the Maker twice. Then he led his camels out of Hilat al-Sarab at a walk that was nearly a trot and did not look back.

Saqi watched him go, still smiling.

Nobody else noticed. Or they noticed and were glad of it. Free water in a frightened village is a wonderful thing. By noon the women were saying that the fright had softened the well-keeper, that grief for his dead wife had finally broken open and let the good out. By evening a grandmother on the north side was saying that the Grey was a punishment for hard hearts, and look, see how Saqi's heart had turned, and perhaps if everybody's turned, the Grey would go back where it came from.

I lay in the shade of the wall and listened, and my hackles never went down all day.

---

The worst of it was his sons.

Saqi had beaten Nadim since Nadim was old enough to stand. Not every day. Not even every week. Just often enough that the boy never stopped flinching at the sound of a belt being unbuckled, often enough that Nadim had learned to be loud and hard and quick with his fists, so that nobody would ever guess there was a man at home who was louder and harder and quicker. Since their mother died, Saqi had not once put his hand on either of his boys except to hit them.

On the second morning, I watched Nadim come out of the house and stop in the doorway.

His father was sitting on the bench by the well in the early sun, with Shibl in his lap. Shibl was seven, and too big for laps, and he was sitting there stiff as a stick, the way a child sits on the lap of a stranger at a wedding. Saqi was combing his hair. Slowly. With his fingers. Working the knots out one by one, the way their mother used to, and humming.

Three notes, and a little turn.

Nadim stood in the doorway and watched it. His face did a thing I had never seen it do. It went young. All the hardness went out of it at once, like a fist unclenching, and underneath it was a boy of about Shibl's age, standing in a doorway, watching his mother comb his own hair, long ago, before there was a Shibl.

Saqi looked up. He smiled at his elder son.

"Come here, my eyes," he said. "Come and sit with your father."

Nadim did not move. Then he did. He came across the court like a boy walking across ice, and he sat down on the bench beside his father, carefully, as far away as the bench allowed. And Saqi reached out with the hand that was not combing Shibl's hair and laid it on the back of Nadim's neck. Just laid it there. Warm and heavy.

Nadim shut his eyes.

He sat there with his father's hand on his neck and his eyes shut, and his mouth trembled, and he did not cry, because he had not cried in front of anyone since he was eight years old and he was not going to start now. But he leaned. Just a little. He leaned into the hand.

I have watched a great many terrible things, little one. I watched the goat-herd walk into the Grey. I will watch worse before this tale is done. But I am not sure I have ever watched anything worse than that boy leaning into that hand.

Because the hand was a lie. Every bit of it. Every gentle thing the well-keeper did in those days made my hackles stand up like a cat's. The kindness came off him sour as turned milk. And Nadim, who could not smell it, who had waited his whole life for that hand, believed every bit of it with his whole heart.

That is how it works. Remember it. The thing that puts people on does not come wearing the face of your enemy. It comes wearing the face of the one you have waited for. It gives you, at last, the thing you always wanted from that face, and you will defend it with your teeth. Nadim would have killed anyone who tried to take that hand off his neck.

Theyab tried anyway. Of course he did.

---

He went to the well on the second evening while Saqi was inside, and found Nadim alone, drawing water.

"Your father," Theyab said.

Nadim did not look round. "What about him."

"He's not—" Theyab stopped. He could not lie, and he did not know the truth well enough to say it. He tried again. "Something's wrong with him. Since the night of the voices. Ghareeb won't go near him. Look at Ghareeb."

I was standing behind Theyab with my head low and my hackles up, which was where I had been standing for two days. Nadim looked at me. Then at Theyab.

"My father," said Nadim, very quietly, "has been better these two days than he has been since my mother died. He's kind. He's *kind*, Laqeet. He gave a stranger coffee. He combed Shibl's hair." His voice cracked on *hair*, and he hated it, and I watched him hate it. "And you come here with your dog that walks in and out of the Grey like it owns it, and you tell me something's wrong."

"It's not him," said Theyab.

He said it plainly, because that is the only way he can say anything. He believed it. Nadim heard that he believed it.

Nadim put the bucket down. He turned round and hit Theyab in the mouth.

It was not a hard blow, as Nadim's blows went. I think he was holding back. I think some part of him was afraid that if he hit as hard as he wanted to, something might break open in him that he could not close. Theyab's lip split against his teeth and he stepped back, and put his fingers to it, and looked at the blood.

I could have had Nadim's throat. I want you to know that. He was a big lad for fifteen, and I am old, but I could have had his throat before he finished turning round.

I did not. Theyab's hand was on my neck.

"Stay away from my brother," said Nadim. "Stay away from my father. Stay away from my well." He picked up the bucket again. His hands were shaking so badly the water slopped over the rim. "And if you say that again, about my father, I'll tell the sheikh it was you that brought the Grey. Everybody already thinks it."

He went inside. He did not look back. In the doorway, for just a moment, I saw Saqi's shape behind him, in the dark of the room, standing very still, listening.

Smiling.

---

On the third day, Shibl came to the wall.

There was a low mud wall at the back of Saqi's yard, crumbling at the top, where the yard gave onto the lane that led out past the last houses to Theyab's hollow. Shibl knew he was not allowed to talk to Laqeet. Every child in the village knew that. But Shibl had been in love with me since the evening at the well when he first looked into my eye. He thought my silver eye was a coin I kept in my head. He used to save me date-stones with a little flesh left on them, and hold them out on his flat palm the way he had been taught to feed a camel, and giggle when my whiskers touched his hand.

He came to the wall in the afternoon, when the noon rest was over and the shadows were beginning to stretch, and he hung over the top of it on his stomach and held a date down toward me.

I took it. I am not made of stone.

Theyab was sitting against the wall on the lane side, mending a sandal-strap with a bone needle. He kept very still. You do not move suddenly near a bird that has landed close to you.

"Is he yours?" Shibl asked him. Whispering. As if the wall had ears. Perhaps it did.

"He isn't anybody's," said Theyab. "He just stays."

Shibl thought about this. "Baba says he's an old one."

"An old dog?"

"No," said Shibl. "An old one." He said it the way you say a word you have heard grown-ups use and are not sure about. Then he lost interest in it, the way children do, and dangled his arm down and stroked my ear with one finger. "Baba talks to the water," he said.

Theyab's needle stopped.

"At night," said Shibl. "When he thinks we're asleep. He goes out to the well and he leans over and talks to it. I go and look through the gap by the door."

"What does he say?"

Shibl shrugged, one-shouldered. "I can't hear. But it talks back." He looked down at me, and then at Theyab, and his face was not frightened at all. It was pleased. It was the face of a child telling a secret he thinks is a good one. "It talks back with Mama's voice."

Their mother was dead. She had died when Shibl was too small to remember anything of her, not her face and not her voice. He had never heard her voice in his life, little one. Except, now, down the well.

"Shibl," Theyab said carefully. "What does your mama say?"

"She says it's warm down there." Shibl rested his chin on his folded arms on the top of the wall. "She says it's always warm. She says I can come and see her when Baba says I'm big enough." He frowned a little. "But Baba says it's not about big. He says it's about light."

From inside the house, a voice called.

"*Shibl.*"

It was Saqi's voice. It was gentle. It had a little turn in the middle of the name, a lift, the way a woman says a child's name when she is calling him in to eat.

Shibl slid off the wall like a lizard off a rock and was gone. We heard his feet run across the yard. We heard the door. We heard Saqi say something, low and warm, and Shibl laugh.

Theyab sat with the sandal in his lap. He had driven the bone needle into the ball of his thumb without noticing. A bead of blood stood on it, very red.

"It's going to take him," he said to me. Not a question.

My hackles stayed flat. It was true.

---

He tried. I want you to know he tried.

He went to Sheikh Mazyad's door at dusk and knocked until a servant came, and said that Saqi was not Saqi, and that something had come up out of the well and was wearing him, and that Shibl was in danger. The servant went in. The servant came back out. The sheikh had said that the Laqeet should go back to his tent, and that the well-keeper's generosity in a hard time was an example to the whole village, and that if the Laqeet came again with stories about the well, there would be people who would want to know why a foundling and a smoke-touched dog were so interested in the only water in Hilat al-Sarab.

He went to Umm Hanan. She listened. She believed him; I smelled it on her. But she was one old widow with a lamp, and she said what any of them would have said, which was that there was a man of the books, a mutawwa' who walked the villages of the Liwa crescent reading over the sick and the troubled, and that she would send word with the next rider going east. There was no rider going east. Nobody was riding anywhere near the Grey.

He went to Old Bakhit's tent at the western edge, but the flap was laced shut, and from inside, a bell rang once, softly, the way a bell rings when somebody brushes it in passing, and nobody came out.

So in the end there was nobody but us. There usually is.

---

That night I went to look down the well.

I waited until the village was dark. Saqi's house was quiet. I went over the wall at the back of the yard, which I could do then without trying, and crossed the court on soft feet, and put my front paws up on the lip of the well and looked down.

The rope hung into the dark. The smell came up past it. Smokeless fire and wet stone. Stronger than before. So strong my eyes watered.

With the amber eye, I saw what you would see. A shaft of stone going down, the faint pale ring of the lip, then black. Somewhere far below, perhaps, the faintest suggestion of water, a darker black that moved.

Then the silver eye began to throb.

It did not ache the way it ached when Theyab was in danger. It *pulled*. As if the eye were a stone on a string and somebody at the bottom of the well had taken hold of the string. I felt my head lean over the lip before I knew I was leaning. And the eye looked down. And the eye saw.

There was no water at the bottom.

There was no bottom.

The shaft went down through the sand and through the stone, and where the water should have been, it simply went on. The stone walls opened out like the mouth of a jar. Below them was a dark that did not end, and the dark was hot. Not warm, the way the Quiet Country was warm, the way the inside of a tent is warm in the early morning. Hot like the inside of a mouth. Heat with no light in it at all, rising up the shaft against my face like breath.

And in the heat, far down, there were rows.

Rows of figures. Hundreds of them. More. Standing in long, straight lines that went away into the dark further than the eye could follow, the way date palms go away in rows in a great grove. They were walking. All of them. Every one of them was walking, steadily, one foot and then the other, their arms loose at their sides and their faces turned forward. And none of them was going anywhere. They walked and walked and stayed exactly where they were, like men walking against a wind that never stopped blowing.

I knew some of the shapes. I want to tell you I didn't. The way the shoulders went on one. The tilt of a head on another, a little to the side, as if listening to something underneath the ground.

The man who had stood at the well saying *bring the bucket*.

The goat-herd.

They were up here, standing in the lanes of Hilat al-Sarab. And they were down there, too, walking in the rows. Both at once.

That is what the emptied are, little one. I had not understood it fully until that night. What stands in the lanes is the robe. What walks in the rows is the rest.

The well was a door. It had always been a door. Every well is, every grave, every cave, every lone tree with its roots in the dark. It had been a closed door, with something kind leaning against it from the inside, for a very long time. Now the kind thing was gone, and the door was open, and down at the bottom, in the heat, something was looking back up at me.

Not the rows. Something behind the rows. Something that did not have a face, because it had so many.

"You know the way down, don't you, old one?"

I nearly went into the well.

My back legs scrabbled on the stone and I threw myself backward off the lip and landed badly in the court, and rolled, and came up with every hair on my body standing straight out.

Saqi was standing in the doorway of his house. I had not heard the door. I had not smelled him come. He stood with his shoulder against the frame and his arms folded, quite relaxed, the way a man stands to watch the sunset, and he was smiling.

"You've been down," he said pleasantly. "I can smell it on you. It's all right. We know you." He tilted his head, a little too far. "We've always known you."

I did not growl. I could not. There was nothing in my chest to growl with.

He went back inside. He did not close the door.

---

I did not tell Theyab about the rows. I could not, and I would not have if I could. But I went back over the wall and I lay down in the lane beside him and pressed my side against his leg, hard, and I did not sleep. Neither did he.

We moved up to the wall in the dark, the two of us, and lay in the shadow at its foot, where the lane met the back of Saqi's yard. Theyab had his khanjar out. I do not know what he thought he would do with it.

The house was a mud house in front and a palm-frond room behind, the kind they call an arish, cooler in summer, where the boys slept. The frond walls were old and gapped. From where we lay, through the gap by the corner post, I could see in.

The two boys were asleep on their mats. Nadim on his back, one arm flung over his face, breathing hard through his mouth the way boys of fifteen do. Shibl curled on his side like a puppy, with his thumb near his mouth, though he was too old for it and would have died before he admitted it.

The moon had risen. It came through the gaps in the fronds in thin white stripes, and lay across the floor, and across the sleeping boys.

Midnight came. I know because the jackals stopped. There is a time every night, little one, when the jackals in the dunes stop calling all at once, the way men in a majlis stop talking when the coffee comes in. That night they stopped, and the silence that came after was very large.

The door between the house and the arish opened.

Saqi came through it. He moved without any sound at all. He was barefoot, and his shirt hung open, and his hands hung loose at his sides, and he was not smiling. That was what frightened me first. I had not seen his face without the smile for three days.

He walked to Shibl's mat. He stood over it.

He stood there a long time. The moon-stripes lay across him. One lay across his eyes.

They were white. Rolled up in his head so far that there was nothing left of them but white, two blind, gleaming shells in the moonlight.

Theyab's hand closed on my neck so hard it hurt.

Saqi bent down. Slowly. His hands came up and hovered over the sleeping child, not touching him, palms down, the way you hold your hands over a fire to warm them, or over a lamb to judge its weight.

And then he spoke.

It came out of his mouth in two voices at once. One was his own, low and rough, the well-keeper's voice that had haggled over every cup of water in Hilat al-Sarab for twenty years. The other was underneath it, laid exactly in time with it, so that every word was said twice in the same breath. A woman's voice. Soft and tired and fond. The voice of a mother leaning over a sleeping child at the end of a long day.

"*This one,*" said the two voices together, very tenderly, "*is light enough to carry.*"

---

*End of Chapter Seven*
