# Chapter Twelve
## Never Heal What the Village Fears

> *Beware the child who glows; the desert has plans for him, and the desert is always hungry.*
> — the grandmothers of Liwa, to children who stay out after the doves have stopped calling

---

"Do it again."

Nadim was on his knees beside us in the dead sand. He'd dropped the sandal. His hands were in the air over his brother's body, not touching, the way you hold your hands over a coal you've been told is out and don't believe it.

"You did it to my father," he said. "In the yard. I saw it. You put your hands out and the light came and it threw the thing out of him. I *saw* it. So do it again."

"I don't know how," said Theyab.

It was true. I stood at his side with every hair on my back lying flat, and I'd have given my silver eye for Nadim to know what that meant. He didn't. He couldn't. Nobody in Hilat al-Sarab had ever learned to read me, except one blind man at the western edge who didn't need eyes for it.

"Not here," Theyab said. "Not in this. I can't, in this. It eats it."

He meant the Grey. He was right. Whatever light he had in him came out thin and sick in there, the colour of the thing around it. He gathered Shibl up against his chest, the boy's head lolling on his shoulder, those white eyes open and staring at nothing over his back, and he stood.

"Out," he said. "We get him out."

---

We came back down the lane the way we'd gone in, past the emptied, who didn't turn their heads this time, who had forgotten us as completely as if we had never come. Theyab walked in front with the child. Nadim walked behind with the sandal, which he'd picked up again; he held it in both hands against his stomach. I walked between them, and every few steps I looked back over my shoulder at the dead grove.

Nothing came up out of the sand. Nothing counted us. That was almost worse.

The feather on Theyab's chest lit the way. Not brightly. A glow the colour of a coal under ash, leaking through his shirt and the hot little silver case of the hirz. Where it fell, the Grey drew back a hand's breadth, the way the edge of a puddle draws back from a hot stone, and in that hand's breadth for a moment there was colour: the dull brown of a mud wall, the green of a door. Then we passed and the grey closed over it again.

I'll tell you what I was thinking. I was thinking about the eye. About the moment I'd turned it on that thing in the shimmer, and she had turned and looked back down it, and the whole dead country had turned with her. I was thinking it the way you worry at a thorn in your paw: you know it's there, you keep putting your tongue to it, and you won't let anyone else look.

Then we stepped over the border and I stopped thinking about anything, because the village was waiting.

---

They'd come out at last.

You'd think they'd have come out when a child was crying *Who am I* down their lanes at midnight. They hadn't. They'd come out when Nadim broke the lamp. Someone had heard glass, and someone else had heard the someone, and in a village that size a sound is a rope that pulls every door open one after another. They stood at the edge of the Grey in a crescent with lamps and torches and old matchlocks they didn't know how to load in the dark. Sheikh Mazyad in his good bisht thrown over his sleeping shirt. Ghanim, with his camel-stick. Women in black, faces half-covered, holding their own children back by the shoulders. The old men from the majlis. Forty people, perhaps. More.

And at the front, held up between two neighbours like a sack that wouldn't stand by itself, Saqi.

He saw his son in Theyab's arms. He saw the eyes.

He didn't scream. He sat down. His legs simply stopped, and he went down into the sand between the two men holding him, and sat there with his mouth open.

Theyab laid Shibl on the hard ground just outside the border, where the lamplight fell. He knelt over him.

And then he looked up.

That was his mistake. I don't blame him for it. Anyone would have. He looked up, and saw them: the crescent of lamps, the forty faces, the matchlocks, the sticks. The eyes, all of them, every one, not on the child dying in the sand but on him. On the boy with the glowing chest kneeling over the well-keeper's son in the place where the Grey began.

I could smell what they were thinking. It doesn't take a dog. It takes a nose and a lifetime of being the stranger.

---

"Please."

Nadim had come round to the other side of his brother. He was kneeling too, close enough to touch Theyab and not touching him.

I don't think he'd ever said that word to Theyab before. Not once in all the years at the well, all the years of mocking and jostling and *Laqeet, Laqeet, did your mother drop you or throw you?* It came out of him rusty and broken, like a well-wheel that hasn't turned in a long time.

"Please. He's my brother. *Please.*"

Theyab put his hands on Shibl's face.

---

I want to tell you he did it like the heroes in Bakhit's stories. I want to tell you he laid his hands on that child with a steady face and the light poured out of him like water from a skin.

His hands were shaking so badly that when he set them on either side of Shibl's head, the boy's head rocked between them. He was thinking of the forty faces. He was thinking about the matchlocks. He was thinking about what they'd said in the market after Saqi, about the word that had started going round from mouth to mouth like a coin nobody wanted to be left holding. And under all of that, deepest, he was thinking about the thing Hilal had seen in him, the thing Bakhit had been half-telling him for years in stories about children with feathers on their chests. He was thinking about himself.

So the light came grey.

It leaked out of his palms thin and sickly, the colour of the dead country behind us. It lay over Shibl's face like dirty water.

And Shibl, under it, smiled.

Slowly. Too wide. The smile of the emptied, the one I'd watched spread on face after face for nine days, on a seven-year-old's mouth. The white eyes didn't change. The chest didn't move. Only that smile, opening like a cut.

Somebody in the crowd screamed. A woman. I don't know which.

Theyab snatched his hands back as if he'd been bitten.

"I can't," he whispered. "I can't, I'm making it worse, it won't—"

I couldn't tell him. I've never been able to tell any of them. In all the long years of me, I have never once been able to say a single word to the one I was walking beside, and that night I would have traded the rest of my life to say three.

So I did what a dog does.

I shoved my long nose up under his chin, hard, so his head came up, and I turned my muzzle against his jaw and pushed, and pushed, and kept pushing, until his face was turned away from the crowd and the lamps and the forty pairs of eyes, and there was nothing in front of him but the child.

*Not them. They're not the ones who need you. Him.*

He looked.

---

And I watched it happen.

I watched him stop being a frightened boy surrounded by people who hated him, and become a boy looking at a child who had lost his own name. A child who was, in that moment, exactly as alone as Theyab had been every day of his life since a noon eight years ago when he woke crying in the lane with nobody calling him in.

That's what it takes, you know. That's the whole secret. Bakhit never told him and Hilal didn't know. It's not courage and it's not faith and it's not anything in the old books. It's just this: you have to look at the one in front of you until you forget yourself.

Most people never manage it once.

The light went gold.

---

It came up out of him gently this time. Softer and warmer than the blaze that had thrown Saqi across the yard. It flowed out of his chest, out of the feather under his shirt, down his arms, and into Shibl's face like honey poured into warm milk. I felt it on my muzzle where I was still pressed against him. It was the only time in nine days I'd been warm.

The smile went first. It went out of Shibl's mouth the way a wrinkle goes out of a cloth when you smooth it with your palm. Then the grey went out of his skin.

And Theyab saw.

I know what he saw because he said it aloud afterward, that same night, to the dark, when he thought only I was listening. I'll tell it to you the way he told it. He never told it to anyone else.

---

He saw a baby born in the killing heat of summer, red-faced and furious, while a boy of eight pressed his nose to the doorframe to look. He saw a woman lying very still on a mat, and the same boy holding the baby while men he didn't know washed her, because his father couldn't stand.

He saw that same boy, older, carrying the baby on his shoulders up the dune behind the well, pointing at the night.

*That one's Suhail. When he comes up, the heat breaks. Those are al-Thurayya, the sisters. That one's the camel, see, and the little one following it is the calf. That's you. The little one. Right next to me.*

He saw Nadim sneaking bread into his brother's hands at the evening meal. He saw Nadim sitting alone behind the house one night, after their father had beaten him, crying into his knees without a sound so nobody would hear. Nobody but the small boy watching from the window.

Then the pictures turned and ran forward.

---

A fire.

A winter fire, he said, the kind you make from ghaf wood, that throws long sparks up into a cold black sky. Dunes all around, red at the rim where the light touched them. Children sitting close, their knees drawn up, their faces lit gold from below. Ten of them, or eleven. He couldn't count them. They kept moving.

And a man. Old, older than anyone Theyab had ever known except Bakhit. A grown man grown old, with a beard gone white and a voice cracked like a dry waterskin, sitting on a rug with his hands moving in the firelight. He was telling them something. A story. The children were leaning in. One of them had both hands over her mouth.

He couldn't see the man's face. The man sat with his back half to the fire, the way old men do when the heat hurts their eyes. Theyab saw his hands, and his shoulders, and the way he tilted his head to one side when one of the children spoke, listening with his whole neck.

Shibl did that. Shibl had done it all his life, since he was small enough to ride on a shoulder. Tilt the head, listen with the neck. Theyab knew it the way you know the walk of someone you've watched for years across a market. So he thought: *That's him. That's Shibl. Grown up. Grown old. Alive.* And something in his chest broke open with gladness.

Brothers share such things, I've noticed since. The tilt of a head. The shape of a shoulder.

He said the old man stammered. That he stopped on a word, a name perhaps, and couldn't get past it, and reached down and poured himself coffee instead, his hand shaking so the coffee spilled on the rug, and the children pretended not to see.

He said there was a dog at the old man's feet. An old dog, a saluki, grey all round the muzzle, lying with its head on its paws.

He didn't say anything else about the dog. He looked at me when he said it, there in the dark afterward. Then he looked away. And I'm not going to say anything else about it either, little one, not tonight. You can look at me all you like.

---

And then, behind all of it, the other thing.

It didn't wait its turn. It came up through Shibl's future like a hand coming up through water, and took hold of him.

The moon, enormous above him, so close it filled half the sky, pale and pitted like a skull left out on the sand. Sand beneath his own bare feet, higher than any dune he'd ever climbed, falling away on every side into the dark. His own arms opening; he couldn't stop them opening. Light running out of the feather on his chest, cracking him from the inside.

And this time he felt it.

Not a beautiful heat. A real one. His skin tightening across his ribs like a drum-hide drying in the sun. His lips splitting. The wet in his eyes boiling, and the world going white and staying white. The smell of honey, and hot metal, and hair. And under it, rising, the smell of meat on a spit.

He tried to scream. He found the fire had already taken that.

He couldn't move. That was the worst of it, he said. Not the pain. The not moving. Like the nights he woke with something heavy on his chest and couldn't lift a finger. He was a statue made of burning, standing on the top of the world with his arms out, and inside it he was screaming and screaming with no mouth to scream through.

Somewhere far below, someone was shouting.

He couldn't hear what. He thought it might be his name. And then he thought, wildly, through the white, through the burning: *which one?*

---

Shibl gasped.

A huge ragged breath, the breath of a pearl-diver breaking up from the bottom with his lungs on fire. His back arched off the sand. His hands flew up and clawed at the air. The colour came flooding back into his face, brown and alive, and his eyes cleared, white to grey to the ordinary dark brown of a well-keeper's son.

He breathed again. And again. Little hitching breaths, like a baby's after crying.

He blinked up at the sky.

And then he said, in a small cracked voice, to nobody in particular:

"I'm Shibl."

As if he'd just found it. As if he'd been walking down a long dark road looking for it, and stooped, and picked it up out of the sand, and was surprised by how small it was in his hand.

"I'm Shibl," he said again. "I'm Shibl. Where's—" His face crumpled. "Where's Nadim? I can't find Nadim, he was calling me, he was calling and I couldn't—"

Then he saw Theyab.

---

I understand why he screamed.

Imagine you're seven. You've just come back from the place where there is nothing. You've been somewhere so far under that even the dark is grey, walking down and down a flight of stairs behind something with small neat feet, and someone reached down and took you by the back of the shirt and pulled you up into the air again. And the first thing you see, the very first, is a face bent over yours.

And the face is burning.

Golden cracks running up its neck and across its cheeks, from the jaw to the hairline, like a clay jar in the kiln a breath before it shatters. Light pouring out of the cracks. Light pouring out of its eyes like tears. Its mouth open in a scream with no sound in it, because it's still standing on the top of a dune somewhere under a white moon with its skin splitting.

And beside it, a long pale dog that walked into the Grey and came out again, staring at you with one eye the colour of honey and one the colour of a coin.

Shibl screamed for all of us.

He twisted out from under Theyab's hands and scrambled backward through the sand on his heels and elbows until he hit his brother's knees, and he grabbed Nadim's legs and climbed up them and clung, and screamed, and screamed.

Theyab's light snapped out like a blown lamp.

He knelt there with his hands still raised, slapping at his own arms, his own cheeks, his own chest, putting out a fire that had already gone out. Tears were running down his face. Some of them were his and some belonged to the boy on the dune, and he couldn't have told you which.

The cracks didn't go out. That was the new thing. They faded, but they stayed, faint gold lines up his throat and across his jaw and along both cheekbones, shining softly in the lamplight like the veins in a leaf held up to the sun. Three nights ago they'd only reached his arms.

And across Shibl's cheeks, where Theyab's fingers had been, lay two thin golden lines of his own. Faintly shining.

---

Nadim saw them.

I watched him see them. I watched it all go through him in the space of a breath, the way a flash flood goes through a wadi: everything at once and nothing left standing.

He looked down at his brother, clinging to his legs and sobbing, with gold scored across his face like a brand. He looked at Theyab, cracked open and glowing and weeping light. He looked at me, the dog that walked in the Grey.

And then, I think, he looked at himself. Inside. At the boy who had stood up in the dead grove a quarter of an hour ago and started to walk toward a shimmer between two palms because it had his mother's voice. Who had been two paces from it. Who had had his little brother's sandal in his hand, and had been about to carry it down a flight of stairs that went on forever, and who had been stopped not by his own love, not by his own strength, but by a dog.

He'd been the one who named Shibl the stars. He'd been the one who held him at the burial. And when it came to it, at the very end, in the dark, he'd turned his back on him and walked away toward a voice.

And Laqeet, the foundling, the nobody, the boy with no mother to call him in, had carried his brother out.

You can't live with that at fifteen. You can't live with it at sixty, most people, but at fifteen there's no room in you for it at all. It has to go somewhere. It has to go out.

That's what fear does, little one. And shame. They reach for the oldest word they know.

"*Monster!*"

It cracked out of him like a camel-whip.

"Get away from him! You *monster*, you thing from under the sand, get your hands off my brother, get *away*—"

---

It went into Theyab the way it always did. Like a hook.

He flinched. Just a flinch, as if he'd been slapped. Then he said quietly, the way he'd said it at the well a hundred times, the way he'd said it his whole life:

"I'm not a monster."

My hackles lay flat as a sleeping cat. Not one hair moved. It was true. It was the truest thing anyone said that whole night.

Nobody was looking at my hackles.

They were looking at the gold in his face.

---

The word went round the crescent.

I heard it go. I've heard words go round crowds before, more crowds than you'd believe, in more villages than there are now; I know the sound of it. It's like the sound of a spark going along a fuse. *Monster.* Low, at first. Then louder. A woman to her neighbour. A man to his son. The old men of the majlis to each other, nodding, as if something long argued over had finally been settled.

They'd argued about everything for nine days. Whether the Grey was a sickness or a punishment. Whether the strange dog was smoke-touched or only ugly. Whether the boy had saved the well-keeper or done something to him. Whether to send for help from Liwa, from Buraimi, from anywhere. Whose fault it was. They'd argued in the majlis and the market and at the well until their voices were hoarse, and settled nothing.

And now here it was. One word. And the whole of Hilat al-Sarab, standing at the edge of its own dying, agreed.

It was the first thing they'd agreed on since the colour went out of Umm Hanan's door.

---

"He's calling it," said Ghanim.

He had his camel-stick in both fists. It was trembling.

"You all heard the Digger," he said. "The one who came for him. *A son of the ones from below.* You all heard it. And we chased the Digger off, and we kept the boy, and what came? This." He flung the stick out toward the Grey. "Nine days."

"The Grey came when he went up the dune," said a woman behind him. "I saw him. The night before. Up on the high dune, all alone, with his face to the wind. He was saying something to it."

A murmur went through them. The ugly sound of frightened people finding out they all agree.

Theyab's hand went to his chest.

Because she was right. He had stood on the highest dune and whispered his name to the wind, the name he'd given himself, the only thing he owned. And the wind had said it back. And the grey line had come up on the horizon that same night and the stars above it had gone out.

"Did you?" said Sheikh Mazyad.

He hadn't spoken till then. He stood with his bisht held closed at the throat with one hand, and he looked at Theyab across the lamplight with the tired, hopeless face of a man who already knows.

"Laqeet. Did you say your name to the wind?"

Every denial that came to Theyab's tongue, the feather caught and burned before it reached his lips. I saw him try. I saw him open his mouth to say *no* and close it again, with his hand pressed hard to his chest and his eyes watering.

The boy couldn't lie. You know that by now. He'd never been able to, not about stealing dates or breaking a jar or anything else in his whole small life, and Nadim and the other boys had made a sport of it at the well for years. *Laqeet, do you wet your blanket? Laqeet, do you cry at night? Laqeet, are you afraid of us?* And he'd stand there with his face burning and answer, every time, because the alternative was the feather.

"Yes," he said.

That was all. One word against their one word.

It was enough.

---

I'll say this for Mazyad: he didn't enjoy it. I'd watched him for eight years and he was a weak man, not a cruel one, and that night I saw him understand what was coming and decide not to stand in its way. Villages have been making that choice since before there were villages. Feed one thing to the dark, and the dark will leave the rest alone. It has never once worked. They keep trying.

He opened his mouth to say the words.

He didn't get to. Somebody else moved first.

---

Saqi had got up.

I don't know how. Minutes before, he'd been sitting in the sand like a man with his legs cut out from under him. Now he was standing. Swaying. Alone; the two neighbours had let go of him and stepped back, the way you step back from a man who's about to be sick. He was looking at Theyab.

I want you to remember what Saqi had been through. Six nights before, something had come up the well-rope and put him on like a robe. He'd been gentle in it, strange, kind; his sons had loved him in it more than they'd ever loved him in his own skin, and he knew that now, and he would know it forever. He'd stood over his own son's mat at midnight and said in a doubled voice that the boy was light enough to carry. He'd thrown Nadim through a wall. And then Theyab's light had struck him in his own yard and the thing in him had screamed with his mouth, and used his tongue to say, in Theyab's own voice, *He did this to me.*

Hilal had read the thing out of him. And Saqi had wept for a whole day, remembering everything. Every word he'd said while he was Worn. Every touch.

He'd seen the light come out of the boy and hit him. That's all he'd seen, at the end. From the inside. Light, and pain, and the boy's face.

And now the light had come again, and here was his younger son with gold on his cheeks, screaming.

I smelled him as he stood there. I want you to know this. I put my nose up into the air and I smelled for it, for smokeless fire and wet stone, the smell that had been on him when he was Worn. I wanted it to be there. I wanted it more than I've wanted most things. If it was there, it wasn't him.

It wasn't there.

It was only Saqi. Sweat and old fear and well-water and the sour smell of a man who has been sick for three days and ashamed for longer. Nothing was wearing him. Nothing was moving his arms. There was nothing in him at all but himself.

He bent down, slowly, like an old man.

He picked up a stone from the edge of the lane, a rough brown stone the size of an egg, the kind every lane in Hilat al-Sarab was full of.

He looked at Theyab. Theyab looked back at him. And I saw Theyab understand, a breath before it happened, and I saw him not raise his hands.

"You put it in me," Saqi said. His voice was a whisper. "You. I heard you. You put it *in* me."

He threw it.

---

*End of Chapter Twelve*
