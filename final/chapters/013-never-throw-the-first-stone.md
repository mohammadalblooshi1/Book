# Chapter Thirteen
## Never Throw the First Stone

> *The stone you throw comes home to your own roof; it only takes the long way.*
> — a saying of the oasis, told to boys who are caught with a sling

---

It hit him above the left eye.

I heard it before I saw it. A flat wet sound, like a date dropped on a stone floor. His head went back. Then it came forward again, slowly, and there was a line of blood running down from his eyebrow into his eye, and down his cheek through the gold, so that the cracks in his face shone red.

He didn't put his hand up to it. He didn't put his hands up at all.

For a moment nobody moved. Forty people, with their lamps and their sticks, looking at the well-keeper with his arm still out from the throw, and at the boy kneeling in the sand with his face bleeding. I think, if the moment had gone on one breath longer, it might have broken. I've seen that happen. A crowd wakes up and looks at its own hands and goes home ashamed.

It didn't go on one breath longer.

The second stone came from somewhere at the back. I never saw who threw it. It hit his shoulder. Not hard. A test. The way a boy throws a pebble at a sleeping dog to see whether it bites.

He didn't bite.

And so the third came, and the fourth, and after that I lost count.

---

The old people tell a stoning as if it were a single thing, *they stoned him*, and it isn't. It's a hundred small decisions, each one made by one person alone in the crowd, each one made easier by the one before.

A woman who wouldn't have hurt a lizard picks up a pebble because her neighbour has. A boy throws because his father is watching. An old man throws because he's frightened, and his arm is weak, and the stone falls short, and he's ashamed of falling short, and stoops for another. Nobody decides. Everybody decides. And the stones come.

They came from every side. Rough brown stones from the lanes, and lumps of dried mud, and once a broken piece of a water-jar that cut his ear open. They hit his back and his arms and his ribs. He'd turned his body over Shibl's when the first one came, out of habit, the way you'd cover a child in a sandstorm; and then he understood that Shibl was safe in his brother's arms already, three paces off, and that the stones were for him, and he straightened up.

He knelt there with his hands open on his knees and let them hit him.

"Get up," I said.

I said it the only way I can. I barked at him. I barked the hunting bark, the one that means *now, now, it's running, go*. I seized his shirt and hauled. When he still didn't move I bit his hand. Not hard. Hard enough.

He looked at the blood on his knuckles. He looked at me.

He got up.

---

Sheikh Mazyad had his arm raised. He was saying something. Nobody could hear it over the shouting, and I don't think he was trying very hard to be heard. I could read it on his mouth. I'd heard him say it to beggars and thieves and once to a man who'd moved his neighbour's boundary stones in the night.

*Get out. You have no guest-right here. You never had.*

That was true. My hackles stayed flat. A guest is someone you've chosen. A foundling is someone the sand chose for you.

He didn't run. I want you to understand that. He walked.

Backward at first, slowly, with his face to them, the way you back away from a camel that's thinking of kicking. Then he turned and walked through them, with stones coming at him from every side. They parted to let him through, and closed behind him, the way a fist closes.

A rock struck his back just under the left shoulder-blade. I heard the sound it made. He staggered. He kept walking.

He never raised his hands. Not once. I want that remembered.

---

A man came at him out of the crowd with a camel-stick lifted over his head like a sword.

I'll tell you what I smelled on him, because it matters. I smelled lies. He was shouting as he came, *it was him at my goats, it was him took my goats*, and my hackles went up all down my back, stiff as a brush. Theyab had never touched his goats. His goats had walked into the Grey on the second day, by themselves, bleating without a sound, and he'd watched them go and done nothing because he was a coward. I knew it. The man knew it. He was shouting it anyway, as loud as he could, so that it would be true.

I took him by the ankle.

To the bone. He went down howling. I let go and kept walking and I'm not ashamed of it, little one, and I'll not tell you whose ankle it was. I could smell from the blood. He has grandchildren. Some of them may be at this fire.

After that nobody came close. They threw from further off. Their aim got worse. Their stones got bigger.

---

One of the stones cut the hirz.

I saw it happen. A sharp-edged flint, flung low, that took him across the side of the neck. The cord parted. The little silver case slid down inside his shirt, and out at the bottom where it hung loose over his belt, and fell into the sand, and he didn't notice. He didn't notice anything by then. He was walking the way you walk in the last hour of a long march, one foot, the other foot, with nothing in his head at all.

I picked it up.

It was hot. It burned my tongue. Something from below was near, it was saying, and I knew that already, little one, because I'd been feeling the eyes on us since the dead grove. I carried it anyway. I carried it the way a saluki carries a hare it's been told to bring back and not eat: very gently, at the front of my teeth, with my lips drawn back off it.

I'll tell you later what I did with it. You won't like it. Hilal wouldn't have.

---

They drove him south through the lanes, toward the palms.

He'd slept for eight years in a tent at the south edge of the village, where the last houses gave way to the gardens and the gardens gave way to the sand: a low lean-to of patched goat-hair over two poles, that he'd built himself from scraps other people threw away. Nine patches. I'd counted them, the nights I lay across the door. Each one from a different family, a different year. He'd sewn them together with gut and a bone needle and he was proud of it the way a man is proud of a house.

Some of the young men had run ahead.

When we came round the corner of the date-store, they were already pulling it down. One of them had the front pole and was hauling it out of the ground. Another was slashing at the goat-hair with a knife. They'd thrown his blanket out into the lane and his waterskin after it, and somebody was stamping on the waterskin, deliberately, so it split and the water ran out into the dust and was gone. Water. In Liwa. In the hot months. I'll never forgive that one. Of all of it, that's the one I can't.

Ghanim was there.

Of course he was. He'd slipped out of the crowd as soon as it started moving and gone round by the back lanes, the way a jackal leaves the pack at the kill to go and find the scraps. He was standing over the wreck with a little clay jar in his hand, the one Theyab kept his annas in, a jar with a chipped rim that he'd dug out of the ruins himself.

He tipped it up. The coins slid out into his palm. Not many. A few annas. A boy's whole wealth: half a year of scrap metal and broken glass and old copper weighed on crooked scales and paid at half price.

Ghanim counted them. He was the kind of man who counts even when he's stealing.

"For the dates," he said to the crowd. He didn't look at Theyab. "The dates the dog stole from my stall. Two years of dates. Everyone knows the dog took them."

My hackles stood up like a brush. I growled. I growled so the young men at the tent stepped back from me.

I have never in my life stolen a date from Ghanim. I'll steal from anyone, little one. I've stolen from sheikhs. But not from him. His dates are bad.

He put the annas in his sash.

Then he dropped the jar, and it broke on the gravel, and he walked away.

Theyab watched him go. He didn't say anything. His face, under the blood and the gold, didn't change at all. That was what frightened me most about the whole night: not the stones, not the crowd. That his face didn't change.

---

The crowd stopped at the last house.

I'd known it would. There's a line around every village in the sand, and nobody draws it and everybody knows where it is. Inside it, the lanes and the lamps and the shouting. Outside it, the dark. The stones could follow him past it. The people couldn't. They stood at the edge of the gardens with their lamps held up and their faces gone strange and tired in the light, the way faces go when the shouting is over and there's nothing left to shout about, and they threw the last of their stones out after him into the dark without aiming.

I heard one voice, from somewhere at the back. Small. High. A child's.

*"Laqeet!"*

Just once. Shibl. Calling after him. Not frightened. Not screaming any more. Calling the way a child calls after someone leaving, a guest, an uncle, someone you want to come back tomorrow.

Then a hand went over his mouth. I heard it. I heard the muffle and the little cross wriggle and Nadim's voice, low and broken: *Hush. Hush, he's gone. Hush.*

Theyab stopped walking.

He stood there in the dark with his back to the village and his face to the sand. He didn't turn round. I think if he'd turned round he'd have gone back. I think he knew it.

He started walking again.

---

There was one house we had to pass. At the very end of the last lane, where the gardens began: a small mud house with a baking-plate and a clay oven behind it, and a door that nine days ago had been painted indigo and was now the colour of a cold hearth.

There was a lamp in its window.

All along the edge of the village behind us, as the crowd broke up and drifted home, the lamps were going out one by one. As if the village were closing its eyes. Or as if something were walking from house to house in the dark, pinching the wicks. This one stayed. It burned perfectly still, a small tongue of yellow that didn't so much as flicker, though the night wind was coming off the dunes and rattling the palm fronds all round it. A lamp the wind couldn't put out.

I knew why. I'll tell you another time. She didn't know. She never knew, all her life. She thought it was a good wick.

The door opened as Theyab passed.

---

Umm Hanan came out.

She was very small, with a face folded like a dried fig, and the black burqa'a of an old woman across her nose, and her hands still white with flour at midnight because she couldn't sleep and didn't know what else to do with them. She didn't look at the crowd. She didn't look at the blood.

She walked straight up to Theyab and pressed something into his hands.

Flatbread, still warm from the plate. A thick round of it, and a fistful of dates, all wrapped in a square of faded indigo cloth knotted at the top.

He knew that cloth. He'd guessed whose it was, these last days; he stared down at it, then up at her, and I watched the guess go all the way down into him and become knowing. Eight years of bundles left on her wall with no footprints leading to them. Eight years of honey-dates and bread on the nights when there was nothing else, and him eating them in the dark, thinking the desert had sent them. Thinking it was luck.

It was warm. That's the thing he told Bakhit later, and told it twice, as if he couldn't get past it. It was *still warm*. She'd baked it that night. At midnight. While the stones were flying. She'd heard the shouting and known what it meant, and she'd got up and lit her oven and made bread, because it was the only thing she could do, and she'd done it.

"Eat, child," said Umm Hanan. Her voice was dry and thin and fierce. "The sand remembers kindness too."

She turned to go. Then she turned back, and took hold of his wrist with her floury fingers, and squeezed it, hard, the way the mothers did in the lanes at noon.

"And keep out of the qayla," she said. "Out there there's no shade but what you make. You lie down at noon, under a cloth, under a bush, under anything. You hear me? You don't walk in the heat. *She* walks in the heat."

Her eyes went past him, east, toward the Grey, and I smelled the fear on her, old and dry as paper. She'd heard the *tik, tik* of small hooves on the stones some noon, I think, lying awake with her two remaining names.

"You come in," she said. "Wherever you are. You come in."

It was the noon-call.

Eight years late, at midnight, with the last stones still falling in the gardens behind us, and a bleeding boy on the edge of the sand. But it was the call. It had his face in it. Shibl had called him in once, a child at play; no mother ever had, until then.

Theyab's mouth moved. Nothing came out.

She let go of his wrist. She went back into her house. Before she shut the door, I heard her start to say the names, the way she did every morning over the dough, the way she'd done for all those years. She was saying them for herself. I don't think she knew she was saying them out loud.

She said her husband's name, softly, the way you'd set down a cup. She said her elder son's. Then: "And ——."

She stopped.

She began again, slower, from the beginning. Her husband. Her elder son. "And—"

The door shut.

I stood outside it a long time, little one. Longer than Theyab did. I stood there with the hirz burning in my teeth and tried to say the third name for her, the way you'd try to lift something heavy for an old woman. I'd heard her say it every morning for eight years. I'd heard it through the wall while I lay in the shade of her oven. It was a short name. A boy's name. The younger son. The one whose voice had called to her out of the Grey, the one she'd stepped toward.

——.

It's gone. I go to pick it up and my teeth close on air.

---

He walked till he fell.

Out past the last gardens, past the last palm, onto the sand. Up one dune and down the other side, so that the village was gone behind it and there was nothing in any direction but the dark and the stars. Up the next. Halfway down the far side of that one, his legs folded under him like a newborn camel's and he went down on his face and didn't get up.

I thought for a moment he was dead. I pushed my nose under his cheek. He was breathing. His breath stank of blood.

He rolled over, after a while, and lay on his back with Umm Hanan's bread clutched to his chest in both arms, and looked up at the sky.

"They'll come," he said. His voice was very calm. "The Grey. Or the hoofed thing. Or the Diggers again. Out here. I've got no iron. My knife was in the tent." He laughed. It was a horrible little laugh, like a goat's cough. "I've got nothing. I've got bread."

I lay down against him. Along the whole length of his side, my back to his ribs, the way a saluki lies against the one it loves on a cold night, all its weight at once.

"Where's my hirz?" he said. And then, after a long time, sleepily: "Ghareeb, where's my..."

He was asleep before he finished.

---

Now I'll tell you what I did with it.

I got up very quietly when his breathing slowed. I took the little silver case in my teeth, still warm, still burning faintly against my tongue, and I carried it up to the top of the dune, thirty paces off, and I dug a hole under a tuft of dry grass and I put it in and covered it over.

Then I came back down and lay where I had been, against his side, and waited.

You'll ask me why. Hilal would have asked. Hilal would have called me a fool or worse, and Hilal wasn't wrong about many things. The hirz kept off the ones from below. All of them. It burned when they came near and they couldn't come closer while he wore it.

*All* of them, little one. Do you understand? The silver doesn't ask which ones mean you harm. Iron doesn't ask. The old words Hilal said over the well don't ask. They push back everything from under the sand, the way a fire keeps off the jackals and the stray dog that only wanted to lie down by it.

I knew who was out there. I'd smelled her since the gardens. I'd smelled her all my life.

So I took the silver off him, and I lay down, and I left him open to the dark, and I trusted that the one who came first would be the right one.

It was the worst thing I'd done in a long while. I'd been a good dog for a very long time, and I was tired.

---

The weight came about an hour before the deepest part of the night.

I felt it before he did. The sand under us went cold. Then the air above us went thick, the way the air goes before a shamal, as if someone had laid a wet cloth over the whole sky. And then something settled on his chest.

I couldn't see it. Even with the silver eye I couldn't see it, only the shape it made in the dark: a darker dark, squatting. Heavy. Like a sack of wet sand set down on a sleeping man. His breath went short. His eyes came open. He looked straight up at nothing, and I saw in his face that he couldn't move. Not a finger. Not his lips. His eyes moved, and his eyes were all he had, and they were rolling toward me.

The Sitter. You'll hear a great deal more about it before we're done. It had come to him before, on other nights, though he never knew its name. It would come again.

I couldn't get up. That's the thing about the Sitter: it doesn't sit on one of you. It sits on the whole night. I lay against him and strained to lift my head and my head stayed on the sand as if someone had poured lead into my skull. I growled. Even the growl came out flat and small, the growl of a dog in a dream.

His breath was going. A little hiss. A littler one.

And then the weight lifted.

Not slowly. All at once, the way a bird goes off a branch. One moment it was there, crushing; the next it was gone, and the air was clean and cold again, and Theyab dragged in a breath so huge and ragged it sounded like weeping.

And then the warmth came.

I don't know how else to tell it. It came up the slope out of the dark and lay down along his other side, the side I wasn't on, and the cold went out of the sand under him. He was shivering, and then he wasn't. His breathing slowed. His eyes closed. His face, with the blood dried black on it and the gold faint under the blood, went smooth and young, the way it hadn't been since he was very small.

And there was a smell.

Milk. Warm milk, the way a she-camel's milk smells in the bowl in the first light, sweet and grassy and faintly sour. In the middle of the empty sand, a day's walk from any camel, at the coldest hour of the night.

He smiled in his sleep. He said something. A word, two words, in a soft quick tongue that no one in Hilat al-Sarab had ever been able to understand, the one he'd talked in his sleep since the first night I watched him in the lane. I understood it.

I won't tell you what it was. It wasn't for me.

---

I lifted my head and looked over him.

There was nothing lying along his other side. Only the sand, faintly dented, and warm. And at the top of the dune, against the stars, the shape of a gazelle.

Standing quite still, watching him. The long ears turned toward him. The thin legs straight. And her eyes, in the starlight, gold. Not the dull gold of an animal's eyes in a lamp. Gold like his cracks. Gold like the light that comes off the feather.

She looked at me.

I looked back. With both eyes. And I put my head down on my paws, politely, the way a son does who's seen his mother across the market and knows she doesn't want to be greeted.

I've known her since the first night of his life, little one. I won't tell you about that night. Not yet. It's the oldest secret I carry, older than the eye, and I've carried it a long way, and it isn't mine to put down.

When I lifted my head again, she was gone.

---

He woke before dawn.

He woke the way he always did, all at once, with his fist closed. He sat up. He touched his face, where the blood had dried, and the cut over his eye, which had closed in the night cleaner than any cut should close. He looked at me.

Then he looked at the sand.

All around where he'd been lying, in a ring, there were prints.

Not round cups, two by two, the soft pale prints of the thing in the dead grove. These were small and sharp-edged and split down the middle, each one like a heart cut in two with a knife. Cloven. A gazelle's. They came down off the dune, and went round him once, close, and round again, and then lay down along his side where the sand was still dented, and got up, and went back over the dune.

He stared at them.

"Ghareeb," he said. "Ghareeb, look. Something was here."

I looked. I looked with my polite face. I yawned.

He put his hand flat in one of the prints. It was the size of his thumb.

"It smelled of milk," he said. "I dreamed. I dreamed something smelled of milk and the weight came off. Something was sitting on me, and it came off." He looked up the dune. "Was it a gazelle? There aren't any gazelles here. There haven't been gazelles near Hilat al-Sarab since before I came."

I didn't answer. You know by now I can't.

But I could have said: *There's been one. There's been one for eight years. She watches you from the ruin wall at noon, when you're scavenging, and you've never once looked up.*

He sat a long time with his hand in the print.

Then I went up the dune and dug out the hirz from under the dry grass, and brought it back down, and dropped it in his lap.

He looked at it. Then at me. He didn't say anything. He knotted the two ends of the cut cord together and put it back round his neck. It was cool now. Quite cool, like any piece of silver in the morning.

---

We went back to the tent while it was still dark.

I don't know why. I think he couldn't think where else to go. The wreck was where he'd left it, at the south edge of the gardens, a flat dark heap of goat-hair and pole in the starlight. There was nobody about. The village was asleep, or pretending.

They'd done more to it after we'd gone.

Someone had poured salt all round it. A thick white ring, unbroken, glittering faintly in the starlight. That's what the grandmothers do round a place where one of the ones from below has been, so that whatever lived there can't follow you home. And in the middle of the ring, on the flattened goat-hair, someone had pressed a hand in soot. Five black fingers spread wide. A small hand. A woman's.

*What was here is not of us.*

Theyab stood at the edge of the salt and looked at it for a long time.

I stepped over the ring. Quite deliberately. I walked into the middle of the wreck, onto the soot hand, and turned round three times, and lay down on it, and yawned. You see the trouble with salt, for something like me. It's mostly for show.

He didn't laugh. But he stepped over the ring after me.

---

He knelt in the wreckage and dug through it with his hands, quietly, carefully, the way he dug through the ruins at noon. A scavenger to the last.

He found the twist of waxed paper first: four dates soaked in honey, the ones he'd been saving. Somebody had stepped on them. They were squashed flat and full of grit. He brushed them off and put them inside his shirt with Umm Hanan's bread.

He found the knife. They'd missed it. It was under the blanket-roll, a short rusty blade he'd found in the ruins at the age of nine and sharpened on a stone ever since. He held it up to the starlight, and looked at it, and put it in his belt. Iron. Not much. Enough to sleep on.

Then he found the cloth.

It was under the broken tent-pole, folded small, where he always kept it. A square of grey fabric, softer than anything else he owned, woven over with small strange marks no woman in Hilat al-Sarab had ever been able to name. Not a pattern. Something more like writing. Something more like names. He'd had it with him the noon he woke in the lane, they told him, wrapped round his shoulders, though he didn't remember. He'd slept with it under his head every night of his life since.

He held it up to his face.

Then he went still.

"It smells of smoke," he said.

It always had. He'd simply never noticed. You don't notice the smell of your own tent. Burnt cloth, very old, very faint, deep in the weave, the way the smell of a fire stays in a goat-hair wall for years after the camp has moved.

I could have told him what kind of smoke. I'm glad I don't have the words. I'd smelled it on the first night of his life, rolling across the dunes from the place where his family had been, along with another smell under it: the one that clings to the robes of men who burn their own names.

He folded the cloth small and put it inside his shirt, against the feather, with the bread and the ruined dates.

"That's everything," he said.

A rusty knife, a grey cloth, some bread, four ruined dates, a knotted hirz, and a dog. Eight years in Hilat al-Sarab, and that was everything.

---

He stood up in the middle of the salt ring and looked back at the village.

At its eastern edge the Grey lay over the houses like a shroud over a man laid out for washing. The rest was dark. Every lamp out. Every door shut.

All but two.

One was the small lamp in the window of the house with the oven, burning perfectly still, the way it had burned all night. The other was out at the far western edge, past the last of the houses, a single stubborn glow through the black goat-hair walls of a tent hung with herbs and bells.

Bakhit was awake.

Theyab looked at that light for a long time.

"I saw it," he said. He wasn't talking to me. He was saying it to the dark. "When I healed him. I saw myself. On a dune. Burning. I couldn't stop it and I couldn't scream." His voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it. "All those stories he told me. The children with the feathers. All those years. He *knew*."

I couldn't answer that. But I came and leaned against his leg, the way a saluki leans, all our weight on you at once. And every hair on my back lay flat and still in the cold.

He put his hand on my head.

Then he stepped out of the salt ring and started walking. Not away from the village. Toward it. Toward the light at its western edge.

They always do, little one. They always walk toward the light. That's the whole trouble with them.

---

*End of Chapter Thirteen*
