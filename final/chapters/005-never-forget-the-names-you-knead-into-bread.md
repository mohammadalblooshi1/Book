# Chapter Five
## Never Forget the Names You Knead Into Bread

> *"What you say every morning is a wall. Stop saying it and see what comes in."*
> — Umm Hanan

---

The Grey came the way cold comes into a tent.

Cold never charges. On a winter night it comes in at the edges first, under the hem of the tent wall, then along the mats, up through the blanket. And then there's a moment, always, when you realise you can't remember the last time you were warm. And then a worse moment, when you can't remember what warm was. Or why you'd want it.

That's how it came into Hilat al-Sarab. Three mornings after the Diggers.

I was where I always was before the doves: lying in the dark of the back lane, outside Umm Hanan's yard, with my nose on my paws and my ears on the sounds of her oven. I'd lain there every morning for eight years. Theyab didn't know. He thought I went off hunting jerboas. Let him.

She came out with the basin on her hip and the lamp in her other hand, the way she always came, and set the basin down by the iron plate and turned to shut her door behind her.

And stopped.

---

Her door was indigo. Her husband painted it the year they married, the blue of the sea a long way out where it goes dark. Every spring she'd talked about having it painted again and never had, because it was the last thing in the house his hands had touched with any care.

It wasn't blue.

It was the flat grey of old ash in a hearth nobody's lit for a year. Not faded. *Grey*, all through. The grain was still there, and the smudge by the latch where her thumb had pressed ten thousand times. Only the colour was gone, the way a word is gone when you reach for it and find a smooth place.

She stood with her hand on the latch and looked at it.

Behind the door, through the gap at its foot, her lamp was burning in its niche. I could see the light. Gold. Steady. It hadn't changed at all.

She put her hand flat on the grey wood. Then she took it away and looked at her palm, as if the grey might have come off on her skin like soot.

It hadn't.

She didn't say anything. She went back to the basin and knelt and pushed up her sleeves and began to knead.

---

You know what she said, kneading. I've told you. Every morning, in the dark, in the same order, in time with the heels of her hands. Three names. Pushed down into the dough like salt.

She said the first.

I won't give it to you. I've told you why.

She said the second. Her elder son. Her hands folded the dough over and pressed down.

And then she reached for the third.

I heard her reach. I heard her breath go in, the way it always went in before the third name, a little deeper, because the third was her youngest and the one who went over the side last, running to catch the others, and she always said his name a little more softly than the rest, the way you'd lay a baby down.

Her breath went in.

And she said:

"——"

---

I'm trying, little one.

Do you understand? I'm trying now, as I tell you this. I had it. I heard it every morning for eight years, lying in the dark of that lane. I could have said it in her voice. I could have told you how it sat in her mouth, which part she leaned on, how the end of it went up a little, like a question. I had it as surely as I have my own four feet.

I reach for it and there's a place.

Not a dark place. Not an empty one. A *smooth* one. Like the socket where a tooth was, that your tongue keeps going back to, and back to, and finding nothing, and going back again. I know there was a name. I know exactly where it was. I know its shape the way you know the shape of a doorway in the dark. I go to step through it and there's only wall.

Her youngest son. Fourteen years old. He ran to the side of the boat to catch his father and his brother, and a voice from the water said his name kindly, and he went over.

His name was ——.

There. You see. I can't.

I'll try again later. Remind me. I'll try at every place in this story where I'm supposed to say it, and every time, I'll fail, and you'll see the place where it should be. I want you to see it. I want you to see what was eaten.

---

Her hands stopped in the dough.

She knelt there with her arms in the basin to the wrist, not moving. The fire under the plate crackled. A dove started up somewhere in the palms, and stopped.

She said the first name again. Clearly. The second. Her hands pressed down. And then her breath went in for the third, and she said:

"——"

And stopped.

She tried it a third time. Her lips shaped something. Her throat moved. Nothing came out of it but breath.

I watched her face. It didn't crumple. It didn't weep. It went very still and very careful, the way a woman's face goes when she's walking across a floor in the dark and has just put her foot down on a place where there should be a board. She was feeling for the edge of it. Feeling how big the hole was.

Then she did something I'll never forget. She took her hands out of the dough, and wiped them slowly on her abaya, and folded them in her lap. And she sat back on her heels in the dark of her yard, by the grey door, with her lamp burning gold behind it, and she began, very quietly, to count on her fingers.

One. Her husband.

Two. Her elder boy.

Three.

She looked at the third finger for a long time.

She knew there was a third. She knew it the way you know you've left something on the fire. She just couldn't find what.

---

Theyab came to the wall at sunrise for the bread. It wasn't a bread day. He came anyway. I think he'd felt it in the night, the way I had: the cold coming in under the hem.

There was no bundle. Instead, at the back gate of her yard, there was Umm Hanan.

She was standing in the lane. She'd never stood in the lane where he could see her. In eight years she'd never once let him catch her at the wall. And now she stood there in the grey light with flour on her abaya to the elbows and her hands knotted together in front of her, waiting, and when he came round the corner of the wall she looked straight at him.

He stopped dead. He didn't know what to do with his face. Nobody waited for him. Nobody had ever waited for him.

"You can't lie," said Umm Hanan.

It wasn't a question. I don't know how she knew. Perhaps she'd heard it through the frond wall, all those noons. Perhaps she'd just watched him for eight years from behind her door the way I'd watched her.

Theyab said, "No."

"Then tell me." Her voice was low and rough. "Did I have three? Three men, on the boat?"

He looked at her a long moment.

"Yes," he said.

She closed her eyes. I watched something go through her: not grief, exactly. Relief, almost. The relief of a woman who has been told she isn't mad.

"Do you know the third one's name?"

"No," said Theyab. "I never heard it."

"No," she said. "Nobody did. I never said it out loud. Not in forty years. Not to anyone but the bread." She opened her eyes. "And now the bread's forgotten it too."

She looked past him then, down the lane, toward the east. I turned my head and looked with her.

The lane ran down between the houses toward the well, and past the well toward the eastern edge of the village, where the last houses faced the open sand and the palm grove. Yesterday at dusk it had been red and gold and brown, the colours of mud brick and dust and dates. Now, at the far end, it was grey.

Not shadowed. Not misted. Grey. The way her door was grey. The walls, the palm trunks, the hanging dates, the dust in the lane, a rope coiled on a doorstep, a child's cloth doll forgotten on a wall. All of it the flat colour of cold ash. And at the edge of it, where the grey met the gold, there wasn't a line. The colour just thinned out, the way milk thins when you add water, a little more every pace, until there wasn't any left.

"Is it coming here?" said Umm Hanan.

And Theyab, who couldn't lie, said, "Yes."

---

It went in three steps that day, as near as I could tell. I've seen it do it since, in other places, with other children, and it always goes the same way. Colour first. Then sound. Then smell.

Colour, you've seen.

Sound went at noon.

The eastern lanes had been grey since dawn, and people had gone in and out of them all morning, quickly, not looking, the way you walk past a man lying in the road. By noon the grey had crept another three houses west. And the mothers called their children in, as they always did. But on the eastern side, the calls went wrong.

I heard one. A woman on the edge of the grey, leaning out of a grey doorway, calling into a grey lane. Calling the way they all called. *Come in! Come in from the sun!* And then a name.

Only the name came out as nothing. Her mouth shaped it. Her breath pushed it. And it went out of her into the grey lane and died a hand's width from her lips, like a spark falling into wet sand. No sound at all.

Her little boy was standing in the lane, ten paces off, in the grey, playing with a stick. He didn't look up. He didn't come. He didn't hear his name, because his name had not reached him. He went on playing with the stick, in the noon, in the empty lane, while his mother called and called with her mouth working and no sound coming out, louder, louder, her face going dark with the effort of it.

The child no one is calling.

I went. Theyab was behind me, but I was faster. I went down that lane into the grey and took the boy's shirt in my teeth and pulled him, stumbling and whining, out of the grey and up to his mother's door, and she snatched him in and slammed it.

I stood in the lane afterward and shook myself, the way you shake off water. It didn't help.

Being in the grey is like being underwater. Not wet. *Under*. Everything pressed close and muffled. The light comes from nowhere. Your own heartbeat sounds very far away. And when you come out again, for a while, the world is too loud, and too bright, and you're not entirely sure it's yours.

---

Then the smell went.

That was the one that frightened me. You have to understand what that means to a dog.

Your world is mostly eyes, little one. Mine is mostly nose. I know a lane by what's lain in it: goat and bread and woodsmoke and the little dead lizard under the third step of the potter's house. I know a man by the oil in his beard and what he ate for supper. When I walk through a village I'm walking through a thousand stories at once, all of them talking.

In the grey, at the end of that day, I put my nose down to a doorstep.

Nothing.

*Nothing.* As if someone had scraped the step clean of everything that had ever happened on it. A wall. Nothing. A heap of dung, fresh that morning, grey as everything else. I put my nose right into it, the way you'd shout into a well to hear the echo.

No echo.

I stood in the middle of the grey lane with my nose full of nothing, and for a moment, a long moment, I didn't know where I was. I mean that. I didn't know which way the well was. I didn't know which way the boy was. I didn't know which way was *out*. I'd lost the world. The world had gone silent in my nose the way a room goes dark when the last lamp dies.

I don't know how long I stood there.

Then a sound. Very faint, from the edge of the grey. Somebody saying a word.

"Ghareeb."

His voice. Calling. Not loud. Not very sure. The way you'd call into a dark well when you aren't sure there's anything at the bottom.

"Ghareeb. Come out of there."

My name. Said by someone who wanted me. In the lane. Like a mother at noon.

I came out.

---

There was a goat in the square that evening.

It had come out of the grey part of the village, wandered across the line, the way goats do, following its nose toward the well. It stood by the trough. It was grey. Not grey-haired: grey all through, like the door, like the dung, the colour of cold porridge. Its eyes were white. Not the yellow of a goat's eyes, with that strange flat bar across them. White, like the inside of a boiled egg.

It stood with its head at a slight angle, as if listening to something under the ground.

A girl threw a date-stone at it. It didn't flinch. It turned its white eyes slowly toward her. And it opened its mouth wide, very wide, wider than a goat's mouth ought to go, and it bleated.

You know the sound: that high, cracked, foolish *meh-eh-eh* that carries half a mile on a still evening.

Nothing came out.

Its throat worked. Its ribs pumped. Its mouth stood open, wide, pink inside, and its whole body shook with the bleat, and there was no sound at all. The square was full of people, and the pulley was creaking, and a baby was crying somewhere, and that goat stood by the trough screaming at the top of its lungs in perfect silence.

The girl's mother took her inside.

The goat stood there till dark, its mouth opening and closing on nothing, looking at the door she'd gone through.

---

That night the village argued about me.

Not about the grey. Nobody would argue about the grey. You don't argue about the weather; you just bar your door against it. They argued about me, in Sheikh Mazyad's majlis, with the coffee going round and the lamps lit and the door open to let out the smoke, and Theyab and I sat outside in the dark against the wall and heard every word.

It was Ghanim who started it.

"That dog," he said. "That's what I want to know about. That dog walked out of the noon with him. Eight years ago. You all saw. Nobody owned it. Nobody's ever owned it. It doesn't eat our food. It doesn't age. My father's father had a saluki, and it was grey at the muzzle by its ninth year. That dog is the same dog it was the day it came. Look at the eye on it."

"It's an old dog," said somebody.

"It's an old *something*." Ghanim's voice was smooth and reasonable and warm. "Did you see it in the lane, the night the strangers came? Did you see it take a grown man down by the knee with one leg broken under it? That's not a dog. That's a dog the way the boy is a boy."

My hackles rose. Not all the way. He believed some of it. That was the worst part. Ghanim believed a little of everything he said, which is what made him good at saying it.

"Smoke-touched," said an old man by the hearth. "That's what my grandmother would have called it. A dog the ones from below have breathed on."

"Then it's dangerous."

"It saved the boy."

"It saved *its own*. That's not the same."

"It went into the grey today. I saw it. Walked straight in, after the potter's son, and walked out again with the boy in its teeth. Who walks out of the grey?"

"Something that came from it," said Ghanim.

There was a silence in the majlis. I heard the coffee cups click.

"I'll tell you what I'd do," said Ghanim. "And I'm a reasonable man. I wouldn't kill it. Killing a thing like that, who knows what comes for you after. I'd sell it. To the coast. The pearl merchants pay well for a good saluki, and they sail for India in the autumn. Let it go to India. Let it go across the sea. Let it be *someone else's* old something."

"And the boy?"

"The boy," said Ghanim, and his voice went softer, "is another matter. Men have been asking after the boy."

My hackles went all the way up, then. I growled. In the dark, against the wall, so low it was almost nothing. Theyab put his hand on my back and kept it there.

Inside, Sheikh Mazyad cleared his throat. He said it was late. He said there was no need to decide anything tonight. He said they'd see. He said a great many things, and none of them were *no*.

They never settled it, not then and not ever. Was the dog smoke-touched? Was it the boy's, or was the boy its? Sold, or killed, or left? It went round the majlis with the coffee and round the well with the water, and nobody ever came down on one side. And that, little one, was worse than if they had. A thing you decide about, you can stop thinking about. A thing you never settle sits in the corner of your house, getting bigger.

Theyab didn't say a word all through it. But when they'd finished, and the door of the majlis swung shut, he leaned down with his face against my neck in the dark and said, very quietly, so that only I could hear:

"You're mine."

It was true. My hackles knew it. I'd have told him, if I could, that it was the other way round as well.

---

I'll give you its name now. It has no use for its own.

I didn't see it that night. Not with the amber eye. I saw it later, with the other, and I'm going to tell you about it here because this is where it belongs.

It is called Ash-Mouth.

It doesn't have a body that you would know as one. It has an appetite, and the appetite has a shape, and the shape, when my silver eye found it from the edge of the grey a few nights later, was something like a mouth. A long, low, grey mouth lying along the eastern edge of the village like a dune lies along the edge of a valley. Without teeth. It doesn't need teeth. It doesn't bite. It *mouths*, the way an old toothless man mouths his bread, slowly, softly, patiently, gumming it into nothing.

What it eats is names.

Not the sounds. The sounds don't matter to it. It eats what the names are *for*. The rope between a mother and her son. The thread that runs from a man's mouth to his wife's ear when he says her name in the dark. Where Ash-Mouth has fed, the threads are gone. The people are still there. The names are still there, mostly, for a while. But they don't go anywhere any more. They don't reach. A mother looks at her son across the yard and says his name and it falls a hand's width from her lips, and he doesn't look up, and slowly, slowly, she stops being sure why she's looking at him.

People forget each other. That's the Grey. That's all it is. That's all it needs to be.

It's the first of them. The Sealed King's mouths. There are others. You'll meet them.

I wish you didn't have to.

---

That night the houses were frightened.

I don't mean the people. I mean the things that live in the houses.

You don't know about those. Nobody tells children any more. In every old house in the sands, in the walls, under the floor, in the dark corner behind the water-jar, there's something living. Not a person. Not a ghost. A Dweller, the old books call them. One of the ones from below, but not a wandering one, not a hungry one. One that came up a long time ago and found a house it liked and stayed, the way a cat stays. Most of them do nothing. Most of them just live there, quietly, eating the warmth and the smell of bread and the sound of children, and asking nothing.

That night they were frightened.

All night I heard it. A pot off a shelf. A water-jar tipping over in the dark with a long gulping splash. Barred doors rattling against their bars from the inside. Babies woke screaming. Mothers got up and stood in the dark with their hearts pounding, looking at the broken pot on the floor, and didn't know why it had fallen, and said a word to the Creator, and got back into bed, and lay awake.

I knew why it had fallen.

I lay in the lane with my silver eye half open and watched them go. Small grey movements, low to the ground, along the foot of the walls, out of the houses on the eastern side. Fleeing west. Like mice out of a granary before a flood. Not running *from* anything you could see. Running from what was coming. The ones that stayed knocked their pots over in their fear and huddled in the corners behind the water-jars, and their houses felt colder and colder around them, and by morning some of those corners were empty, and I don't know where the things that had lived in them went.

And all night, all over Hilat al-Sarab, the lamps guttered.

Every lamp in every niche in every house. The flames went low and blue and sideways, the way flames go when the door's left open in a high wind, although every door was barred and the night was still. I saw them through the gaps under the doors, one after another down the lanes: little gold eyes going dim, going blue, going out.

All but one.

---

Behind the grey door at the end of the back lane, Umm Hanan's lamp burned all night.

Straight. Gold. Tall. Not a flicker in it. I lay outside her yard and watched the light come steady and warm through the gap at the foot of the ash-coloured wood, and once, near the worst of the dark, when every other lamp in the village was guttering blue, I let my silver eye open all the way, and I looked.

It was there. The thing I'd seen the night the Diggers came. Tall and thin and patient, standing by the niche with the little clay lamp in it. Bending over the flame, the way you bend over a sleeping child, with what might have been hands cupped round it. And all around the outside of her house, along the walls and the doorstep and the roof, I could see the grey pressing in. Leaning on the house. The way water leans on a dam. Trying the walls with soft grey fingers, testing for a crack.

It couldn't get in.

Not *wouldn't*. *Couldn't*. Ash-Mouth could eat the blue off her door. It could eat the third name out of her bread. But it couldn't come through the walls of that house while that tall thin thing stood by the lamp.

Do you know why?

I told you I'd tell you. Here it is. Every morning, for forty years, before she poured the boiling water from her pot out onto the ground of her yard, Umm Hanan had bent over it and named the Creator, and told whatever might be lying under the earth there to move aside. Every single time. Ten thousand mornings. In case something was sleeping under her yard that a splash of boiling water would scald.

Something was.

And it never once got scalded. Not in forty years. And a thing from below doesn't forget that, any more than a dog forgets who fed it. It had watched her do it, ten thousand times. And when the dark came for her house, it stood up.

I call it the Lamp-Keeper. It doesn't have a name it would give me. It doesn't need one.

The next morning, Umm Hanan knelt by her basin and said her husband's name, and her elder son's name, into the dough. Clearly. Both of them. And reached for the third, and found the smooth place, and said "——," and went on kneading.

Two names. While every other woman in the eastern lanes was standing in her doorway staring at a husband whose name she'd known for thirty years and could not now say. She held two. She held them a whole day longer than anyone else in that village held anything.

One day.

You'll think that isn't much. You'd be wrong. You'll see what one day bought.

---

At dusk there was a man at the well.

I didn't know his name then. Afterwards I learned he mended ropes. He lived on the eastern side, in a house that had gone grey two days before, and he'd gone into his own yard that afternoon to fetch a tether for his goat, and he'd been in there a long time. Too long. His wife had called him. I heard her. I heard her call and call, at the edge of the grey, and I heard the calls go out of her mouth and die.

At dusk he came out.

He walked across the square to the well. Slowly. Not stiffly; easily, the way a man walks when he's at peace. The square was full: women with their jars, men coming back from the palms, children who'd been let out after the heat. People stepped aside for him without quite knowing why. He came to the stone lip of the well and stood there with his hands loose at his sides, and he looked down into the dark of it.

He was grey. All of him. His beard, his dishdasha, his skin. And he was smiling.

Not a big smile. A small, gentle, pleased smile, the smile of a man who has just remembered something nice.

"Bring the bucket," he said.

Saqi looked up from his stool.

"Bring the bucket," the man said again. Pleasantly. To nobody. To the well. "The bucket. Bring the bucket, the bucket."

His wife was coming across the square toward him. Fast. Her abaya flying. Her face all one thing, the way a face goes when it's been calling and calling and not been heard. She took hold of his arm.

"——," she said. His name. Her mouth shaped it. Nothing came out.

He turned his head and looked at her. His eyes were white. Boiled-egg white, all through, like the goat's.

"Bring the bucket," he told her kindly. "The bucket. Bring the bucket."

She held on to his arm. She stared up into his face. Her lips were moving, trying, trying, shaping a name that wouldn't come, and the square had gone dead silent around them, and Theyab beside me had gone stiff as a tent pole, and every hair on my back was standing up, and I didn't know why, because nothing in the square was lying.

That's the thing about the Grey. It doesn't lie. It doesn't need to.

The woman's lips stopped moving.

She stood there holding her husband's arm. Her face went slowly smooth. All the wanting went out of it, the way water goes out of a skin with a hole in it. And she turned her head, and looked down into the well beside him. And the corners of her mouth went up.

"Bring the bucket," she said.

Softly. Pleasantly. In her own voice.

"The bucket. Bring the bucket, the bucket."

---

*End of Chapter Five*
