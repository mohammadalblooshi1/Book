# Chapter One
## Never Walk at Noon

> *"Come in from the sun, or the Noon Mare will take you."*
> — what the mothers of Hilat al-Sarab call at noon

---

*The teller said…*

*That is how he begins. He says it to the fire first, the way a man greets his host before the other guests.*

*It is the first night of winter at the camp in Liwa, and the cold has come down off the dunes faster than anyone expected. The fire is ghaf wood. It burns slow and sweet and throws a red circle on the sand, and the children have pressed into that circle as close as their mothers allow. There are eleven of them. Count them; he does. His lips move over each small face as if he were telling beads. Above them the stars are spilled so thick that the sky looks wet, and Suhail stands low and red in the south, the herders' star, the one that means the heat is broken.*

*He will not let the boys pour the coffee. He takes the dallah from the eldest boy's hands without a word and pours himself, and his hands shake, and the coffee runs over the rim of the cup and across his knuckles and hisses in the sand. He does not look at the burn. He pours the next cup and spills that one too.*

*"The teller s-said…" He stops. His jaw works on the word as if it were gristle. The children wait. They have heard he stammers. They have not heard that it is worst at the beginning, when the story is still deciding whether to let him in.*

*"The teller said," he tries again, and this time it comes, "that there was a boy in the sands of Liwa who had no name, and a dog who had too many. Hold your breath, little ones."*

*The smallest girl puffs out her cheeks and holds it until her face goes dark, and lets it go in a gust that makes the fire lean.*

*"You'll say it's a tale," the old man tells them. "Your fathers said so. Their fathers said so, and they were wrong, and they are dead, and I am not. I tell you it happened. I was there." He sets the dallah down in the embers. "I was younger than some of you. And stupider than all of you put together."*

*At his feet lies a saluki.*

*It is the oldest dog any of them has ever seen. The feathering on its ears has gone white as salt. One of its eyes is amber, the colour of a date held up to a lamp, and it watches the children steadily and without much interest. The other eye is gone. Over the place where it was, the lid is sealed shut by an old scar, puckered and shining, like the seam where a leather bag has been sewn closed by someone in a hurry.*

*The children cannot stop looking at that seam. The eldest boy swears, later, that when the fire jumped he saw something under it catch the light. Something thin and bright, like a coin at the bottom of a well. The others say he is lying, and then, one by one, over the nights that follow, each of them sees it too.*

*The old man puts his hand on the dog's head, very gently, the way you touch a thing you owe money to.*

*"I won't tell it my way," he says. "My way has too much of me in it. I'll tell it the way the dog would tell it. The dog saw all of it. All of it." His thumb moves once over the scar. "And the dog forgives less than I do."*

*"How long is it?" asks the eldest boy.*

*"As long as the winter. Each night I'll carry you as far as I can, and then I'll put you down, and what he found there, I'll tell tomorrow, if the wind lets me." He looks into the fire. "Tonight I'll give you the end first. So you know what you're walking toward. So none of you can say I didn't warn you."*

*The saluki sighs through its nose. Out past the firelight the wind comes over the dunes and touches the edge of the circle, and goes away, as if it had only come to count.*

---

He is burning.

The moon stands full above Tal Moreeb, the badr, so swollen it looks like something that has fed. The great dune of Liwa lies under it, the highest sand in all the world, and on its crest a boy stands with his arms open, as if someone has just told him to catch.

It doesn't begin at his hands. It begins in the chest, under the small gold mark he has carried all his life, and it splits him the way a date-stone splits in wet ground: from the inside, because the thing in it has grown too big to stay. Light comes through the cracks of him. It runs along his arms and up his throat in lines like gold thread pulled through cloth. It comes out of his eyes, and his eyes go with it.

There is a smell. The songs never mention it. Honey, and hot metal, and under those, faint and patient, hair. My nose is a great deal better than yours, little one. I smell it still. I will smell it when I am dust.

He doesn't scream.

I used to think that was courage. Lately I think the fire takes the scream first, the way a thief cuts the bell off a camel's neck before he leads her away into the dark.

And somewhere below him on the slope, someone is shouting a name, over and over, and the wind is taking down every letter of it.

---

But that is the end.

I have to start before the beginning, in a place I have never once described to anyone, and you'll have to forgive me if I do it badly. I'm a dog. We aren't made for describing. We're made for running at things.

But first you'll want to know what I am. Everyone does, and no one ever likes the answer.

I am a saluki: the old hunting dog of the Bedouin, lean as a tent-pole, feathered at the ears and tail. They call me Ghareeb. The stranger. It's the one name of mine people ever got right. Back then I had one amber eye and one silver one, and the silver one looked at things I would rather it hadn't. We'll come to that eye. Don't hurry me toward it.

I can't speak to you. I couldn't speak to him. Everything you'll hear is the inside of an old dog's head.

What I can do is smell a lie. A lie smells sour, like milk on the turn. When I smell it, my hackles rise from skull to tail and a growl starts in my chest before I can stop it. The truth smells of nothing. That's the trouble with the truth, little one. It never raises the hair on anyone's back.

---

There was a well where the water was sweet.

That's the first piece. Start there.

Not a well the way you know wells: a hole, a rope, a stone lip worn smooth by hands. It was the other end. The bottom. A basin of pale stone, the water so clear you could see the grain of the rock beneath, turning slowly the way a cat turns before it lies down. When the boy was thirsty he only had to put his hands near it, and the water rose. It came up out of the basin of its own accord and filled his cupped palms and stopped exactly at the brim and did not spill one drop. He never once lowered a bucket. He did not know what a bucket was.

I drank from that basin for seven years and it never tasted of iron, or of rope, or of anything that had died in it.

---

The ghaf tree, little one. The one green thing standing alone in the sand when everything else has given up. The herders will tell you its root goes down thirty men deep. They're proud of that, the way people are proud of things they've never seen.

Did you ever wonder what it's reaching for?

Down there, the roots came through the ceiling. Ghaf roots, thick as the pillars of a fort, coming down out of a roof of sand held up by nothing, and between them, halls. Long quiet halls of root and pale stone, going away in every direction, warm as a tent in the early morning when everyone is still asleep. He used to run between the roots and slap each one as he passed, and each gave back a low sound, like a camel grumbling in its sleep.

He thought the roots were holding up the world. In a manner of speaking he was right.

---

There was light, and no sun.

It didn't come from anywhere. It was in the air the way warmth is in a blanket, the colour of milk with a little honey stirred into it, and it never moved across the floor, because nothing was moving it. Nothing cast a shadow. Do you understand what that means? A child who has never seen his own shadow. When he came up, later, into our sun, and saw that black thing lying at his feet and following him everywhere, he screamed. For a whole season he would not walk with the sun behind him.

No. That isn't right. It was the colour of the inside of a pearl. Or it was blue.

I have told myself this so many times, on so many nights, that I no longer know which pieces I saw and which pieces I have only polished. An old dog dreams, little one. An old dog dreams harder than you would believe. Keep that in your pocket. You may need to take it out later and look at it.

---

There was a gazelle.

Her eyes were gold, the way a coin is gold when you hold it to the fire. And wherever she had been, the air was warm and smelled of milk, as if a tent had just been opened after the evening milking.

Sometimes she was a gazelle. Sometimes, when the boy was very small and crying, she was a woman. Or something that had heard about women and had decided to try it, kindly, for his sake. She had the gazelle's eyes even then. She held him against her and she was warm, and when he had stopped crying she put him down very carefully on the sand, the way you set down a full cup.

She never said a word. Not once in seven years. I never heard her voice.

I won't tell you what I called her. Not yet. Some names you don't give away, even to someone you love, even in the dark.

---

There was a voice.

There was no dawn down there. But there was a time when the light grew thicker, the way a fire grows when someone feeds it, and that was when the boy woke. And at that time, every time, a voice at his ear said:

*Wake up.*

Only that. Not a man's voice or a woman's. It came from no one, and it said *wake up*, and he woke, and stretched, and rolled over onto me, because I was always there, and pushed his cold feet under my belly.

I want you to remember that voice. I'm going to ask you about it, much later, when it's late and you're frightened and you can't tell one voice from another.

---

There were others.

Something that came down the root-halls like a breeze and turned before it touched him, so that his hair moved and nothing else did. Something that stood where one hall became another, very still, the way an old man stands at the mouth of a tent at night with his back to the people sleeping. The boy used to leave a date-stone at its feet before he lay down, because he'd seen me lie across thresholds and thought it was manners.

None of them had names. That's the piece I'd like you to keep, if you keep only one.

Down there, nobody had a name, and nobody needed one, and nothing ate them. When the woman with the gold eyes wanted him, she was simply there. When the water wanted him, it rose. He was seven years old and had never once heard his name in anyone's mouth, because he didn't have one, and he had never missed it. Think of that. Think of a child with nothing for the wind to carry.

---

And at the edges, heat.

Out past the farthest roots, where the light thinned the way milk thins when you water it, the warmth went dry, and close. There was a smell out there, very faint, of burnt hair and of frankincense that has been burning for so long it has forgotten it was ever sweet. And sometimes, far out, past where the light gave up, there was a sound like a great many feet walking, slowly, without getting anywhere.

I was the one who stood between him and the edges. I'd stand across the hall with my hackles up and he'd come back, sulking, and pull my ears.

My silver eye never ached down there. Not once in seven years. I used to think that meant something good.

Remember that I thought that.

---

How did a human child come to be in such a place, nursed on milk that came from no goat, by a woman who was sometimes a gazelle?

No.

Not tonight. That one I'll keep. There are pieces I'll give you when you're older, and that one I may never give you at all.

---

He was about seven when he lost the path.

I've turned it in my mouth like a bone until there's no taste left in it, and I still don't know exactly what happened. Here is what I know.

Between under and over there have always been doors, and they aren't where you'd think. A well is a door. A grave is a door. A lone tree in the empty sand. The cracked crust of the salt flats. A ruin. The hour between dusk and full dark. And the noon.

You've seen it, little one. At the top of the day, when your own shadow crawls under your feet to hide, the air above the sand begins to shake like water. A lane you've walked a thousand times fills with a bright, trembling lake that isn't there. The old people don't go out in it. They have reasons.

It's a door, the oldest kind. And one day, at the top of one hall, where the roots grew thin, a light came down between them that was not ours, white and hard and hot. The noon door stood open.

He followed a gazelle up.

Or he followed a sound.

There was a gazelle. I saw its rump go white between the roots, high up, where it shouldn't have been. But I didn't see its eyes. I never saw its eyes. I have asked myself, since, whose gazelle it was, and I don't like any of the answers.

And there was a sound. It came down through the white light, thin and far. Women's voices. A great many women, calling, one after another, high and sing-song and not entirely playing:

*Come in! Come inside! Come in from the sun, or the Noon Mare will take you!*

And after each call, a name. A different name each time. Each woman calling her own child, by name, into the shade.

He didn't understand a word. He didn't need to. Every child born understands the sound of being called home. He stood at the bottom of that white light with his mouth open, listening to the names come down, one after another after another, and his face—

I can't tell you what his face did. I won't.

He went up.

I went after him. I was close enough to catch the hem of his shirt in my teeth. I want that written down somewhere, if anyone ever writes any of this down: I was right behind him. The roots fell away and there was nothing under us at all, only a wobbling brightness like the inside of a shaken water-skin, and the calling voices grew nearer, and then—

It closed.

I felt it go. Behind us, below us, like an eye shutting. Not a slam. A door slamming is an honest thing. This was soft, and sure, and final, the way a mother closes her hand over a coin a child has found in the dirt.

And I knew. Before I'd even opened my eyes I knew.

---

He woke crying in a lane in Hilat al-Sarab at noon.

That's his first memory. He doesn't remember the roots, or the basin, or the gold eyes, not the way you remember a thing. He remembers the lane. White walls of mud brick on either side, so bright they hurt. Gravel hot enough to raise blisters. Shuttered doors. A strip of shade so thin it might as well not have been there. And the air above the end of the lane shaking, shaking, like something trying to make up its mind.

He lay on his back with a grey cloth knotted round his shoulders, a weave no woman in that village could ever afterwards name, screaming with his whole body, heels drumming the gravel. And all around him, from behind every shuttered door and every palm-frond screen, the mothers of Hilat al-Sarab were calling their children in.

*Come in! Come in from the sun!*

Name after name after name.

Not one of them was his. How could it be? He had none.

No one called him.

One door opened. I remember that. One door, halfway down the lane, opened a hand's width, and a woman's face looked out at the strange child lying in the noon glare in a cloth she'd never seen, crying in no language she knew, with a strange dog standing over him. She looked for a long moment. And she shut the door. I heard the bar drop on the other side.

I've had a long time not to blame her. In Hilat al-Sarab they knew exactly what came out of the noon, and none of it was a gift.

I stood over him with my hackles up, between him and the end of the lane where the air still trembled, until the sun slid west and the shimmer died and the children came out, blinking and safe, because their names had been called and they had answered.

Sheikh Mazyad came and pulled at his beard and said a great deal. In the end they kept the boy, the way you keep a kid-goat that wanders in after a storm. They fed him when they remembered and cuffed him when they didn't. They called him *Laqeet*, the foundling. Nobody ever gave him another name. You don't name a thing you're waiting to give back.

And me, they didn't keep at all. I simply stayed.

Within a season he had their words. By the end of the year he'd lost almost all of his own. Almost. He still talked in his sleep. He still does. When he talks in his sleep, little one, it is not in any language spoken between the Hajar mountains and the sea.

---

Eight years later he was still the only child out at noon.

Fifteen, near enough. Tall for it, and too thin, with eyes too light for his brown face and a way of standing very still that made the village uneasy. He scavenged. It was the only work anyone would let him do. There were ruins a short walk into the dunes from the village, half swallowed by sand: the bones of some settlement so old that even the stones had forgotten whose hands stacked them, with a dry channel running through where a falaj once carried water. Every few weeks the wind uncovered a new room, and every few weeks he went into it before the wind changed its mind.

He went at noon, because nobody else was out, because of what the mothers called from the doorways every day, and nobody, not once in eight years, had called it to him. He'd learned to use that the way a lame man learns to use a stick.

That day the noon pressed. The sky had gone white. The ruin walls ticked as they swelled. I walked in his shadow, which at that hour was about the size of a prayer rug. He had his headcloth wound over his nose and mouth and an old khanjar in his belt, a blade with no silver left on the hilt, bound up in rag and rusted along the spine. He was humming. He didn't know he hummed. It was a tune with no words, and I knew it, and I'm not going to tell you where it came from.

He went down into a room the wind had opened in the night: three steps, a floor of packed sand, the corner of a hearth-stone showing, and began to dig with his hands.

I didn't go in. I lay down in the slit of shade at the top of the steps, and that was when I saw her.

She was standing on the ruin wall across the court, against the white sky. A gazelle, small, fine-legged, the colour of the dunes at evening, perfectly still in the full blaze of noon, where no gazelle with any sense would ever stand. She stood in the sun as if the sun had nothing to say to her.

Her eyes were gold.

She wasn't looking at me. She was looking down into the room where he knelt in the sand with his back to her, digging. She looked at him the way—

I looked away.

I didn't run to her. I didn't so much as lift my head. I looked away, politely, at a lizard on a stone, the way a grown son looks away when he sees his mother across the crowded market and understands she doesn't want to be seen. Not by him. Not there. Not yet.

When I looked back the wall was empty, and the heat was shaking above it.

He never saw her. He was digging.

---

He found the coin a little after.

It came up out of the sand in his fingers with a sound, a small clean *tink* against the hearth-stone, and he went quite still. Then he sat back on his heels and held it up to the slit of light coming down the steps.

Silver. A big coin, worn soft at the edges, with a foreign queen on it, fat-chinned, a veil pinned in her hair, and letters round her that no one in Hilat al-Sarab could read. A Maria Theresa thaler. Ghanim kept a box of them under his mat and counted them at night by lamplight like a man telling the beads of his own heart. Theyab had never held one. Theyab had never held anything that was worth more than a day's dates.

He laughed, once, a short surprised sound like a bird startled off a branch, and then he clapped his hand over his mouth and looked up the steps as if someone might have heard.

Then he put the thaler into his left hand, and closed his fist on it.

He always kept that fist closed. He ate and dug with his right hand and slept with his left curled shut against his chest like a sick bird. Nobody had ever told him to. He'd come up out of the noon with that hand closed and had hardly opened it since. Across the palm, under the coin, under the fingers, there was a line. A single crease from one edge of the hand to the other, straight and unbroken, the way no line runs across a palm that the One who made the sands meant to leave alone. He didn't know what it was. He only knew, the way a hare knows about hawks, that it was not for showing.

And as his fist closed on the silver, my eye began to ache.

The silver one. A dull throb like a tooth gone bad, and then sharper, pushing. I knew that ache. I'd known it long before him, with other children. It only ever came when the one I walked beside was in danger.

I went down the three steps and stood against his legs and looked hard at every corner with both eyes. Sand. Stone. The corner of a hearth.

"What?" he said. "What is it?"

I couldn't tell him. I never could. I leaned my whole weight on him until he stumbled, and he laughed and shoved me off and climbed out into the sun with his fist shut tight and the silver hot inside it.

The ache stayed all the way home, pulsing behind my eye like something that had found a way in and was making itself comfortable.

---

The lanes of Hilat al-Sarab were empty when we came in. Behind the shut doors the village slept: someone snoring, a baby fretting. Somewhere a mother was telling a story to keep her children quiet, low and slow, and I caught one word of it through a frond wall as we passed. *Hooves.* A child giggled, frightened, and was hushed.

His tent stood in a hollow just past the last house, between the village and the first dune, where the ground dipped a little and kept the worst of the wind off. It was patched from nine scraps that had been nine different things: black goat hair, a piece of pearling sail, a rice sack from Basra. In the noon he slept outside it, in the strip of shade along its eastern side, on a blanket of faded striped wool that he'd found in a ruin when he was ten and had washed in the trough until it was nearly clean.

He stopped at the lip of the hollow.

So did I.

There were tracks in the sand.

Small round cups, each one neat as if it had been pressed out with the bottom of a coffee cup. Not a goat's split hoof. Not a camel's soft pad. Round, and hard-edged, and deep at the front: a donkey's hoof. But a donkey walks on four feet, and these went two by two. One. Then the next, a long stride on, straight and unhurried, the way a woman walks when she's carrying a full water-jar on her head and has decided not to spill a drop.

They came down out of the noon-white lane behind us, where I would have sworn there had been nothing. They came down the lane and over the lip of the hollow and they went round it. All the way round. A slow, wide circle, every print the same distance from the tent, like someone pacing out the size of a thing before buying it.

And then they turned in, and crossed the hollow, and stopped.

Two prints, side by side, close together, at the edge of his blanket. Facing it. As if something had stood there for a long time, in the dead white middle of the day, looking down at the place where he would lie.

I put my nose down to them.

Hot dust. Hot hide, like a beast worked too long in the sun. And laid over that, sewn on where it shouldn't have been, a woman's smell: hair-oil, and hearth-smoke, and the sour edge of milk left out too long in a bowl.

He crouched. He put out one finger and touched the rim of the nearest print.

"It's warm," he said.

The ache behind my silver eye went bright as a coal.

He looked up the lane, at the shut doors and the shaking air at the end of it, for a long time. Then he took the blanket, and shook it out, hard, three times, and carried it inside the tent, and didn't come out again until the sun was low.

---

That night he dreamed of a well.

I know, because I was lying against his back, the way I always lay, and I felt the dream come up into him like water into a basin. His breathing went slow and deep. His shut fist uncurled a little against his chest. And a smell came off his skin, faint, that I hadn't smelled on him in eight years, not since the noon he came up: sweet water and warm stone, and very faintly, under those, milk.

His lips moved. He smiled in his sleep, the way small children smile when someone they love has come into the room.

Then, near dawn, he woke all at once, sitting straight up in the dark with his eyes open, and he spoke.

Not to me. Not to anyone in the tent. He said it out loud, clearly, to the dark beyond the tent flap, the way you'd answer someone who had just said your name. Five or six words, soft, with a lilt in them like water running over stone.

They weren't words of the Bedouin, or of the coast, or of the Persian carpet-sellers, or of the Indian moneylenders at the oasis market. No one in Hilat al-Sarab had ever heard them.

I had heard them. A long time ago. Under the roots.

He sat there in the grey half-dark with his mouth still open, looking at the tent flap. Then he turned and looked down at me, and I have never in my long life seen a face so lost.

"Ghareeb," he whispered. "What did I just say?"

Outside, very faintly, from the direction of the lane, something went *tik*.

Then nothing at all.

---

*End of Chapter One*
