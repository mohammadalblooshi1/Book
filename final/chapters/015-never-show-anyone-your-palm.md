# Chapter Fifteen
## Never Show Anyone Your Palm

> *Hide the child's hand in your sleeve; there are men who read palms with knives.*
> — what the mothers of Liwa whisper over a marked baby, and never say aloud by day

---

He didn't open it.

Not at first. He sat with his left fist on his knee, knuckles white, and looked at Hilal's open hand held out to him in the firelight, and didn't move. The way a hare doesn't move in the grass when the falcon's shadow passes over. As if stillness could make him not there.

I knew that fist better than I knew my own paws.

He'd kept it shut all his life. Every day of the eight years in Hilat al-Sarab, scavenging the ruins, hauling water, eating Umm Hanan's bread in the dark: always the left hand closed, the thumb folded in over the fingers, the way you'd close your hand over a coin in a crowded market. He ate with his right. He dug with his right. When Nadim and the others held him down at the well and tried to pry the left one open for sport, he'd bitten Nadim's thumb to the bone rather than let them, and taken the beating after without a sound. He slept with it closed. I've watched him sleep with it closed, a thousand nights. Even in the deepest sleep, when the rest of him went loose as a dropped rope, that hand stayed a fist.

He didn't know why. If you'd asked him, and nobody ever did, he'd have said he didn't know he was doing it.

I knew why. I knew who'd taught him. I watched it being taught, a long way from here, in a place with no sun, to a very small boy, by someone who smelled of milk and was very afraid.

I won't tell you that either. Not yet. I'm sorry, little one. There are so many things I won't tell you yet. I'm an old dog, and the long road's the only one I know.

---

"I can't," Theyab said.

"You can," said Hilal. "You just don't want to. Those aren't the same, though they wear each other's clothes." He didn't lower his hand. "I asked you twice, in your own hollow, and you hid it in the crook of your arm like a boy with a stolen date. I let you. I shouldn't have. I went away down the Liwa road thinking about your hand the whole way, and I couldn't have told you a word the camel-boy said to me." He paused. "I sent him on with read-over water for the sick child. She'll live, I think. No thanks to me. It'll be her mother's soup."

The bell didn't ring.

"Show me," Hilal said. "Before someone else asks you who won't ask so nicely."

Theyab looked at Bakhit.

Bakhit had his face turned toward the fire. He didn't say anything. But he nodded, once, very slightly. The way you nod to a child on the edge of the falaj who's afraid to jump. *Go on. I'm here.*

Theyab turned his fist over on his knee.

And opened it.

---

I'll tell you exactly what it looked like, little one, because I've seen it since on another hand, a much smaller one, and I want you to know it if you ever see it too.

The palm of a hand is written on. You know that. Look at your own. Lines, crossing and branching and running off into nothing, like the tracks of birds on wet sand. The old women at the coast read them and tell you you'll marry twice. Every hand has them. Every hand is different.

His wasn't.

Across the middle of his palm, from the edge under the little finger all the way to the soft web of the thumb, ran one line. One. Straight as a bowstring, deep as a cut, unbroken from end to end. It didn't branch. It didn't fade. It didn't cross anything, because there was nothing on that palm for it to cross; the smaller lines that should have been there, the ones above and below, were faint, faint as the marks a wind leaves, as if they'd been drawn in afterwards by a less careful hand. There was only the one line. Like a seam. Like the line where the two halves of a split bead meet.

Like a door, I thought. Like the crack under a door.

Hilal didn't touch it at first. He looked at it a long time. His lips had stopped moving. That was the first time I'd seen them stop.

"Ah," he said softly.

The word went out of him like a breath going out of a man who's been hit in the chest.

---

"Marked," said Hilal.

He said it the way you'd say the name of a sickness. Quietly, so it wouldn't hear.

"The Marked. A Marked child. That's what the old books call it, the ones that dare to call it anything." He was still staring at the palm. "I've seen it twice before. Twice in my whole life. Once in a book, drawn in red ink by a man who'd been dead six hundred years, and once—" He stopped.

"Once?" said Theyab.

Hilal looked up at him. Then at Bakhit. Then back.

"Once on a girl in the Hajar," he said. "In a village above the wadis, when I was a young man. A goatherd's daughter. Eleven or twelve. Her mother kept her hand bound in a cloth from the day she was born. Told everyone it was withered. Told everyone it was a curse, which was true, in its way." He was quiet a moment. "Some men came through the village, one spring. Traders, they said. They asked a lot of questions about the old tombs in the hills. They stayed three nights. On the fourth morning they were gone, and so was the girl."

"Did they find her?"

"Yes."

The bell didn't ring. I wished it would.

"Up in the hills," said Hilal, "at the mouth of an old tomb that had been sealed since before the wadis had names. The seal was broken. The stone door was standing open, and whatever had been inside was gone. She was lying across the threshold. They'd opened her hand with a knife, along the line, and held it over the seal, and let it run till it stopped. She was white. White as salt. They said it looked as if she'd been sleeping." He looked down at his own hands. "Her mother took the cloth off the dead hand to wash her for burial. That was the first time anyone in that village had ever seen it. The line. They'd lived beside that child twelve years."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke.

"That's what it does," Hilal said. "That's what it's for, if you ask the men who want it. The blood of a Marked child opens what's been shut. Sealed doors. Guarded places. Tombs the old kings closed with words, cisterns the ones from below have stopped up, treasure buried under the sand with something sitting on it to keep it. Locks that iron can't break and words can't open. A Marked hand opens them all." He took a breath. "And so there are men who hunt for them. Treasure-diggers. Sorcerers. The kind that sell their dreams to the ones from below in exchange for a map. They'll pay a fortune for a Marked child. They'll ride a month to steal one."

"The Digger," Theyab whispered.

Hilal's eyes sharpened. "What digger?"

So Theyab told him. The stranger in the market with clay under his nails and a book of buried places, asking Ghanim about the foundling's hands. The sacks in the dark. The gag. Umm Hanan's lamp held high and her screaming the lanes awake. And the words the old sorcerer had spat over his shoulder as he ran, the words the village had passed from mouth to mouth for weeks until they wore smooth.

*A son of the ones from below. His blood is owed to us.*

Hilal's face had gone dark.

"Ghanim," he said. "Ghanim told them."

It wasn't a question. Theyab didn't answer it. He didn't have to. He'd opened his fist for Ghanim once, on the scales, to take a few coins, and Ghanim had looked down at it and smiled and said nothing.

"That man," said Hilal, "would sell the rope off his own mother's bucket."

The bell didn't ring for that either.

---

"Is it true?" said Theyab.

He was looking at Bakhit now.

"What he said. The Digger. *A son of the ones from below.*" He said it fast, as if it would hurt less fast. "Is that what I am? Is that why I can't— why the light— is my father one of *them?*"

Bakhit turned his blind face toward him.

For a long time he didn't answer. I watched him, and I've known him longer than you'd believe, and I'd never seen him look as old as he looked then.

"I don't know who your father was," he said at last.

The bell hung on its pole and didn't move.

It was true. He didn't know. Neither did I, not then. I'd smelled something on that first night, in the wind from the north, among the other smells: a man's sweat and a camel's, and under them, faintly, something that wasn't a man. Smokeless fire and wet stone. I'd smelled it and put it away in the deep place. I didn't know what it meant. When I learned, years later, it was in a place you haven't come to yet, from a mouth you wouldn't believe.

"Men like the Digger say a great many things," said Hilal. "Half of them to frighten. Half of them because they've frightened themselves. A boy who walks out of the noon with no mother and talks in his sleep in a tongue no one knows, with a light in his chest and that line on his hand?" He shook his head. "Of course they'd say it. They'd have said it if you were the sheikh's son."

That was true too. My hackles stayed down. It didn't answer the question, and everyone in that tent knew it didn't, and nobody said so.

---

Then Hilal reached out and took his hand.

Gently. The way you'd lift a fledgling that had fallen out of a palm. He turned it to the lamp and laid it open on his own palm, and the line lay across it, dark and clean, like a crack in a pot.

"Someone hid this," he said.

Theyab looked at him.

"From the day you were born. Look at it. A line like this, a midwife would see it before she'd cut the cord. She'd scream. She'd run to the sheikh. The whole tribe would know by sundown, and every sorcerer from here to the sea by the new moon." His thumb moved, slowly, along the edge of the palm. "But nobody knows. You've lived eight years in a village of gossips and nobody knew till Ghanim cheated you. You've kept this fist shut so long you don't know you're doing it. You didn't learn that by yourself, son of no one. Babies don't. Somebody taught you. Somebody who knew exactly what this was, and was very frightened for you."

Theyab's face had gone strange. As if he were listening to something a long way off.

"I don't remember," he said. "I don't remember anything before the lane."

Hilal's thumb touched the end of the line, there under the little finger, and began to trace it.

Slowly. All the way along. The way you'd run your thumb along the edge of a knife to test it.

---

And my eye began to burn.

I don't mean ache. It had ached when Hilal came up the gravel reciting; it had ached in the dead grove; it ached most days of my life, a little, the way an old wound aches. This wasn't that.

It burned like a coal.

As if someone had taken one of the embers out of Bakhit's hearth and pressed it into the socket. As if the eye itself had caught fire. I yelped. I couldn't help it. A thin high sound like a puppy stepped on. I scrambled up off the rug and backed into the goat-hair wall with my head down, pawing at my face.

And I couldn't look away.

That was the worst of it, little one. That's what I need you to understand. Everything in me wanted to turn my head. I *tried*. I strained my neck round toward the wall till the cords stood out in it. And the silver eye stayed on his palm. As if it were nailed there. As if something behind it, something on the far side of the window, had leaned forward to the glass and put both hands on the sill and was *looking*, hungrily, at that one clean line under Hilal's moving thumb. Looking the way a starving man looks at bread through a shop door.

It saw. Whatever it was, it saw the line, all the way along, from end to end.

Then Hilal's thumb reached the web of the hand and stopped, and lifted.

And the fire went out of my eye, all at once, like a lamp blown out.

---

They were all looking at me.

Theyab with his hand still lying open on Hilal's palm. Bakhit with his face turned toward the sound of me. And Hilal. Hilal looking at me the way he'd looked at me on the gravel outside, with his eyes narrowed, like a man who sees a word in a book he doesn't know and decides that he'll look it up later, and that he won't like what he finds.

"Ghareeb?" said Theyab.

I came back to him. Slowly, with my tail down. I pushed my head under his free hand. My eye was watering. It felt as if it had been scraped with sand.

"What's wrong with him?" Theyab said.

"Old dogs," said Bakhit after a moment, "have old pains."

The bell didn't ring. Because it wasn't a lie. It just wasn't an answer.

Hilal said nothing. He let go of Theyab's hand. And I saw him, out of the side of my good eye, put his own hand into the cloth bag at his side, and close it on something, and keep it there a while, and then take it out again empty.

He was looking at me. He kept looking at me, off and on, for the rest of that night. I didn't look back.

I'll tell you what I thought, lying there with my head under Theyab's hand and my eye streaming. I thought: *the dead grove.* I thought: *she turned and looked at me, and then everything turned and looked.* I thought it, and I put it in the deep place with the others, and I pressed the lid down hard on it with both paws, and I lay on the lid.

You can do that for a long time, if you're stubborn enough. Years. Longer. You can do it until someone you trust opens it for you, in front of everyone.

---

"There's worse," said Bakhit.

Hilal looked over at him sharply. "Bakhit."

"He's asked what he is," said Bakhit. "He's earned the rest. He paid for it in stones." He turned his face back to Theyab. "You've heard of the Nameless."

Theyab had. Everyone had. The way you hear of the Lantern Man and the Noon Mare when you're small: a thing the grandmothers say to keep you near the tents.

Men in white masks. Men who'd burned their own names in a fire and scattered the ashes, so that nothing could call them, so that they belonged to nothing but what they served. They rode at night, they said, on pale camels. They took children. Nobody had seen them in Hilat al-Sarab in living memory. Nobody, I should say, who'd lived.

"They're real," said Bakhit.

The bell didn't ring.

"They serve something," he said. "Something under the sand, a long way south and west of here, under a city the sand swallowed before your grandmother's grandmother was born. Ubar. You've heard that in stories too. There's a well in that city, and under the well, there's a door, and under the door there's a thing the old people call the Sealed King. It's shut in a brass vessel. It's been shut a very long time. It doesn't like it."

"The Grey," said Theyab.

"The Grey's its breath," said Bakhit. "Coming up through the cracks. When the seal weakens, the doors open everywhere. Wells. Graves. Dead groves. The noon. That's why the ones from below are walking in your lanes, child. Not because of you. Because the seal is cracking, and they smell it, and they're coming up to see."

He was silent a moment.

"The Nameless want it out," he said. "Out, and on its throne, and themselves at its feet. They've wanted it for longer than there have been Nameless. And for that they need two things." He held up one finger. "The Light, on the dune. They have their own reasons for wanting it burned, and their own way of wanting it done. It's a long story, and an ugly one, and you'll hear it." He held up a second finger. "And the blood that opens the last seal. A Marked child's blood, poured at the door under Ubar's well."

He let his hands fall.

"You carry both," he said. "In one body. The feather on your chest and the line in your hand. Do you understand what that means, child? Every Digger in the desert wants your hand. Every one of the ones from below that guards a buried thing wants your blood. And the Nameless want all of you. They want you twice over. They'll want you on that dune and they'll want your hand open over that well, and they'll not mind which comes first."

---

Theyab looked down at his hand.

He was holding it open now, on his own knee, palm up. I don't think he could have closed it if he'd wanted to. He looked at it the way you look at a scorpion that has walked onto your sleeve.

"They'll come here," he said.

Nobody answered.

"They'll come *here*," he said again. "The Diggers came here. Because of me. The Grey—" He stopped. "You said it wasn't because of me. But it came when I said my name. And the hoofed thing came for Shibl because of me. Because I took him away from it once and it wanted him back. And the Digger said they'd come again. *His blood is owed to us.* And now these. The masks. If they want me twice over, they'll come to wherever I am. And whatever's in the way."

He looked up at Hilal.

"Shibl was in the way," he said. "Tonight."

Hilal didn't say anything. He didn't need to. The bell would have rung for anything he said.

---

I watched him decide.

It didn't take long. That's the thing that broke me, little one. I'd seen it coming all night and I still wasn't ready for how quickly it came.

He sat there with his open hand on his knee and his back cracked with bitter paste and his face full of gold, and I saw him go back through the whole night in his head, the way you go back through a dream in the morning. The dead grove. The four emptied in their neat square. The white eyes. Nadim on his knees saying *please*. The gold on Shibl's cheeks. The forty lamps. *Monster.* Saqi's stone. The goat-man shouting lies. Ghanim tipping out the annas. The salt. The soot hand.

And Umm Hanan's bread. Still warm.

And Shibl, calling *Laqeet* after him into the dark. Once. Before a hand went over his mouth.

"I'll go," he said.

---

"Go where?" said Hilal sharply.

"Away. Out. Into the sand." He said it simply. "If I'm not here, they won't come here. If they come for me, they'll come where I am. Where there's nobody in the way."

"They stoned you," said Hilal.

He said it flatly, without heat. He wasn't arguing. He was a man who'd spent forty years reading over sick villages, testing the ground ahead with his stick, and he wanted to be sure it would bear the weight.

"They put salt round your tent. They put a soot hand on it. They broke your water-skin in the dust in front of your eyes. That man Saqi, whose life you saved with your own arms in his own yard, threw the first stone. And you'll go out into the desert alone, with the Diggers and the masks and the things from below all looking for you, so that the people who did that to you can sleep at night?"

Theyab looked at him.

"Umm Hanan didn't," he said.

Hilal opened his mouth. Closed it.

"Umm Hanan didn't stone me," said Theyab. "She baked. She got up at midnight and baked. And Shibl didn't. He called after me. Did you know that? He called my name." He looked down at his hand again. "Not my name. The name they gave me. But he called it. Like you'd call after someone you want to come back."

He curled his fingers in. Not into a fist. Just in, loosely, the way you'd hold a small bird.

"I'm not doing it for the ones who threw stones," he said. "I'm doing it for the ones who didn't."

---

The bell didn't ring.

Nothing rang. Nothing moved. The fire had burned down to a red glow in the pit, and the lamp burned low and steady. Bakhit sat with his blind face turned to the boy, and his old dry hands lay still on his knees, and I saw something run down out of one of his milk-white eyes and into his beard. I've never seen him do it before or since.

Hilal sat back.

He looked at Theyab for a long time, and the two deep lines beside his mouth went deeper, and I don't know what he was thinking. I never knew what Hilal was thinking. That was one of the things I liked about him. But I smelled him, and I'll tell you this: there was no pity in it. Pity smells of something sweet going off. This was something else. Something clean and sharp, like the air after rain in the Hajar.

It was respect, I think. I don't smell it often. Not from men like Hilal, toward boys like Theyab.

"Where?" said Hilal.

Theyab looked at Bakhit.

"The glass ruins," he said. "You said there are carvings. Children with feathers. You said a man counted them." He swallowed. "I want to count them."

Bakhit was quiet a long time.

"Ghidd al-Zujaj," he said at last. "South and west. Across the sand. Past where any sane caravan goes." He breathed out. "Not tonight. Tonight you'll sleep. In the morning we'll talk about the road. I have some things to give you. And some things to tell you, that I should have told you long ago, and didn't. You'll hate me for those too."

"I don't hate you," said Theyab.

And the bell rang.

One clear note. Small. Sweet. Hanging in the air.

Theyab flinched. Then, for the first time that whole long night, for the first time since the dead grove, something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of one. The shape a smile leaves when it's been and gone.

"Not yet," he said.

The bell was silent.

Bakhit laughed. A dry cracked sound, like a stick in the fire. He laughed till he had to wipe his eyes.

---

Hilal got up a little after that. He said he'd walk the edge of the Grey till dawn and read along it, the way he'd read over the well, to hold it back a day or two. He said the village needed reading more than Theyab needed company, and that the dog would be company enough, and that the dog would know what he meant. He looked at me when he said that. I didn't look back.

At the flap he stopped and turned round.

"When they call you monster," he said to Theyab, "remember who said it first. I told you that. I'll tell you the other half now, since you've earned it." He pointed his stick at the boy's open hand. "Remember who said it *last*. That'll be the one who's right."

Then he went out, and his stick went *tock, tock* away across the gravel, and the low endless saying went with it, fading, and my eye stopped aching by degrees, like a burn cooling.

A little while after, the thin old thing came back to the threshold and stood there with its back to us, facing out. I felt it settle. The guest's glass on its stone was empty now.

Theyab lay down by the embers with his head on the grey name-cloth. He was asleep before he'd finished lying down. His left hand lay open on the rug beside his face, palm up, for the first time in his life. As if he'd forgotten how to close it.

I lay down across the threshold, beside the thing that watched the door, and put my head on my paws.

And I slept, and I dreamed.

---

I'll tell you the dream. I've never been sure it was one.

I was standing on a high dune a long way from Hilat al-Sarab, south and west, in the deep sand where the dunes go up like mountains and come down like the sea. It was night. The same night. I know it was the same night because the moon was the same, a few nights past full and lopsided, hanging low over the dunes like a cracked bowl.

And down in the hollow below me there were riders.

Twelve, perhaps. Fifteen. On pale camels, Omani racers, long-legged and quiet, moving in a line across the floor of the hollow without a sound. Their robes were white. Their headcloths were white, wound up round their faces. And where their faces should have been, in the gap between headcloth and robe, there were masks. White. Smooth. Without mouths. Only two holes for the eyes, black, like the holes a finger makes in wet clay.

They were riding north-east. Slowly. Searching. The way riders ride when they know roughly where a thing is and not exactly, and are prepared to take a long time.

And as I watched, the one at the front reined in.

He didn't pull hard. He just stopped. The camel stopped under him. The others stopped behind, one after another, down the line, without a word.

He sat very still in the saddle. Then he lifted his masked face. Not toward me. Past me. Toward the north-east, toward the edge of Liwa, toward a village a long way off that he couldn't possibly have seen. He held it there, lifted, the way a hound lifts its nose into a wind that's brought it something.

Then he turned his camel's head.

Toward Hilat al-Sarab. Straight toward it, as if someone had stood up on the far horizon with a lamp and waved it. And down the line, one by one, the others turned after him, and they rode out of the hollow at a trot, then faster, and the sand rose behind them white under the late moon.

---

I woke with my eye burning.

Not like a coal, now. Like the memory of a coal. The cold dark was still in the tent, and the boy was still asleep with his hand open on the rug, and the thing at the threshold still stood beside me with its back to us and its face to the night.

But it had moved. While I slept. It had moved a half-step nearer to the boy, and it was standing a little differently now. The way a man stands at a door when he's heard something on the road.

I lay there and counted.

I've counted it many times since, little one. I've lain awake in a great many places and worked it out over and over, the way you worry a knot. How long since the riders turned. How long it takes a man to trace a line across a palm with his thumb. Where the moon was. Where my eye was looking.

I'll tell you what I worked out. I'll tell you plainly, and then I won't say anything else about it tonight, and you mustn't ask.

They turned at the moment I looked at his palm.

---

*End of Chapter Fifteen*
