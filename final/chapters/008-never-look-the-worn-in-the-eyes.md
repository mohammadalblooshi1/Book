# Chapter Eight
## Never Look the Worn in the Eyes

> *"The one who loves you most is the best door for the Smokeless."*
> — a saying of the oasis women

---

His hands came down.

Theyab was through the wall before I was. I want you to know that. I am a saluki; I am faster than any boy who has ever lived, and I was lying with my shoulder against the frond wall with every muscle wound tight, and still he was through it first. He did not go round to the gap. He went straight at the wall and through it, palm fronds tearing and cracking round him like dry bones, and he landed in the moonlit room on his knees between the mats.

Saqi did not look at him.

He scooped Shibl up off the mat the way you lift a bundle of washing, one arm under the child's middle, and tucked him against his hip. Shibl came awake in mid-air. I saw his eyes open. I saw him see his father's face above him, with the white eyes and no smile, and I saw him not understand it.

"Baba?" he said.

Then he screamed.

It was not the scream of a child who has skinned a knee. It had nothing in it but fear, the sound a kid-goat makes when the knife comes, and it went up through the fronds and out over the roofs of Hilat al-Sarab and it did not stop.

Nadim came off his mat like a fired gun.

He saw his brother screaming in his father's arms and the foundling on his knees in a ruin of torn fronds, and he chose. Of course he chose. He went for Theyab.

He got one stride. Then he saw his father's face.

"Baba," he said. "Baba, what are you—"

Saqi swung his free arm backward without looking.

It was not a blow. There was no wind-up to it, no weight behind it, nothing a fighting man would call a blow. It was the motion a man makes brushing a fly off his shoulder. It caught Nadim across the chest and lifted him off his feet, and threw him, all fifteen years of him, sideways and back through the frond wall of the arish as if it were a curtain. The wall burst outward. I heard him hit the ground in the yard beyond. I heard the breath go out of him in one great hoarse cough, and then I did not hear him at all.

And Saqi walked out through the door to the court, carrying his screaming son, toward the well.

---

Here is what you must understand about the Worn, little one, and you must understand it now, because there is no time for it later.

The thing inside does not care about the body.

When you lift a stone, there is a voice in every muscle that says *not that much, you'll tear*. That voice is what keeps you whole.

The Smiling Hunger does not hear that voice. It will lift a camel with a man's arms and let the arms come apart doing it, and when they are useless it will walk on the legs, and then crawl, and then go and find another robe.

That is the strength of the Worn. It is not a strength the thing gives you. It is a strength the thing *takes from you*, all of it, all at once, the way a thief empties a purse.

Saqi crossed the well-court at a walk. He did not hurry. Shibl was kicking and clawing at the arm round his middle, his small fists beating on his father's wrist, and Saqi walked as if he were carrying nothing at all.

I hit him from the side.

I took him at a full run, low, the way a saluki takes a gazelle, and I went for the arm that held the boy. I got it. My teeth went in above the wrist and met in the meat of his forearm and I set my legs and *wrenched*, the whole of my weight thrown sideways, the hunting wrench that breaks a gazelle's balance and brings it down.

It was like biting a palm trunk.

He did not stagger. He did not stop. I hung off his arm with my legs dragging, and his blood was in my mouth, and it was cold. Little one, it was cold as well-water. Blood should be the hottest thing in the world, and his was cold, and it tasted of wet stone.

His wrist turned in my jaws.

I do not mean he twisted his arm. I mean the wrist turned. It went round in my mouth the way a lid turns on a jar, further than a wrist goes, much further, and I heard the bones grind against each other, and still the hand at the end of it held his son.

He looked down at me then. The white eyes looked down.

He let go of the boy with his other hand, the one I had not got, and took me by the scruff of my neck, and lifted me off his arm the way you lift a kitten. My teeth tore out of him. I felt them go. And he held me up in front of his face, all of me, my legs kicking in the air, an old saluki as long as a man is tall, held out at arm's length by one hand.

"*Not you,*" he said, in the two voices. Gently. Almost fondly. "*Not yet.*"

And threw me.

I went across the court. I remember the stars wheeling. I remember the edge of the stone water-trough coming up at me very fast, and the sound my ribs made when they met it, a short flat crack like a branch broken over a knee, and then I was lying in the wet sand under the trough with all the air gone out of the world.

I could not breathe. I lay there with my mouth open and my sides heaving and nothing coming in. I watched him walk on.

---

Theyab came out of the arish at a dead run and jumped on his back.

He had the khanjar in his right hand. He got his left arm round Saqi's throat from behind, his legs round his waist, and hung there, and I saw him lift the blade.

I saw him not use it.

He held it over the place where the neck meets the collarbone, where any herder would put a knife to finish a beast, and his arm shook. It was a man he had known all his life. It was Shibl's father, and he was still in there somewhere, and Theyab could not.

So he turned the khanjar in his hand and hammered at Saqi's head with the hilt instead.

Once. Twice. The rag-wrapped hilt thudding into the side of the well-keeper's skull, hard enough to drop a man, hard enough that I heard skin split.

Saqi's head turned round to look at him.

Not his body. His head. It turned on his neck while his body kept walking toward the well, turned slowly, with a sound like a wet rope being wrung, further over his shoulder than a head can turn, until the white eyes were looking straight back into Theyab's face from a hand's width away.

And it smiled.

There it was at last. The smile. Saqi's face, with the lines at the corners of its mouth from twenty years of not smiling, and the mouth spreading wide, wider, too wide, the lips pulling back off the teeth and the corners going up and up toward the white eyes.

"*Laqeet,*" it said, in Saqi's voice and under it the soft dead mother's voice. "*Go home.*"

Then it bucked. The whole body, from heels to shoulders, in one convulsion like a camel throwing a bad rider. Theyab came off over its head. He turned in the air and came down on his back across the rim of the well, on the stone lip, with his head and shoulders out over the shaft and the dark open under him.

He grabbed the roller-post. He hung there, half over the hole.

Saqi stepped up onto the lip of the well beside him.

---

I got up.

I don't know how. Something was broken in my side and every breath went in through it like a knife, but I got my legs under me and I got up out of the wet sand, and as I got up I felt it. The other thing. The silver half of me, the part I don't speak about. It sat up in me the way a sleeping hound sits up at the sound of the hunt, and it was *interested*, and it was strong, and it did not care about my ribs any more than the thing in Saqi cared about his wrist.

My shadow went across the court ahead of me in the moonlight. It was too long. It had too many joints in the legs.

I did not look at it. I ran.

Saqi was standing on the lip of the well with Shibl under his arm. The boy had stopped screaming. He had screamed himself empty, and now he was only making a thin high sound on every breath, *ee, ee, ee*, and holding on to his father's shirt with both fists, because when the world is ending a child will hold on to anything, even the thing that is ending it.

And Saqi stepped off the lip, into the well.

He did not fall.

He went down the inside of the shaft the way a lizard goes down a wall, head first, his free hand and his bare feet finding the old stones and gripping. His back bent against the far side of the shaft for purchase, bent wrong, the spine arching so far I heard it creak. He went down into the dark with his son under his arm, like a spider carrying its kill back to its hole.

Theyab let go of the post and threw himself flat on the lip and reached.

He got Shibl's wrist.

His whole arm went down into the shaft to the shoulder. His fingers closed round that small wrist and held, and the weight of the child and the thing that held the child came onto his arm all at once and pulled him forward over the lip, his chest scraping on the stone, his head and his other shoulder going over the edge into the dark.

I came across the last of the court in one stride and took the back of his sash in my teeth and set my legs.

We held.

For a moment, little one, we held. The boy on his belly over the black mouth of the well with his arm down the shaft. The old dog behind him with his teeth in the boy's sash and his broken ribs grinding and his feet braced on the stone. And below, in the dark, Shibl hanging between his father and the boy who had come for him, stretched like a hide on a frame.

Then Saqi began to climb.

Not down. Up.

I felt the pull change. He was coming back up the shaft toward us, hand over hand on the stones, and as he came he did not let go of the boy. I heard him come. I heard the slap of his palm on the wet stone, and the scrape of his feet, and his breath, which was not breath, which was a long, slow, even sound like the sea pulling back over shingle. And the smell came up ahead of him, smokeless fire and wet stone, so strong now that my eyes ran and my nose burned.

His face came up out of the dark.

It came up right under Theyab's face. A hand's width apart. Both of them with their heads in the mouth of the well, the boy hanging down and the man climbing up, and the moon straight overhead, laying its white light down the shaft between them.

There is a warning, little one. You have heard it from your grandmother. *Never look the Worn in the eyes.* It is not a thing old women say to make children behave. It was learned the way every true warning in the desert is learned: by burying the ones who did not.

Theyab looked.

---

I have only his word for what happened next. He told it to me much later, on a night when he could not sleep, lying with his face in my neck, in a whisper. He did not know I understood him. Or he did, and needed to tell someone who could never tell it back.

He said that when he looked into the white eyes, they were not white.

He said they were holes. Two round holes in the man's face, and the holes went down, the way the well went down, and he fell into them.

He said he fell for a long time, down a shaft that was cold, so cold, cold the way the bottom of a well is cold in the hottest summer, with the stone sweating and the air standing still. He said it was dark and the dark was whispering. Not one voice. Thousands. All round him, close to his ears, whispering names. Every name you have ever heard. The names of the dead and the names of the living, all said quickly and softly, one after another, like a merchant counting coins in the dark to make sure they were all still there.

He said that as he fell, he felt the whispering *notice* him.

He said it was like falling past a crowd of people on the stairs of a deep well and feeling them all turn their heads to watch him go by. And then, he said, they started to look for his name. He felt them doing it. Like fingers going through his clothes. Like hands going through the folds of his robe and into his sash and under his headcloth, patting, searching, quick and light and thorough, the way a thief searches a sleeping man.

*Laqeet,* they found first, and threw it away. That was not a name. That was what the village called him.

And then they went deeper. Into the place under the place where you keep things. And he felt them find it.

The name he had given himself at seven, sitting on the lip of this very well, saying it over and over under his breath until it made him laugh. The name he had never told anyone. The name he had whispered to the wind on the high dune, once, and heard the wind say back.

The fingers closed on it. He felt them close.

And every voice in the dark said it at once. Softly. Delighted. The way you say the name of a friend you have been waiting for at a well, when you finally see him coming over the dunes.

*Theyab.*

*There you are.*

---

One breath. That was all it was. One breath, he said. When he came back up out of the white eyes, Saqi's face was still a hand's width from his, and the moon was still overhead, and Shibl's wrist was still in his fingers, and nothing at all had changed. Except that the thing looking out of the well-keeper's eyes knew his name now. And he could see in its face that it knew.

Saqi's smile went wider.

He opened his mouth. And what came out of it, little one, was not the dead mother's voice and not Saqi's voice.

"*Theyab,*" it said, in a voice I had heard every day for eight years. "*Let go.*"

It was Theyab's voice.

His own voice, saying his own secret name, out of another man's mouth, at the bottom of a well, at midnight. Exactly his voice. The little crack in it that had started that summer. The flat, careful way he said things because he could not say them any other way.

I felt him go still. I felt every muscle in his body go still at once, through my teeth in his sash, the way a hare goes still when the falcon's shadow passes over it.

And I felt his fingers start to open on Shibl's wrist.

*No,* I thought. *No, no, no*—

And then something else happened. Something I had felt in him, on and off, his whole life, and never seen him reach for.

---

There was a warmth under the feather mark on his chest. There always had been. You will hear a great deal about it later, more than you will want to. That night all I knew was that I had felt it, through his shirt, on cold nights when he slept curled against me. A small banked warmth like a coal under ash. It had come out of him before, once or twice, never because he asked it to. Like a sneeze. Like tears.

He had never once reached for it.

He reached for it then.

I felt him do it. I felt him stop being afraid of it, and stop being afraid of the voice, and stop being afraid of the name. I felt him think of the small wrist in his hand and nothing else in the world. And I felt him go down into himself, past the cold of the well and the whispering, down to the place under the feather where the coal was, and put his hand on it.

And pull.

The light came out of him like the sun coming up over Tal Moreeb.

It came out of his chest first, through his shirt, gold, so bright that the moon went out. It ran down his arm. I watched it run, little one. I watched it pour down his arm like water poured down a channel, from the shoulder to the elbow to the wrist to the fingers, and out of his fingers into Shibl's wrist, and through Shibl, the whole small body lit up gold from inside like a lamp made of skin, and out of Shibl into the hand that held him, and up that arm, and into the well-keeper's face.

Saqi screamed.

Both voices. All the voices. Saqi's and the dead woman's and Theyab's, and under them, for one instant, something else, a sound that was not a voice at all, a sound like water thrown on hot iron, like a forge quenched, a hiss and a shriek together.

And he was thrown.

Not down the well. Up and out of it. The light hit him the way a falling wall hits a man, and he went up out of the shaft past us backwards, his arms flung out, and his hand opened, and Shibl came free. Saqi went over our heads. He went clear across the well-court through the air like a sack thrown from a roof, and he hit the mud wall of his own house on the far side with a sound I will hear until I am dust: a deep heavy thud, and inside it, very clearly, many small cracks, one after another, like a handful of dry sticks broken in a fist.

He slid down the wall and lay at the foot of it.

And Theyab was lying on the lip of the well with his arm down the shaft, and Shibl's wrist still in his fingers, and Shibl hanging in the dark below, lit gold from inside, alive.

I pulled.

I set my back legs and pulled with my teeth in his sash and everything I had, broken ribs and all, and he came back over the lip, and his arm came up out of the shaft, and Shibl came up with it, and the two of them fell backward together onto the stones of the well-court in a heap of gold light.

---

The light did not go out at once.

It ran off him slowly, the way water runs off a man who has climbed out of the falaj. It ran off his face and his hands and pooled on the stones round him and sank into them and was gone. Shibl was curled against his chest, shaking so hard his teeth rattled, both fists knotted in Theyab's shirt. He was not making any sound at all.

Theyab sat up with the child in his arms.

He looked down at his own arms.

There were cracks in them.

From his wrists to his elbows, along the inside of both forearms, thin bright lines had opened in his skin. Like the cracks in a glazed water-jar that has been left in the sun too long. Like the lines on the floor of a dry wadi. They glowed. Gold, faint, pulsing very slightly, in time with his heart. They did not bleed. They looked as though they should.

"Ghareeb," he said. His voice was quite calm. "Ghareeb, it hurts."

I came and put my head against his side. It was all I could do. My own side was on fire and I could not get a full breath, and I put my head against him, and he put his free hand on my neck, and I felt the cracks in his palm, hot as a cooking stone.

Across the court, at the foot of the wall, Saqi lay where he had fallen.

He was not moving. One arm was underneath him. The other lay out on the sand, the wrist I had bitten turned all the way round, the palm facing up at the stars where the back of the hand should be. His shirt had come open. Even in the moonlight I could see the shape of his chest was wrong, caved in down one side like a goatskin with the air let out of it. There was blood at the corner of his mouth, black in that light. His eyes were open.

Still white.

---

They came with torches.

They had heard the scream, the whole village had heard it, and by the time I looked up, the lanes round the well-court were full of fire. Men with palm-frond torches and oil lamps, men with old matchlocks and camel-sticks and one with a hoe. Sheikh Mazyad with his hennaed beard and his robe on inside out. Ghanim behind him, holding his lamp high and well back, the way a man holds a lamp when he wants to see and not be seen. Women at the edges, in their black, with their hands over their mouths.

And Nadim. On his feet in the gap of the broken arish wall, one hand pressed to his ribs, palm fronds stuck in his hair, and a long cut down his forehead bleeding into his eye.

They saw it all at once. I watched them see it.

The well-keeper, the gentle well-keeper, the man who had given a stranger coffee and combed his son's hair, lying broken at the foot of his own wall, his arm turned round backwards and his chest stove in.

And in the middle of the court, by the open mouth of the well, the foundling.

On his knees. With the well-keeper's son clutched against his chest. With gold light still dying on the stones round him and gold cracks shining in his arms. With an old dog at his side, a dog whose shadow, for one more breath, in the torchlight, was the wrong shape.

Nobody said anything.

The torches hissed. Somewhere a camel groaned in its pen. Shibl's teeth chattered against Theyab's collarbone.

Then Saqi lifted his head.

It came up off the sand slowly. His neck should not have been able to do it. Something in his chest grated when he moved, and blood ran out of the corner of his mouth into his beard, and the white eyes turned in his face until they found the crowd. And then they found Theyab.

He opened his mouth.

"*He did this to me.*"

It was Theyab's voice.

Every man in that court knew it. They had heard it all their lives at the edges of things, saying true things they did not want to hear. The careful, flat, honest voice of the foundling who could not lie. Coming out of the broken well-keeper's mouth, clear and quiet, in the torchlight.

"*He came in the night,*" said Theyab's voice, out of Saqi. "*He came for the little one. And when I stopped him, he did this to me.*"

I felt my hackles go up. And then I felt them not know what to do.

Do you see how clever it was, little one? I can smell a lie. It has been my one useful gift among people. And what the thing was saying was a lie: Theyab had not come for Shibl, not the way it meant. But it was also true. He *had* come in the night. He *had* come for the little one. And he *had* done that, to that man, with his own light, in front of my own eyes. The bones in that broken chest were broken by Theyab.

The lie was built out of truths, the way a wall is built out of good bricks. My hackles stood half up along my spine and stayed there, uncertain, and my growl started and stopped, and started, and every man in the court saw the old dog growl at nothing and stop, and growl, and stop.

Sheikh Mazyad looked at Theyab.

"Laqeet," he said. His voice was not steady. "Is it true? Did you do that?"

And Theyab, who has never been able to say a thing he did not believe, looked at the broken man at the foot of the wall, and at the cracks in his own arms, and opened his mouth.

"Yes," he said. "I did. But—"

Nobody heard the *but*.

They heard *yes*. I watched it go through the crowd like wind through dry grass. I watched faces close. I watched hands tighten on matchlocks and camel-sticks. Ghanim's lamp dipped as he leaned to whisper in the ear of the man beside him.

Nadim came across the court.

He walked past his father without looking at him. He walked straight to Theyab and bent down and took Shibl out of his arms. Theyab did not hold on. Shibl did, for a moment, his fists still knotted in Theyab's shirt. Nadim pulled, and the shirt tore, and Shibl came away, and Nadim held him against his own chest and turned his back.

He did not say anything to Theyab. Not one word. He did not look at him.

That was worse than anything he could have said. I think Theyab would have taken a stone more easily.

---

They tied Saqi to the post in the sheikh's yard with camel-rope, because nobody knew what else to do. They tied him sitting, with his broken arm bound across his broken chest, and he did not fight. He sat against the post with his head hanging and his white eyes open, and he smiled.

They did not tie Theyab. Not that night. But four men with matchlocks sat round the fire in the sheikh's yard until dawn, and they sat between Theyab and the lanes, and they did not let him go home.

So we sat against the far wall of the yard, the boy and the old dog, across the fire from the Worn man at the post. I lay with my head on Theyab's knee, breathing in short shallow pulls because a long one hurt too much. Theyab sat with his arms held out from his body, palms up, as if they did not belong to him. The cracks were dimmer now. They still glowed when his heart beat hard.

Every so often, through the long hours, when the men round the fire were dozing or talking low among themselves, Saqi lifted his head and looked across the yard at us.

And whispered. Too low for the men to hear. Just loud enough for a boy and a dog.

In Theyab's voice.

"*Theyab,*" it whispered, very softly, very fondly, the way you say the name of a friend. "*Theyab. Theyab. There you are.*"

Theyab put his hands over his ears. The cracks in his palms lit the sides of his face gold. It did not help. You cannot shut out your own voice, little one. It is already inside.

---

Dawn came up grey. Not the Grey; only the ordinary grey of an early morning in the cold season, with the dunes the colour of ash and then of sand and then of honey as the light came. The men round the fire stood up and stretched and spat and did not look at us.

And from the east, out of the flat dead place where the colour stopped, a man came walking.

He was thin, and old, older than Sheikh Mazyad and younger than Old Bakhit, and the sun had burned him nearly black. He leaned on a stick of ghaf wood. He wore a goat-hair cloak, the rough brown kind the shepherds wear, though he was no shepherd, and it was dusty to the knees, as if he had walked a long way. He was not hurrying.

He came out of the Grey. Straight out of it, out of the eastern lane where the emptied stood at their doors. And they turned their faces away from him as he passed. One after another. The way you turn your face from a wind full of sand.

His lips were moving. He was reciting something under his breath, steadily, the way a man walks to a rhythm, and the rhythm came ahead of him up the lane like the first cool air before a shamal.

At the post in the sheikh's yard, Saqi stopped smiling.

*End of Chapter Eight*
