# Chapter Four
## Never Tell the Wind Who You Are

> *"Say your name to the wind and the wind will find someone to sell it to."*
> — a herders' saying

---

He hammered on the sheikh's door before dawn.

He'd lain awake all night with Shibl's sandal in his hand. It went cold, eventually, about the time the moon set. He watched the grey from the lip of the hollow until the stars above it went out in a long ragged line, sister after sister, and then he got up and walked into the dark village with sand in his hair and his left fist shut, and he beat on Sheikh Mazyad's door with the flat of his right hand until a lamp was lit.

It was the only house in Hilat al-Sarab with a wind-tower: a square chimney of mud and palm-trunk that caught the breeze off the dunes and pushed it down into the rooms below. The only house with a majlis, with carpets from Persia and a coffee hearth where the men sat in the evenings. Theyab had never been inside. He'd never been invited to sit on anyone's carpet in his life.

The door opened a hand's width. Then a little more.

Mazyad was a heavy man with heavy eyes and a beard like a thornbush, hennaed orange at the tips. He stood in the doorway in his undershirt with the lamp held up and his face creased from sleep, and he looked down at the foundling on his step the way you'd look at a goat that had climbed onto your roof.

Theyab told him.

The line of grey to the south and east. The stars going out above it. He didn't tell him about the voice in the wind. He didn't tell him about the hoofprints or the warm sandal or the thing in the shimmer that had called him home. He was a truthful boy, not a stupid one.

Mazyad listened with his eyes half closed. When Theyab had finished he said, "Sea fog. Off the salt."

"The salt flats are four days away."

"Dust, then. A storm, far south."

"It isn't moving like a storm." Theyab hunted for the word. "It's moving like something that's listening."

Mazyad's face did nothing for a while. Then something crossed it, quick and ugly, and his eyes slid past Theyab's shoulder, toward the east, where the sky was only beginning to grey. Just for a moment. Just a glance.

I was lying in the dark across the lane. I smelled it come off him.

Not a lie. Not exactly. Fear. Fear is the lie's older brother; it comes first and makes the bed the lie sleeps in. It came off the sheikh thick and wet, like the smell off a goat that knows what the knife is for.

He'd seen it. He'd been up on his roof, in the dark, looking. He knew.

"Go back to your tent, Laqeet," said Mazyad, and shut the door. I heard the bar drop across it on the other side, heavy, the way you'd bar a door against raiders.

Theyab stood on the step with his hand still raised.

Then he went away down the lane in the first light, slowly, with his head down, and I followed. I could have barked the village awake. I could have howled in the square until every child woke screaming. I didn't.

I have rules. The one I walk beside has to see it first. He has to be the one who knows. The times I've broken that rule, things have gone worse.

Or so I tell myself. I've had a great deal of time to practise.

---

Old Bakhit's tent stood at the western edge of the village, farther out than anyone's, farther even than Theyab's. Long and low and black, of goat hair woven so tight that rain ran off it like oil. Bunches of dried harmal hung from the guy-ropes, and wild thyme, and sprigs of something grey that smelled of camphor. And everywhere, on threads, on the ropes and the poles and the edges of the flap, little brass bells. Dozens of them. They hung there in the dawn without a sound.

And on the main pole, just inside the door, one bigger bell. Old brass gone brown, with marks stamped round its rim that nobody in Hilat al-Sarab could read. It didn't hang loose like the others. It sat on a hook. It never swung in the wind. It only rang when it wanted to.

"You're up early," said a voice from inside, "for a boy who sleeps at noon."

Theyab stopped.

"And you smell of lamp-oil and somebody else's door. You went to Mazyad." A dry sound, like a stick snapping. "Of course you went to Mazyad. Come in. The tea's poured. It's been poured since the moon went down; it'll be cold and bitter, which is how you deserve it."

Theyab went in. I went in after him.

Bakhit sat on a mat at the back of the tent, cross-legged, with a brass tray in front of him and a little charcoal brazier beside it, glowing. He was the oldest man anyone in Hilat al-Sarab had ever seen. His skin was brown and creased like the bed of a wadi after a hundred dry years. His beard was white as salt. His eyes were open and milky and pale, and they didn't look at anything, and they didn't need to. He'd been blind longer than the oldest woman in the village had been alive. Bakhit means *the lucky one*. That's how the old Arabs name a blind man: gently, the way you'd step round a hole in the dark.

The village came to him for scorpion stings and fevers and bad births, and left as fast as they could. Theyab came for the stories. He'd come since he was eight, since the first time the old man had called him in out of the dark as he passed. *You there. You with the dog. Come and drink something.* Seven years of nights. Bakhit was the only one who'd ever called him anything but *Laqeet*. Bakhit was the only one he'd told his name.

There were two glasses on the tray.

There were always two. One for Theyab. Small, thick, gold-rimmed, the tea in it black as tar. And the other, its twin, poured just as full, Bakhit had already set on the ground. Not by Theyab. By the threshold. Just inside the door of the tent, on the bare sand, at the place where the inside became the out.

"For the guest," Bakhit said, as he always said.

There was no guest. There had never once been a guest, not that Theyab had ever seen. But every night, for as long as anyone in the village could remember, and for a long time before that if you believed the old women, Old Bakhit had poured two glasses, and set one at his threshold, for the guest. In the morning the glass was always empty.

It was never wet.

I didn't lie across the doorway that morning, the way I usually did. I lay down beside it. A little to one side, leaving room. I didn't decide to. My body did it, the way it moves out of the path of a camel without asking me. There was a stillness in the doorway, beside the glass, the way there's a stillness at the mouth of a tent at night when an old man is standing there with his back to the sleepers. You couldn't see it. You could only feel where it was, by where the air wasn't moving.

I'd felt a stillness like that before. Somewhere warm. Somewhere with roots.

I put my chin on my paws and didn't look at it, and I'd swear it didn't look at me. Out of politeness. The way two old men who've both outlived everyone they knew sit at the same fire and say nothing.

---

Theyab drank the tea. He hated it. He'd hated it for seven years, every night, and he'd have died before saying so. It was black and bitter and smoky with some green thing Bakhit would never name, and it went down like a punishment, and afterwards, always, he felt better.

He told Bakhit everything.

He couldn't have told it any other way. The thaler. Ghanim's smile. The snake. The name on the dune, and the wind saying it back. The grey. The tracks at his blanket. Umm Hanan's tale through the frond wall. Shibl walking toward the shimmer. The voice that called him home.

Bakhit didn't interrupt. He sat with his blind face turned a little to one side, the way a man listens to a far-off camel bell to tell whose camel it is. When Theyab got to the part about the dune, the old man's hand stopped halfway to his glass, and stayed there, in the air, for the rest of the telling.

When it was done, Bakhit said, "Are you frightened?"

Theyab thought about it. Truly. He always had to; it was the only way he could answer anything. He thought about the voice on the dune and the warm sandal and Mazyad's barred door, and he looked inside himself the way you'd look into a dark tent for a lost knife, and he didn't find fear. He found anger. He found a sick tiredness. He found something else he didn't have a word for. But not fear.

"I'm not afraid," he said.

The feather on his chest stayed cool. I smelled nothing on him. Nothing at all. He meant it.

And the bell on the pole rang.

Once. One clear, cold note, like a drop of water falling into a deep well. Not loud. It didn't need to be loud. It hung in the tent for a long time after, and all the little bells on their threads stayed perfectly still, as if listening to it.

Theyab stared at the bell.

"I didn't lie," he whispered. "I can't. It didn't burn."

"No," said Bakhit. "You didn't lie. You believed it." He set his glass down on the tray, very gently. "That bell doesn't care what you believe, boy. It only cares what's true. The liar it rings for is very often the one who's speaking. Most people go their whole lives without once hearing it ring for them. You should feel honoured."

Theyab didn't look honoured. He looked like someone who's just been told there's a room in his own house that he's never been into.

"What's in it?" he said. "The bell."

Bakhit smiled with half his mouth. "Something that can't lie, and can't leave. Drink your tea."

My hackles stayed flat. Every word of that was true. I've wondered since how a blind old man came to own a bell like that, and what he paid for it, and to whom. I've never asked him. He'd have told me, and I'd have had to know.

---

"There are four things," Bakhit said.

He held up his hand, the fingers crooked as ghaf roots.

"Four things the old people say never to do. Not the mutawwa's. Older than that. Your grandmothers' grandmothers. You've heard them all, but you've heard them the way children hear things, as noises the grown ones make. Listen to them now the way a man hears them."

He folded down one finger.

"Never walk at noon."

Another.

"Never answer the Grey. If it calls, and it will, in whatever voice it wears, you don't answer. Not *yes*, not *who's there*, not your own name. Nothing."

A third.

"Never follow a light. In the dunes, at night, where no fire should be. However kind it looks. However close."

And the last.

"Never say your name to the wind."

He left his hand there, with the thumb alone standing up, crooked, like a man left on a dune after the caravan has gone.

"Which have you done?"

The tent was very quiet. The brazier ticked. By the threshold, beside the glass, the stillness didn't move.

Theyab didn't answer straight away. Not because he wanted to lie. He couldn't. Because he was counting.

"Two," he said.

Bakhit's blind face didn't move at all. But I saw his thumb, still standing up by itself in the air, begin, very slightly, to shake.

"Two," he said. He lowered his hand. "Well. Nobody ever called you in from either of them."

He didn't say anything else about it. He poured more tea instead, for Theyab and for the guest, and he talked about scorpions, and about the price of rice, and about a camel he'd once owned that could find water by the smell of it under a hundred feet of sand. He talked until the sun was well up and the tea was gone. It was the talk of an old man filling a silence because he doesn't trust what's waiting at the bottom of it.

But when we left, he said, at the flap, without turning his blind face toward us:

"Theyab."

The boy stopped.

"Keep that dog close. Closer than you think you need to."

I looked back at him. He was looking at me. I mean he had turned his face to exactly where I was, and his milky eyes were on me, and he was blind, and he was looking.

My silver eye gave a single throb.

The bell on the pole didn't ring.

---

The stranger rode in after noon.

I saw him first because I was lying in the shade of Ghanim's back wall, which was where I always lay when Theyab was at the well, because Ghanim's back wall was where you could see the whole square. A camel came in from the south, from the deep sands, down the long lane between the palms. An old grey she-camel with a split ear and a saddle hung with leather bags. On her, a man.

He was old. Not Bakhit-old; nothing was Bakhit-old. But old in the way the sand is old, weathered down to the frame. A Bedouin of the deep sands by his dress: a brown cloak, a headcloth wound high and tight, a rifle across the saddle, an old Martini with the stock wrapped in leather. His beard was grey and short. His face was long and lined and patient, the face of a man who has spent most of his life waiting for things to come up out of the ground.

He made his camel kneel at the edge of the square, and got down, stiff, and came across to Ghanim's stall.

I smelled him.

Wet clay. That was the first thing, in that dry place where nothing was wet. Cold wet clay, like the bottom of a well that's been dug and abandoned. And under the clay, old earth. Not sand: earth. The deep, dark kind that's never seen the sun, that you find only when you dig a long way down. And under that, faint, so faint I almost didn't catch it, the smell of a grave that's been opened.

His hands were long and brown and knotted, and there was clay under every nail. Grey clay, packed in so deep that it must never come out. As if he'd been digging all his life and had given up trying to wash.

Under his arm, wrapped in oiled cloth and tied with a cord, he carried a book.

I'd never seen a Bedouin of the deep sands carry a book. They carry their knowledge in their heads, in songs and sayings and the shapes of dunes. A book, out there, is a strange heavy thing, like a stone a man won't put down.

"Dates," he said to Ghanim. His voice was quiet and dry. "Your best."

Ghanim's oiled face had gone very still.

They knew each other. Not well, not as friends. The way a man knows the buyer he sent word to. I watched Ghanim weigh out dates with no thumb on the scale at all, which I'd never once seen him do, and I watched the stranger pay with a coin that rang on the mat like a bell. Silver.

And I watched them lean together, over the dates, and speak low.

My ears are better than yours. Most of it I heard.

"…the foundling," said the stranger. "The one from the noon."

"Yes."

"Which hand?"

"The left. He keeps it shut. Always. But I saw. When I paid him." Ghanim's voice dropped further. "Straight across. Unbroken. Edge to edge. I swear it on my children."

My hackles twitched at the oath, since Ghanim had no children, and lay down again at the rest, which was true.

The stranger didn't say anything for a long moment. He picked up a date and looked at it and didn't eat it.

"Where does he sleep?"

"In the hollow at the south end. Past the last house. A tent of rags." Ghanim licked his lips. "And the other thing? The thing I sent word about? The silver you said—"

"You'll be paid," said the stranger. "When it's done."

I smelled the lie off him the way you smell smoke off a man who's been standing by a fire. Sour, and old, and comfortable. A lie he'd told so many times, to so many men like Ghanim, that it had worn smooth.

Ghanim didn't smell it. People never do.

The stranger turned to go. And then he stopped. And turned his head. And looked across the square, past the well, past the palms, straight at Ghanim's back wall. At me.

He looked at me for a long time.

Not at my body. At my eye. The silver one. His own eyes were dark and narrow and very, very patient, and they went into the silver the way a spade goes into soft ground.

My eye ached. It ached so hard I couldn't see out of it for a moment. It was like having a hand pressed over it from the inside.

"That's an old dog," said the stranger, softly, to no one.

Then he went back to his kneeling camel and got up on her, stiff, and rode out of the square the way he'd come, south, toward the deep sands, without hurrying. The book was still under his arm.

Ghanim stood behind his stall and watched him go. And then he looked at his own clean, oiled hands. And for a moment, just a moment, he looked like a man who has sold a well and has just remembered that he lives in the desert.

His name, I learned later, was Haddaj. Haddaj al-Hafar. Haddaj the Digger.

I'll tell you about the Diggers. Not all of it. Enough.

---

They came that night.

I'd like to tell you I fought them off at the door of the tent. I'd like to tell you I heard them a mile away and was waiting with my teeth bared. I'd like to tell you a great many things, little one, and I won't, because you'd smell it on me, the way I smell it on everyone else.

Here's what happened.

Near midnight, out in the dark beyond the hollow, I heard a sound. *Tik.*

Just once. Small and clear. On a stone.

I was up before I'd thought. Out of the tent and up the lip of the hollow with my hackles standing and my lips back, staring into the dark lane where the hoofprints had come down at noon. Staring hard. With both eyes. Waiting for the next *tik*.

It didn't come. Of course it didn't. Something had dropped a single small stone on a single flat rock, once, to see what the dog would do.

And while I stood on the lip of the hollow facing the lane, they came over the dune from the other side.

I heard the tent ropes go. I heard a sound inside, short, muffled, cut off in the middle, the sound a boy makes when a hand comes down over his mouth in his sleep. By the time I'd turned and come down the slope they had him.

Five men. Six. It was dark; the moon was a fat white slice going down in the west. They had their faces smeared grey with clay so that in that light they had no faces at all, only grey shapes with holes for eyes. Two of them had Theyab, one at his shoulders and one at his knees, and a third was tying a cloth round his mouth. He was fighting. His right fist was hitting anything it could reach. His left, even then, was shut.

A fourth man knelt beside him with a clay pot in his hands. He dipped his fingers in it and smeared wet grey clay across Theyab's eyes. Thick. Over the lids. Like you'd seal the mouth of a jar.

"So he can't see the way down," said a quiet, dry voice from the dark. "So he can't call them up."

Haddaj.

He was standing a little way off, at the top of the dune, with his book under his arm. He wasn't helping. He was watching, the way a man watches someone else dig a well he's paid for.

I went for the one with the pot.

I'm a saluki. I'm not a fighting dog. We're not made for it; we're made for speed, for running down a hare across open gravel and breaking its neck before it knows it's caught. But I'm old, and I've learned things. I went in low and fast and I didn't go for his throat, which he was guarding. I went for his wrist, and I closed my jaws on it, and I felt the bones in it give.

He screamed. The pot went flying and broke on a stone and wet clay went everywhere, grey in the moonlight, like something spilled out of a body.

Then something hit me across the back. A club, or a rifle butt. The world went white. I was on my side in the sand with my legs going, and a man was standing over me with something raised, and then Haddaj said, sharp, "Not the dog. Not yet. Leave the dog," and the man stopped. And they had Theyab in a sack. A big grain sack, rough brown cloth, and they hauled it up between two of them and ran.

Not back over the dune. Through the village.

Their camels were on the far side, hobbled in the palm grove, where the ground was soft and the hoofbeats wouldn't carry. The shortest way to the grove was through the lanes. They thought the village was asleep. They thought, quite rightly, that the village wouldn't stir itself for Laqeet.

I got up.

I don't know how. My back was a long bar of fire. One of my back legs didn't want to work. I got up anyway and I went after them into the dark lanes of Hilat al-Sarab, and I'll tell you the truth, little one, I wasn't running like a saluki any more. I was running like something else. Something heavier. Something that didn't care about a leg that didn't work. The lane walls went past me in a long dark smear and I could hear the sack bumping and the boy in it making sounds through the gag, and the men's feet, and their breathing, and I came up behind the last of them and took him behind the knee.

He went down like a cut tent. I went over him. The next one turned and swung his rifle at me and I went under it and had his robe, and then his leg, and he was screaming, and the sack came down. They'd dropped it. It was lying in the lane, moving.

I stood over it.

There were four of them still standing, and Haddaj somewhere behind them in the dark, and me, with one leg I couldn't trust and my back on fire, standing over a grain sack with a boy in it, in a lane in a village that wouldn't wake up.

Then the light came on.

---

It was Umm Hanan's lamp.

We were in the back lane, right outside her house. I hadn't known. I hadn't been looking at anything but the sack. And behind the indigo door, through the gap at its foot, a light came up. Not slowly, the way a lamp comes up when someone wakes and fumbles for the flint. All at once. As if someone had breathed on an ember.

I saw it with my silver eye. Only for a heartbeat. I'll tell you exactly what I saw, and you can do with it what you like.

Inside the door, over the little clay lamp that sat in the niche by the threshold, something was bending. Tall. Thin. Patient. Bending over the flame the way an old servant bends over a sleeping child to tuck in the blanket. It had no face I could see. It had a kind of stillness that was almost a face. And the flame of the lamp stood up under it straight and tall and gold, and then it reached across the dark room toward the mat where the old woman lay asleep, and touched her, very lightly, on the shoulder.

Then the silver eye went dark with pain and I couldn't see it any more.

The door flew open.

Umm Hanan came out into the lane in her sleeping-dress with her hair loose and grey down her back and her lamp held up over her head in one hand, high, as high as her old arm would go. The light fell on the lane. On the men with their clay faces. On the sack, moving. On me, standing over it with my lips back and my legs shaking.

And Umm Hanan screamed.

Not a word. Not a name. Just a scream, high and long and terrible, the scream of a woman who has heard something take a child out of a lane before and has never, not for one day in forty years, stopped hearing it. It went up between the walls and out over the roofs and into every house in Hilat al-Sarab.

The men with clay faces stood frozen in her lamplight. Grey. Faceless. Like things that had come up out of the ground and been caught there.

Doors opened.

---

I want to tell you that the village came running.

They didn't. They came. That's different.

They came out of their doors one by one, men in their undershirts, women with their abayas thrown on, children peering from behind their legs, and they stood in the mouths of the lanes and they looked. At the old widow with her lamp up. At the strangers with no faces. At the sack in the lane, and the shape in it, and the dog standing over it.

They knew who was in the sack. You could see them know it.

And for a long moment, nobody moved.

I remember every face. I'll remember them when I'm dust. Sheikh Mazyad, in his doorway, with his lamp, not coming down off his step. Ghanim, in the shadow of his stall, very still, not looking at anyone. Saqi, with his stick in his hand, and Nadim behind him, and Shibl behind Nadim with his face pressed against his brother's back. Men I'd watched draw water and mend ropes and pray and quarrel and bury their fathers. All of them, standing in the mouths of the lanes with sticks and lamps in their hands, looking at the strangers who were carrying off the foundling.

Thinking.

I could smell them think it. It came off them like heat off a stone. *Let them. Let them take him. Whatever he is. Wherever he came from. Let them take it back.*

Nobody said it out loud. Nobody had to.

It was a long moment. It was the longest moment I've lived in a very long life.

And then Saqi spat in the dust and came down off his step.

Not for Theyab. I want to be exact. He didn't come down for the boy. He came down the way a man comes down when strangers are doing something in *his* village, in *his* lane, under *his* well, without asking him. His face was dark and ugly with it. "Not here," he said. "Not in my lane, you sons of dogs." And he lifted his stick.

That was all it took. One man. The others came down after him, the way goats follow the first goat through a gap. Sticks. A torch. A woman with a pestle. Somebody's old matchlock that hadn't been fired in ten years, pointed at the sky.

The Diggers broke.

They didn't fight. They weren't there to fight a village; they were there to steal a boy in the dark while the dog was looking the wrong way. They ran. They dragged their two hurt men and they ran for the palm grove, and a moment later I heard camels groaning up off their knees, and hooves on the soft ground, going south.

Haddaj went last.

He came down out of the dark at the end of the lane, slowly, without any hurry at all, with his book under his arm, and he stopped at the edge of Umm Hanan's lamplight. The village stood with their sticks raised and looked at him, and he looked back at them with his patient narrow eyes. He wasn't afraid of them. I smelled no fear on him at all. Only a kind of mild contempt, like a man looking at sheep.

Then he looked at the sack.

"You know what you're keeping?" he said.

Nobody answered.

"That's a son of the ones from below." He said it quietly, the way you'd say the price of rice. "You found it in your lane at noon, and you never asked where it came from. Well. I'm telling you. And his blood is owed to us. We'll come back for it."

He spat into the dust. A small, neat, deliberate spit, a hand's width from the sack.

Then he turned and walked away into the dark toward the grove, and nobody went after him.

---

Nobody asked what it meant.

Not that night, and not the next day, and not for all the days after. Nobody went to Old Bakhit and said, *what is a son of the ones from below?* Nobody went to the mutawwa' in Liwa. Nobody said it at the well.

But everybody remembered it.

I could tell. For weeks afterward, when Theyab walked through the square, I'd see it. Not in what they said. In where they looked. They'd look at his hands. At his face. At his feet, sometimes, as if checking how they touched the ground. And women would draw their children in a little closer as he passed, the way you draw your robe back from a fire.

*A son of the ones from below.* The village had never had a word for what Theyab was. They'd had *Laqeet*, and *monster*, and *the thing from the noon*. Now a stranger had ridden in and handed them one, and they took it the way a hungry man takes bread from anyone. They didn't chew it. They just swallowed.

*His blood is owed to us.*

They swallowed that too.

---

Saqi cut the sack open with his knife.

Not gently. He slit it from top to bottom the way you'd slit a goat, and the boy came out of it onto the dust in the lamplight, and lay there, with the gag still in his mouth and his eyes still sealed shut with grey clay, his chest heaving. His left fist was still closed. Even then.

Saqi looked down at him. He didn't help him up. He didn't say anything. He looked at him for a long moment, and his face was hard to read. Then he turned and walked back to his house and took his sons in with him, and barred the door.

One by one, the others went in too.

It was Umm Hanan who knelt.

She didn't speak. She set her lamp down on the step, and its flame stood up straight and gold and unwavering, though the night wind was blowing down the lane hard enough to lift the dust. She untied the gag. Then she took the corner of her sleeping-dress and wiped the clay off his eyes, one and then the other, slowly, carefully, the way you'd wipe the face of a child who has fallen in the mud.

He opened his eyes and looked at her.

She looked at him back. For the first time in eight years, the old widow and the foundling looked at each other's faces, close, by lamplight.

Then she picked up her lamp and went inside, and shut her indigo door, and didn't come out.

He lay in the lane a while longer. I lay against him and licked the last of the clay from the corners of his eyes. It tasted of the deep earth, of the place a long way down where the sun has never been.

---

He walked home alone. I walked with him, limping.

The lanes were dark again. Every door was barred. Behind them I could hear people not sleeping, talking low, the words coming through the walls the way the wind came through the frond walls of the arish. *From below. Owed. Blood.*

We came out past the last house, onto the hard ground where the hollow was, and the dunes rose up in front of us, black against the stars, with the moon gone down and the grey lying along the far south like a held breath.

And the dunes were hissing.

They do that at night. The wind runs over the crests and lifts the fine sand and pours it down the slip-faces in long thin streams, and it makes a sound. A hiss. A whisper. A long dry *ssssss* that goes on and on, like the sea far off. The herders say if you listen long enough you can hear words in it. They say it to frighten children.

Theyab stopped.

I stopped too.

Because in the hiss, very faint, very close, there was a word.

*"Theyab."*

His own voice.

Not out on the far dunes this time. Not on the wind from the south. Close. Just behind him. Exactly one step behind him, at the height of his own mouth, as if someone were walking in his footprints and had leaned forward to speak into the back of his neck.

*"Theyab."*

The way you'd try a word to see if it fits.

He didn't turn round. I'll give him that. He'd been told, and he'd listened. *Never answer the Grey.* He stood with his shoulders up round his ears and his left fist shut so tight the nails cut his palm, and he didn't answer, and he didn't turn round.

I turned round.

There was nothing there.

Nothing. Sand. Our own two tracks, his and mine, going back toward the village in the starlight. Nothing standing in them. Nothing walking. No smell. Not a lie, not a fear, not clay, not milk, not the hot hide of the Noon Mare. Nothing at all.

And every hair on my back stood up. From my skull to the tip of my tail. Stiff as the spines on a thornbush. And the growl came up out of my chest, low and long and shaking, the growl I've only ever made for one thing in all my years. For a lie.

At nothing.

At an empty place in the sand, one step behind him, the exact height of his mouth.

*"Theyab,"* said the dunes, in his voice, gently.

And then, so softly I wasn't sure I'd heard it at all:

*"Turn round."*
