# Chapter Three
## Don't Let the Old Women Frighten the Children

> *"The noon has teeth, and the teeth have hooves."*
> — the grandmothers of Liwa

---

In the morning the grey was still there.

It lay along the bottom of the south-eastern sky like a smear of ash on a clean plate, and in daylight it was easy not to see. The sun bleached it. A herder looking that way would have called it haze, or dust off some far storm, and turned back to his goats. Theyab looked at it all morning. Every time he looked away and looked back, he couldn't be sure it was nearer, and he couldn't be sure it wasn't.

He didn't tell anyone. Not yet. That day he only watched, and at noon, when the mothers began to call, he walked into the lanes the way he always did. I went with him.

You'll say: *after what was standing at his blanket, he went out at noon again?*

Yes, little one. Where else would he go? Nobody called him in.

---

Umm Hanan's house had a summer room.

An *arish*: palm fronds lashed to a frame of trunks, that let the wind through in a thousand thin threads and kept the sun out. Hers was old and grey and stood against the back lane, a hand's width of dry frond between the inside and the out.

That day, passing in the white glare, Theyab heard a voice through the fronds, and he stopped.

It was her voice. Old and dry and low, the way you speak to children when you want them to lean in. And he heard Shibl say, "Tell the one about her. Tell the one with the hooves."

Theyab sat down in the slip of shade against the frond wall.

I put my chin on his foot. Through the fronds I could smell them all: Umm Hanan's flour and smoke, Shibl's dates and goat, and two others, a girl with a cough and her small brother, whose herding mother had gone to the coast. Umm Hanan kept them through the noon. Saqi didn't want his younger son underfoot while he slept, and there was no mother at the well-keeper's house to want him. So Shibl came to the widow's arish, and she gave him bread, and he gave her someone to talk to. It was a fair trade. Most of the fair trades in that village happened where Ghanim couldn't see them.

"That one's not for small children," said Umm Hanan.

"I'm seven," said Shibl.

"That's what I said."

"*Please.*"

A long pause. Fronds creaking. Then a sigh, and the sound of an old woman settling her bones on a mat.

"Lie down, then. All of you. Faces to the roof. Hands on your bellies. And don't you dare tell your fathers I told it."

I'll give it to you the way she gave it. I've kept her words, as near as an old dog can keep anything. Hold your breath, little one.

---

*There was, and there was not, in the old time, when the wells were sweeter than they are and the sand was the same as it's always been, a creature that walked at noon.*

*From the waist up she was a woman. Tall. Taller than your father. She had long hair, black hair, so long and so heavy that it hung down over her face like a curtain over a door, and you never saw what was behind it. Never. Not her eyes, not her mouth. Only hair.*

*From the waist down, she was a she-donkey.*

*Grey. Dusty. The grey of a donkey that's been worked all its life and never once brushed. Her legs were thin and her knees bent the wrong way, and at the bottom of them she had hooves. Small hooves. Neat as cups. And when she walked on the hot stones of a lane, her hooves went* tik. *And* tik. *And* tik.

*She is the Noon Mare.*

*She doesn't come at night. You're all afraid of the night. No. The dark is honest. She comes at noon, when every grown person is asleep with a cloth over their face, and the lanes are white and empty, and nobody is watching. Up one lane and down the next. Slowly. Tik. Tik. Looking at the doors.*

*She's looking for children who are out.*

The small boy said something. Too low for me to hear.

*No. She doesn't eat them. Who told you that? She's not hungry for meat. Hush and listen.*

*She has rules. Everything from below has rules. If you know them, you might live. Here are hers.*

*The first rule. She speaks in your mother's voice.*

*Not like your mother. In it. If your mother has a cough at the back of her throat when she says your name, the Noon Mare has the cough. If your mother calls you* habibi *the way mothers do when they're tired, the Noon Mare says it with the tiredness in it. She stands at the end of the lane in the shaking air, where you can't see her legs, and she calls you. Come in. Come in from the sun.*

*And you go. Of course you go. Wouldn't you go?*

*That's why mothers call their children in at noon. Do you understand now? It isn't to be cross. It's so that you've already heard the real voice, so that you're already inside, with the door shut, before the other one starts.*

*The second rule.*

Umm Hanan stopped. I heard her breathe. In and out, long, the breath of someone lifting a heavy jar onto her head.

*The second rule is that she only takes the children no one is calling.*

*If your mother has called your name at noon, the Noon Mare can't have you. She can stand at your door all she likes. She can scratch the wood with her hooves till it's white. She can't come in. Your name's been said by someone who loves you, and that's a wall. But a child nobody called, a child out in the white lane with nobody's voice behind him…*

*That one, she can have.*

Beside me, against the frond wall, Theyab went still.

Still the way a hare goes still when the falcon's shadow crosses it. His left fist closed tighter on his knee, and I felt the shudder go down him through the foot my chin was resting on.

*The third rule,* said Umm Hanan.

*She never takes them far.*

*That's the cruellest. She takes them just over the next dune. Just past where you can see. And she keeps them there. And they call.*

*For days, they call. You can hear them from the village. "Mama. Mama. I'm here. I'm just here." Close. So close. Just past the next dune. And you go out and you climb the dune and there's nothing. And from the next one: "Mama, I'm here." And you climb that one. And from the next.*

*Until one day the voice stops calling Mama.*

*And starts calling your name. In your child's voice. Calling you out. At noon.*

Nobody inside the arish said anything for a long time. The fronds creaked. Somewhere a dove started, and stopped, as if it had thought better of it.

Then the girl with the cough said, very small, "Is it true, Umm Hanan?"

---

She didn't answer straight away.

And then she told them the rest. Not as a tale. She didn't say *there was and there was not*. She just started talking, in a different voice, lower and flatter, the way you'd tell someone where you'd left the goats.

"There was a woman in this village," she said, "forty years ago. Young. Younger than your mothers. She had a husband who went down to the coast every summer for the pearls, and two little sons not yet old enough to go with him, and a daughter. The daughter was the eldest. Five years old. Fat cheeks. A gap in her teeth. She could never sit still. Never.

"One summer it was very hot. Hotter than this. The woman had been up all night with the younger boy, who had a fever, and at noon she was so tired her bones hurt. And her daughter said, *Mama, let me play in the shade by the wall. Just by the wall. I'll stay in the shade.* And the woman was so tired. So tired. And it was only the wall, just outside the door, just a step, and the shade was there.

"And she said, *Yes. Stay by the wall.*

"And she lay down. Only for a moment."

The fronds creaked.

"She woke because someone was calling. In the lane. A woman's voice. Calling her daughter's name. Sweetly. *Come, come to Mama, come in from the sun.* And she lay on her mat half asleep and she thought, *Who is that calling my girl? What woman is calling my girl?* And then she understood whose voice it was.

"It was hers.

"It was her own voice. Her own exact voice. With the tiredness in it."

The small boy began to cry. Quietly. Shibl hushed him. I heard the rustle of an arm going round him.

"She ran out. The lane was empty. White. Hot. Nobody. The shade by the wall was empty. And in the dust of the lane there were little hoofprints, two by two, going away toward the end of the lane where the air was shaking.

"And one sandal.

"One small sandal, lying on its side in the lane. She picked it up." Umm Hanan's voice did something, then. Not a break. A bend, like a palm in a shamal. "It was warm. Warm as bread just off the plate. As if a foot had only that moment come out of it.

"The men went out. They searched three days. And for three days, from the dunes, the woman heard her daughter calling. *Mama. Mama, I'm here.* Just over the next dune. Always the next one. And on the fourth day…"

She stopped.

"On the fourth day, the girl's voice stopped calling *Mama*. It started calling the woman. By her name. Her own name, that nobody had called her since her own mother died. From the dunes. At noon. In her daughter's voice.

"Her husband tied her to the tent pole. For a whole month at noon he tied her, every day, so that she couldn't go. She bit him. She screamed until her voice went. And then one noon the calling stopped, and it never came again.

"That's all."

The girl with the cough whispered, "What was the daughter's name?"

And Umm Hanan said, "Hanan."

---

You know what it means, little one. *Umm Hanan.* The mother of Hanan. In the sands you're called by your eldest child's name. Forty years, every person who greeted her at the well said her lost daughter's name to her face, and had no idea they were doing it.

"Is that true?" whispered the girl again. "Is it true, Umm Hanan? Did it happen?"

And Umm Hanan said, "It's a tale. Go to sleep."

My hackles rose.

Slowly, all the way down my back. I couldn't help it. A growl started in my chest and I swallowed it so hard it hurt. Theyab put his hand on my neck without looking at me, and felt the hair standing up under his palm, and left his hand there.

---

It was Shibl who asked about the sea.

Children always ask the wrong question at the wrong time. It's the only reason anything true ever gets said.

"Was it her who took your men too?" he said. "The Noon Mare? The ones on the boat?"

"Shibl," said the girl, scandalised.

"No," said Umm Hanan.

She said it so quietly that I almost didn't hear. Then she said it again, louder, the way you'd shut a door.

"No. Not her. She keeps to the noon and the lanes and the dunes. The sea's not hers." A pause. The rustle of her abaya. "The sea has its own."

"Its own what?"

"Go to sleep."

"Its own *what*, Umm Hanan?"

I heard her breathe out. Long and tired. And when she spoke again her voice had gone somewhere very far away, out past the dunes and the salt flats, to the coast and the dark water beyond it.

"The crew came to see me after. The ones who came home. They sat in my yard and drank my coffee and they wouldn't look at me. And the eldest of them said that on the last night of the season, out on the banks, with the boat at anchor and the men asleep on the deck, there was a voice from the water.

"From the water. Not the shore. Out there, where it's deep and black and nothing stands. Calling. Calling names. My husband's name. My sons' names. One after another. Sweetly. And the eldest of the crew said—"

She stopped. I heard her swallow.

"He said it was my voice. That's what he said, sitting in my yard. That it was my voice, calling them in, the way I used to call them in from the sun when they were small. And my husband stood up from his sleep and walked to the side and went over, without a sound. And my elder boy went over after his father. And my younger boy, who was fourteen, ran to the side to grab them, and the voice said his name, just his name, so kindly, and he went over too. And the water closed. And nothing came up. Not a hand. Not a bubble. Nothing.

"The mutawwa's have their books," said Umm Hanan. "Big books, with all the things from below written down in them, every kind and every rank and what they eat and how to drive them off. I asked one, once, in Liwa, about the thing in the water that calls with a mother's voice.

"He said it wasn't in any book.

"He said, *then it isn't real.*"

She laughed. It was the worst sound I heard in that village, and I heard a great many bad sounds there.

"It's in no book," she said. "Go to sleep."

---

They slept. Children do, after a story like that. Fear tires them out faster than play.

Theyab didn't move from the wall.

He sat in the slip of shade with his hand still on my neck, and the noon burned on the lane in front of him, white and empty, the air at the end of it shaking like water. Every door was shut. Every child in Hilat al-Sarab was inside, behind a wall, with a cloth over its face, safe. Because somebody had called its name.

And there was one child in the lane.

He'd heard the mothers call for eight years, the way you hear the pulley at the well: a sound that had nothing to do with him. He'd never once wondered what it was *for*.

Now he knew.

It wasn't a game. It wasn't the mothers making a fuss. It was a wall. Every noon, in every house, every mother in the village built a wall around her children out of their names. And every noon, for eight years, every mother in the village had looked out at the boy in the lane and decided not to.

He was the child no one calls.

He'd always known that. But he'd thought it was a kind of loneliness. He hadn't understood until that noon that it was a kind of *door*.

I felt him shake. Not crying. He almost never cried where anyone could see, even me. Just a long fine tremor, like a rope pulled tight.

Then the arish rustled, and a small face came round the end of the frond wall.

Shibl.

He stood barefoot in the burning lane, squinting, his jug ears pink in the sun, and didn't seem surprised to see us at all.

"You heard," he said.

Theyab couldn't say no. "Yes."

Shibl thought about that. He thought about things very seriously, Shibl, with his whole face, as if thoughts were heavy and had to be carried with both hands.

"Who calls you in?" he said.

"Nobody."

Shibl looked at him a long time. Then he looked up and down the empty white lane, at the shut doors, at the shaking air at the end of it. And he took a breath, a big one, the way a small boy breathes before he jumps into a well-pool, and he said in a high, piping, perfectly serious voice:

"Come in, Theyab. Come in from the sun."

Theyab stared at him.

So did I.

Not *Laqeet*. Not *foundling*. *Theyab.* The name nobody in the world knew but a blind man and a dog. And here it was, in the lane, at noon, in the mouth of a seven-year-old boy who had no business knowing it.

"How do you know that?" Theyab whispered.

Shibl frowned, the way children frown when grown people ask them something obvious.

"You said it. Last night. On the big dune. I was on our roof. The wind brought it." He shrugged. "I liked it. It's better than Laqeet."

Theyab opened his mouth, and nothing came out of it.

The wind brought it. To the well-keeper's roof. And to other places. I thought of how far a word can go, once you've given it away.

"Go inside," Theyab said at last. Hoarse. "Shibl. Go inside. It's noon."

"You come too."

"I can't."

Shibl considered this. Then he nodded, as if it were a perfectly reasonable answer, and went back round the end of the frond wall and lay down again with the others, and in a moment I heard him breathing slow.

Theyab sat in the lane with his face in his hands.

It was the first time in eight years that anyone had called him in. He didn't go. He didn't know how.

---

The next noon, Shibl was gone.

Umm Hanan didn't miss him at first. The arish was dim and the children were three humps under a cloth, and then two, and she was old and had been up before the doves, and she slept. Saqi was asleep in his own house with the door barred. The village was asleep. Every door was shut.

Theyab was in the back lane, because that's where he'd been the day before, and because some part of him had wanted to be near that frond wall again. Near the place where someone had said his name.

He heard a voice.

Shibl's voice. High and glad. From the next lane over.

"Coming, Mama!"

Theyab was on his feet before the words finished.

Shibl's mother had been dead seven years. She'd died the night he was born. He'd never heard her voice in his life.

---

He ran. I ran ahead of him.

Round the corner of Umm Hanan's wall into the widest lane in Hilat al-Sarab, without a finger's width of shade from one end to the other, the light coming off it like heat off an iron plate. At the far end of the lane, where it opened onto the sand, the air was shaking so hard it looked like water poured over glass.

Shibl was in the middle of the lane. Walking. Barefoot. Not hurrying. One sandal on and one off. Walking the way a child walks toward his mother at the end of a long day, with his arms a little lifted, ready to be picked up.

And at the end of the lane, in the shaking air, someone was waiting for him.

I'll tell you what my amber eye saw. Only the amber. I want you to know that I kept the other one half shut, squinting, the way you squint into a dust wind, and I didn't know why I was doing it. Some old animal part of me simply didn't want to look at her with the silver. As if looking were a kind of answering. As if she might look back down that eye and see where it went.

She was tall. Taller than a man. She stood in the heat-shimmer where it was thickest, and the shimmer made her waver, the way things waver at the bottom of a well-pool. Long black hair hung down over her face to her waist, thick as a curtain, and didn't move, although the hot wind was moving everything else.

From the waist down she was wrong.

That's the only word I have. The shimmer bent around her there, the way it bends around something very hot. I saw legs. Too thin. Grey. Dusty. Bending at the knee the way no woman's legs bend: backwards, like a beast's. I saw them shift as she waited. And I heard, across the whole white length of that lane, very small and very clear on the hot stones:

*Tik.*

*Tik.*

"Habibi," she called. Soft. Warm. A young woman's voice, a little hoarse, tired, the voice of a woman who has been up all night with a baby. "Come, habibi. Come to Mama. Come in from the sun."

Shibl walked toward her.

"Shibl!" Theyab shouted.

The boy didn't turn. He didn't hear. He was smiling. I could see it from behind, in the round of his cheek. He'd never heard that voice in his life, and he knew it the way you know your own hand in the dark.

Theyab ran. I ran faster.

A saluki was made to run down a gazelle across open gravel, and I went down that lane like a thrown spear, and I passed the boy and turned and planted myself in front of him, between Shibl and the end of the lane, with every hair on my body standing up and a sound coming out of me that I'd never heard myself make. Not a growl. Lower. Something from before dogs.

Shibl walked into me. Bumped against my chest. Blinked.

The figure at the end of the lane went still.

The hair over her face didn't move. But the shimmer around her drew in, slowly, the way a breath draws in. And I felt her look at me. Not see. *Look.* The way the heat looks at you when you step out of the shade.

Then Theyab was there. He swept Shibl up off his feet, one arm under the boy's knees and one round his back, and held him against his chest, and turned.

And she called again.

Not to Shibl.

"Come in," she said. In a new voice. Not the young tired mother. An older voice. A voice that wasn't any particular woman's, and was every woman's in Hilat al-Sarab, every mother who had ever leaned out of a doorway at noon. Warm. Loving. Exactly the voice he'd heard calling other children every day of his life.

"Come in, Laqeet. Come in from the sun."

He stopped.

I saw his whole back go loose, the way a man's back goes loose when he's carried something heavy a very long way and someone finally offers to take it. Eight years of noons. Nobody had ever said it to him. And here it was, said kindly, by someone who wanted him.

He turned his head. Slowly. Toward the end of the lane. Toward the shaking air.

"Come in, habibi," said the voice. "It's so hot. Aren't you tired? Come in. Come home."

*Home.*

He took a step toward her.

And in his arms, Shibl stirred, and woke out of whatever he'd been in, and looked up at the bright sky with no idea where he was, and said, cross and sleepy and very loud, the way children talk when they've just woken in the wrong place:

"Theyab? Why are we *outside*?"

His name. In the lane. From a small mouth that loved him.

Theyab stopped.

The voice at the end of the lane said nothing.

Then he turned his back on it. He turned his back on the only noon-voice that had ever called him home, and he held Shibl tight against his chest, and he walked. Not ran. He walked, steady, back up the white lane, away from the shimmer, with me pressed against his legs so close I was nearly tripping him, and my head turned all the way round over my shoulder, watching.

Behind us, very close, much closer than the end of the lane:

*Tik.*

*Tik.*

*Tik.*

I didn't let him look back. I leaned against him every step, pushing, herding, and he let himself be herded. The hoofsteps came behind us all the way up the lane. They didn't hurry. They didn't need to. They came exactly as fast as we went, never closer, never farther, the way a mother walks behind a child who is going home anyway.

Umm Hanan's door was nearest. Theyab didn't knock. He kicked it, and it opened, and he went in with Shibl and I went in behind him and turned in the doorway.

The lane was empty.

White. Hot. Nobody.

But in the dust, right up to the threshold, between our own prints, there were small round cups, two by two. And the last two stood side by side at the very edge of Umm Hanan's step, facing in. Close together. As if something had stood there a while, and looked at the door, and not been able to come any farther.

---

Nobody believed him.

You knew that. I knew it as we came through the door. Umm Hanan woke and screamed. Saqi came, roused by the screaming, with his stick in his hand and his beard full of sleep, and found his younger son sunburned and blinking in the widow's house in the arms of the foundling. At noon. With one sandal missing.

Theyab told him. Every word. The voice. The lane. The tall shape in the shimmer, the legs that bent the wrong way. He told it plainly, with his hand flat on his chest the whole time because the feather was quiet and he wanted Saqi to see that it was quiet.

Saqi didn't know about the feather. Nobody did.

"You took him out," Saqi said.

"No."

"You took him out at noon, to the sand, to do whatever it is your kind does—"

"He was walking to her. I carried him back."

"*Her*." Saqi's face went dark red under the sunburn of a lifetime. "There's no *her*. There's the Noon Mare in old women's stories and there's you, and I know which one I've seen walking my lanes at noon for eight years."

And then he looked down at his son. "Shibl. Tell me. Did he take you out?"

Shibl was sitting on Umm Hanan's mat with a wet cloth on his head and a cup of water in both hands. He looked at his father. He looked at Theyab. His big dark eyes were very far away.

"Mama called me," he said.

The room went very quiet.

Saqi's face did something I've seen only a few faces do. It went white under the red, from the forehead down, like water draining out of a skin. His wife had been dead seven years. He'd never let her name be said in his house.

Then he hit Theyab. Open-handed, across the face, so hard that Theyab's head snapped round and he went down on one knee on Umm Hanan's floor.

"You put that in his head," Saqi said. His voice was shaking. "You. Whatever you are. Stay away from my sons."

He picked Shibl up and carried him out into the noon, his stick in one hand and his son over his shoulder, and down the lane toward his own house. Shibl looked back over his father's shoulder all the way. At Theyab. At me. He didn't wave. He just looked.

And Umm Hanan stood in the middle of her own floor, her hands knotted in her abaya, and looked at Theyab on his knee, and at the door, and at the small round prints in the dust of her threshold. She looked for a long time.

She didn't say anything. She didn't help him up.

But I saw her go to the door when he'd gone, and look out at the empty lane, and stand there. And I saw her lips move. Calling, very quietly, under her breath, into the white noon.

Not his name. She didn't know his name.

But she was calling.

---

That evening, at dusk, when the lanes were full again and the children were out and the heat had gone, Theyab went back down the long white lane alone. To the end of it, where it opened onto the sand.

I went with him. I didn't want to. I went.

Shibl's other sandal was lying in the lane.

On its side. Small. A little child's sandal of plaited palm, worn through at the heel. Lying exactly at the place where the shimmer had been thickest, where the tall shape had stood.

Round it, in a slow circle, small round cups. Two by two.

Theyab crouched and picked it up.

He held it a long moment in his right hand. Then he looked at me, and his face in the dusk was the colour of the grey on the horizon.

"It's warm," he said.

The sun had been down an hour.

It was warm as bread just off the plate.

---

*End of Chapter Three*
