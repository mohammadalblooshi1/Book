# Chapter Six
## Never Answer When the Grey Calls

> *"If the dark calls you by name, it learned it from someone you trusted."*
> — a grandmother's warning, said with the lamp already lit

---

Listen to me, little one. A voice is the last thing to leave.

When a man dies, the body goes into the sand before the next sunset, because the heat will not wait. His camels are shared out. His sandals stand by the door until somebody's son grows into them. His face fades first; ask any widow.

But the voice stays. You will forget the colour of a man's eyes long before you forget the way he said your name when he came in from the camels, and if you heard it again in the dark after ten years, you would not stop to think. You would answer. Everybody does.

That is the oldest trap there is, and it was old when I was young.

The night after the first emptied stood smiling at the well, the Grey learned to talk.

---

It began a little after the last light had gone.

Sheikh Mazyad had sent the boys round the lanes at sunset with his orders: lamps out, doors barred, children inside. The man at the well and the woman who had started saying his words were gone. Where they had stood there were two sets of footprints facing the bucket, and nothing walking away.

So the village shut itself up like a fist and lay in the dark and listened.

Theyab's tent stood where it always stood, in its hollow past the last house, as far from everyone as the village could put him and still call him theirs. He sat in the mouth of it with his rag-hilted khanjar across his knees and his left fist closed, as it was always closed, and looked east. I lay across his feet. I do that when I don't want him to stand up. It has never once worked.

The first voice came from the far end of the village, from the lanes the colour had left.

It was a child's voice, a little girl's. "*Yumma? Yumma, I'm cold. Let me in.*"

Not loud. That was the cleverness of it. A shout you can refuse. This was the voice a child uses at a door when she knows she has done wrong and hopes to be let in anyway. Small, and close to the wood.

Somewhere in the dark a woman made a sound. Just one, quickly stopped, like a hand put over a mouth.

"*Yumma. It's me.*"

And then another voice, further along. An old man, coughing, the long rattling cough of someone with sand in his chest. "*Who's barred the door? Who's barred the door on me? Open it, I'm tired.*"

And then another. And another. A young man laughing at a joke nobody else had heard. A woman singing three notes of a grinding song and stopping. The coffee-seller the summer killed nine years ago, calling his wares with the little upward break in the middle of the cry that every child in Hilat al-Sarab had imitated behind his back.

Theyab's hand had found my neck. His fingers were very cold.

"Those are dead people," he said.

I did not answer him. I cannot answer him; you know that. But my hackles stayed flat, because it was true, and he felt them stay flat under his hand, and that was answer enough.

Dead people's voices, and the voices of people only far away: a son gone pearling, a daughter married out to the Bani Yas. All coming out of the Grey together, calling at barred doors.

I knew what it was. I want you to know that I knew, and I did not like knowing.

The thing that was speaking had no voice of its own. It is one of the five that serve the Sealed King under Ubar, and I name it only because you need to know what you are listening for. It is called the Thousand-Voiced. It keeps every last word it has ever swallowed, the way a magpie keeps bright things, and uses them the way a fisherman uses a bright thing. It does not want to talk to you. It wants you to open the door.

And it was learning. I could hear it learning. Every time a voice called and nobody answered, the next voice was a little better. The child's voice came back, and this time she was crying. The old man came back, and this time he called a name.

---

Umm Hanan's house was the last on the lane behind the well, and her lamp was the only light in Hilat al-Sarab.

She had never put it out, not in all the years I had known her, not on the night the boats came home without her men. It stood in the niche inside her door, a little clay lamp with a wick of twisted cotton, and it burned the way it always burned: steady, upright, unbothered, a small yellow tongue of flame that the draught under the door never seemed to find.

Other houses had tried to keep a lamp that night. Every one had guttered and gone blue at the root, though there was no wind.

Hers did not even flicker.

There is something in that house, little one. It is old and quiet and does not like being looked at, so I did not look at it, but I could feel it at the threshold the way you feel a big dog lying in a dark doorway. Something from under the ground had once been treated kindly there, and had never forgotten.

Theyab went to her house. Of course he did.

He did not decide to. He was simply standing, and then he was walking down through the palms toward the one light, with the khanjar in his hand, and I was walking with him with my ears flat to my skull.

We were twenty paces from her door when the voices of her sons began.

---

They came up the lane from the east, from the Grey, and they were wet.

That was the first thing. They were young men's voices, a little rough, the voices of men who had shouted across water for a living, and there was water in them. A bubble. A drowned catch at the back of every word, as if each sentence had to be pushed out past something heavy and cold in the throat.

"*Yumma. The boat's in. Open the door.*"

Then the other one, the younger one, laughing the way he must have laughed: "*Yumma, we're starving, what did you bake? Yumma. Yumma, it's us.*"

They had drowned off the pearl banks years before, before ever the boy came up out of the noon, the two sons and their father on the same night. The other divers said a voice had called them over the side, using their names. A thing the divers talk about only when they are very drunk or very old, a thing in no book, that they call the Deep Caller.

I think the things from below talk to each other, little one, the way traders talk at a well. I think the Deep Caller kept those voices in the cold under the sea, the last bubbles of three drowning men, and that night it lent them inland. As a favour. As a joke. I do not know what passes for a joke down there.

The door opened.

Umm Hanan stood in it, her grey hair uncovered and loose, barefoot, the lamp behind her making her a black shape with a yellow edge. Her face was perfectly calm, the way faces go when the thing you have prayed for every night for years finally happens and there is no room left in you for any feeling at all.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm here, my eyes. I'm coming."

"*Yumma,*" said the younger son's voice, much closer now, from the dark mouth of the lane where the colour stopped. "*Yumma, I'm so cold. I've been so cold.*"

She stepped off her threshold.

Theyab dropped the khanjar and caught her.

He took her round the waist from behind with both arms, the way you hold a goat that wants to bolt, and dug his heels into the sand. She was small and he was fifteen and thin as a tent pole, and she very nearly dragged him. I had never seen grief move like that, the whole weight of a body thrown toward a sound.

"Let me go," she said. Quite reasonably. As if he had simply misunderstood. "Laqeet. Let me go, it's my son."

"It isn't," said Theyab into her shoulder. "It isn't. It isn't, Umm Hanan, it isn't."

He could not lie. You know that. He has never in his life been able to say a thing he did not believe. So when he said it, he said it with the whole of himself, and I think she heard that, somewhere under the noise.

"*Yumma,*" said the voice. And then, very gently, in the same drowned young man's voice: "*Laqeet. Let my mother go.*"

That was when she hit him.

She twisted round in his arms and struck him across the face with her open hand, hard enough that his head snapped sideways and his teeth clicked. The sound of it went off down the lane like a stick breaking. I heard a shutter move somewhere, someone watching.

He did not let go.

She stood there breathing. She looked at his face. There was a mark coming up on it already, the four fingers of a woman who has kneaded dough every morning of her life. She looked at it for a long time.

Then she lifted the same hand and laid it flat against his cheek, over the mark, the way you lay a hand on a child's forehead to see if he is hot. And she left it there.

Nobody had ever touched his face like that before. I want you to understand that. Fists, yes, and once the corner of her sleeve wiping the clay away. But not a hand laid on it to keep it. Not in eight years. Not once. I had seen him flinch from a hand raised to shade an old man's eyes.

He did not flinch.

Behind her, inside the door, the lamp flared. It did not flicker. It *stood up*, little one: the flame went tall and white and still, as tall as a man's hand, and threw her shadow and his out across the lane and all the way to the mouth of the Grey. Where the light touched the Grey, it stopped, the way light always stops there. But the voice stopped too.

For one breath there was nothing. No voice. No sound from any lane in the village. Only Umm Hanan's breathing and Theyab's breathing and the soft roar of a lamp burning far too high.

Then, from much further off, the voice of her younger son said, "*Yumma?*" in a small, puzzled way, like a boy who has walked into the wrong house.

She shut her eyes.

"What was his name," she said. Not to Theyab. Not to anyone. "What was his name. My little one. I had it in the bread. I had it every morning of my life."

I tried to tell her.

I never knew her sons. They were under the water before we came up out of the noon. But I had heard her say that name every morning for eight years, through her wall, slapped into the bread like salt. I had it. It was right there.

I opened my mouth in the dark, and what I had was this:

——

Nothing. A smooth place. A gap with the shape of a word around it, the way a tooth leaves its shape in the gum.

I have forgotten a great many things in my long life, on purpose and otherwise. That was the first time something reached into me and took a name out like a date out of a bowl, and I felt the fingers do it.

Umm Hanan took her hand from Theyab's cheek. She picked his khanjar up out of the sand and put it into his palm and closed his fingers round it, quite firmly. Then she went inside and shut the door. The lamp settled back down to its ordinary steady tongue.

Through the wood we heard her begin to knead. At night. In the dark. Slap, and turn, and slap. And two names, said over the dough. And then a silence where the third should have been, and then the two names again, from the beginning, as if by starting over she might take it by surprise.

---

The Grey did not get Umm Hanan that night. It got the goat-herd.

He kept the sheikh's goats on the scrub to the south, a quiet man with a split lip from a camel's kick that made him lisp. Two winters before, his wife had died bringing a child into the world that did not stay in it. You would see him at dusk outside his hut with her old grinding stone between his knees, turning it with one hand.

I won't give you his name, little one. He has no use for it any more, and neither should you.

His hut was on the southern edge, nearer the Grey than it had been that morning. We were coming back from Umm Hanan's when we heard a woman's voice, low and amused, saying his name, the way a wife says it through a tent wall when she knows perfectly well he's awake.

Then, warmer: "*Are you going to leave me standing out here?*"

The door of his hut opened before we could reach it.

He came out like a sleepwalker, upright and careful, his hands a little ahead of him. There was only starlight, and it stopped at the edge of the Grey like water at a dam, and there, just inside the edge, something stood.

I saw it. With the silver eye I saw it.

I wish I had not.

It was the shape of a woman, and it was the shape of nothing. It was a place where the dark was folded, the way cloth is folded, so that the creases made a waist and shoulders and the tilt of a head. Out of the fold came her voice. It was very good. It had her lisp, which she had taken from him over the years the way married people take things. It had her laugh.

"*There you are,*" it said.

"Is that you?" said the goat-herd. He had stopped on the edge, one foot in the starlight and one foot on the flat dead sand. "Is that you? Is it really you?"

Theyab shouted at him. He shouted the man's name, and told him to come back, and told him it was not her, and the shout went out of his mouth and across twenty paces of open ground and into the Grey, and stopped, as sound stops in there, a hand's breadth from the goat-herd's ear.

I ran.

I ran as fast as I have ever run, low and long, the way a saluki runs down a hare, and I would have had his robe in my teeth. I was ten strides off.

He said, "I'm here."

And he stepped in.

I stopped at the edge. I am ashamed of that. I tell myself I stopped because Theyab was behind me, and because a man who has already answered leaves nothing to bring out. But I also stopped because the fold in the dark had turned its creases toward me, and for a moment it had looked pleased to see me.

The goat-herd walked away from us into the Grey with his hands held out in front of him, saying, "Is that you? Is that you?" and the shape went ahead of him, a little out of reach, the way a mother walks backwards in front of a child learning to walk.

We waited at the edge for an hour. Theyab crouched and stared into the flat grey nothing, and called the man's name, and it died in the air.

Then the goat-herd came back.

He walked out of the Grey on his own, just where he had walked in. His face had gone the colour of cold ash. His eyes were white as the inside of a shell. His split lip was turned up at both ends, too far and too slowly, the way the emptied smile.

"*Is that you?*" he said, to the air in front of him. "*Is it really you? Is that you?*"

He walked past us. He did not see us. He went to the door of his own hut and stood in front of it, with his palm flat on the wood, as if listening for someone inside.

Theyab made a sound I had never heard him make. Then he put his fist in his mouth and bit it.

---

It was not the goat-herd coming back that broke the village that night. It was what happened half an hour later.

The voices started again. All of them. The coughing old man and the coffee-seller, the singing woman, the little girl who was cold. Umm Hanan's sons. The goat-herd's wife, laughing.

And then a new one.

"*Brother? Brother, are you awake? Come out a moment, I have something to show you.*"

It was the goat-herd's voice. Lisp and all. Calling at the door of his brother's house on the north side, in exactly the tone a younger brother uses when he has found a fox's earth and wants company to dig it out.

He had been taken an hour ago. He was standing at his own door, white-eyed, saying *Is that you*. And the Grey already had his voice, and was already using it on the people who loved him.

That is the thing I have never made anyone understand. The Grey is not a sickness. It is a crowd. It is made of us. Every voice it swallows becomes bait, until the last living man in the last house lies in the dark listening to his whole village calling his name at the door in perfect, loving harmony.

The goat-herd's brother did not open his door. But I heard him, through the wood, answer. "I'm here," he said, before he could stop himself. Just that. "I'm here."

And the voice said, very happily, "*There you are.*"

---

I knew then that the village would not last the night like this.

Doors were moving all over Hilat al-Sarab. Not opening, not yet. Bars were being lifted and set down again. Behind every one was somebody whose dead were calling. A child on the north side had begun to answer the little girl's voice, "*I'm cold too*," and her mother was hissing at her, and the child was crying, and the crying was being learned.

And the Grey was moving. I could see it in the starlight. The edge of it was no longer a line. It had fingers. They went down the lanes toward the sound of every answer, slow and flat, like spilt oil finding the low places in a floor.

I thought: *it is listening for the answers. It goes where it's answered.*

I thought: *then let it listen to something else.*

I did not think it through. I am a saluki. When something is running the wrong way, I go and put myself in front of it.

I left Theyab at the edge of the goat-herd's yard. I did not look back at him, because if I had looked back I would not have gone. I trotted out into the middle of the empty lane where the starlight lay flat on the sand, and I lifted my head and barked, once, the short hard bark a saluki gives at the start of a hunt to tell the falcon he's seen the hare.

Then I walked into the Grey on purpose.

---

It closed over me cold and smooth, the way it always does, like being licked by something without a tongue.

Inside, there is no sound. But for something like me there is a different kind of sound, under the place where sound should be. I heard the voices the way you hear your own heart with your fingers in your ears. More than a thousand, the whole of what the thing had ever swallowed, a river of last words, and every word of it heading for the doors of Hilat al-Sarab.

I walked into the middle of the river and stood there.

And it turned.

I felt it turn the way a crowd in a market turns when somebody important comes through the gate. The voices came round me like water round a stone, and began to say what they said into my ears instead of into the doors. *Yumma, I'm cold. Who's barred the door. Is that you. Brother, are you awake.* Close, close, all of them, saying their sentences to an old dog standing alone in the dark.

And something under all of them said: *Ah. You.*

Not in words. In the silver eye, the one that is not amber. It began to ache, the deep tooth-ache it gives me when Theyab is in danger, and then it began to see. The Grey was not flat. It went down. The sand under my feet was a lid, and under the lid there was heat with no light, and rows of shapes walking without going anywhere, and at the very bottom something enormous lay curled around a smell of old frankincense, and turned its attention very slightly toward me, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound in the next room and does not wake.

*You,* said the Grey, in all its voices. *You've been away. Come in. Come further in.*

It knew me. I don't know how. I told myself then that it knew me the way a hound knows a hare: as something it has chased before. I told myself that for a long time.

I walked.

I walked east, slow, the way a mother bird drags a wing away from her nest. The voices came with me. I felt the fingers of the Grey draw back down the lanes after me like a cloth pulled off a table, and out past the dead palms the whole river of voices walked with me, delighted, as if I were taking it somewhere wonderful.

I walked until I could not hear the village at all. Then I turned round, very calmly, and walked back out the way I had come.

It let me go.

That frightened me more than anything else that night. It did not cling. It parted for me at the edge the way a tent-flap parts for the man of the house, and the last thing I heard was the coughing old man's voice, very fondly: *Don't be long.*

---

They were on the roofs.

The whole village. They had come up through the roof-hatches while the voices were quiet, to see why, and they had seen. Fifty people, black against the stars. They had watched the old sand-coloured dog walk into the Grey and the Grey turn after him like a flock after a bell-goat, and they had watched him walk back out, unharmed, the same colour he went in.

I sat down in the middle of the lane, shaking. I scratched behind my ear, because it is very difficult for people to be afraid of a dog who is scratching behind his ear.

It is not, it turns out, impossible.

"It went in," said Ghanim from his roof, very loud, the way he says the weight of a thing when he knows it's wrong. "You all saw. It went in and it came out."

Nobody answered him.

"It called them," said Ghanim. "They know it. It's one of them. Haven't I said so? Since the Laqeet came, that dog's been—"

"Be quiet," said Sheikh Mazyad from his own roof. But he said it without force. He was looking at me.

They were all looking at me.

I watched fifty people who had just been saved decide, one by one, that they would rather not have been saved by me.

Theyab came and stood beside me. He put his hand on my neck. His face still had Umm Hanan's handprint on it.

He looked up at them all. He did not say anything. He has never known what to say to them.

One by one, they went back down through their hatches and barred them from inside. And for the rest of that night, though the voices did not come back, I heard them through the walls. Not the Grey. The village. Whispering.

*The dog went in.*

*The dog brought it.*

*The dog came with the boy.*

---

Before the sun came up, I went round the lanes alone and counted.

I counted the emptied in Hilat al-Sarab the way a herder counts his flock at dawn, and I learned what each one was saying, because someone should. I will give them to you as I heard them, without their names. Names are not safe this year.

*Bring the bucket, the bucket.* The man who had been first, at the well. He was standing in the date grove now, facing a palm, with his hands cupped as if something were about to be poured into them.

*Bring the bucket, the bucket.* The woman who had stood beside him. It was not her sentence. It was his. She had caught it from him the way you catch a yawn, and she had nothing of her own left to say.

*Who left the goat pen open?* A boy of twelve or so, the son of a family on the north side, standing in an empty pen with the gate wide.

*Two more days and they'll be sweet.* A man under a palm with his face turned up toward the dates.

*Mind the fire, it's spitting.* An old woman, squatting by a hearth that had been cold since the afternoon.

*Is that you? Is it really you?* The goat-herd, at his own door.

*There you are.* His brother. He had opened his door at last before dawn and gone out to see. He was standing behind the goat-herd now, both of them facing the wood.

*I only went to look.* A girl of fourteen, promised to a cousin in Liwa. The henna on her hands had gone grey.

*Hush now. Hush.* A young mother, rocking nothing.

*I'm cold too.* The child who had answered the little girl's voice. Her mother had held her and held her, and it had not been enough, because once you have answered you are already halfway out.

*I'm here.* Her mother. Sitting beside her.

Eleven. In one night.

Dogs don't write. But I have kept them all these years, and I say them over in the order I found them when I cannot sleep, so that at least one creature in the world remembers they were said.

---

In the morning, nobody threw stones at me. I'll give them that. They simply stepped round me in the lanes the way you step round a snake you are not sure is dead.

Ghanim said the dog should be sold to the coast. Somebody said a dog that walks into the Grey and out again is no dog, and should be burned with salt. They argued all morning at the sheikh's door. They never settled it. They never have; you'll see.

Theyab sat outside his tent all day and did not eat. Once I caught him whispering into my ear, very low: "What are you? What are you, then?"

I put my chin on his knee. It is the only answer I have ever had for him.

And all day, the Grey was quiet. It did not need to call. It had eleven new voices to learn, and a twelfth that it had liked very much.

---

That night I could not sleep. My silver eye ached and would not stop. So I went and lay in the shadow of the wall by the well, where I could see the eastern lanes and the Grey beyond them, and I watched.

The well at Hilat al-Sarab is the oldest thing in the village, older than the palms. It goes down through sand and then through stone, a long way, and the women who draw it have a saying: *Name the Maker before you pour.* They say it so often they no longer hear themselves, the way you no longer hear your own feet.

A little before midnight, Saqi the well-keeper came out of his house.

He was a hard man, Saqi. A widower with a short temper and a broad leather belt that both his sons knew too well. Every drop that came up in Hilat al-Sarab came up on his rope, and he let you know it.

He did not look hard that night. He looked like a man who had lain awake listening to his dead wife's voice at the wrong side of his door, and had not answered, and was sick with it.

He went to the well. He took up the bucket. He shook out the rope.

And he lowered it.

He did not name the Maker. I have heard Saqi say it ten thousand times in a flat bored voice, the way a man greets a neighbour he dislikes. That night he did not. He let the rope run hand over hand into the dark, and as he did it, very softly, he began to hum.

It was a grinding song. A woman's song. Three notes, and a little turn, and three notes again.

I knew it. I had heard it in the dark only the night before, coming out of the Grey.

The rope went down and down. The bucket touched the water. I heard it touch: a small, far, hollow sound, like a cup set down in an empty room.

Saqi stopped humming.

And from the bottom of the well, from the water, up the long stone throat of the oldest thing in Hilat al-Sarab, something hummed the song back to him.

Three notes, and a little turn.

And three notes again.

---

*End of Chapter Six*
