# Chapter Ten
## Never Name What You Haven't Met

> *"Four winds, four waters, four ranks of the hidden, and none of them your friend."*
> — Mutawwa' Hilal

---

He read over the well in the morning, before the heat, and the women came to watch.

They came the way women come to a birth, quietly, in twos and threes, with their water-jars empty on their hips. Nobody had drawn from the well for two days. The jars in every house were nearly dry, and the trough where the goats drank was cracked and white at the bottom like a dry wadi.

Hilal sat on the lip of the well with his legs over the dark.

I did not like that. I lay at the edge of the court with my chin on my paws and watched him sit there, an old man as thin as a stick, with his heels hanging over the place where, two nights before, a thing had climbed up the shaft like a lizard with a child under its arm. He did not seem to mind. He set his clay bowl on the stone beside him, and filled it from a skin, and dropped in his pinch of salt. Then he leaned over the shaft, the way a man leans over a well to call down to someone working at the bottom, and began to read into it.

His voice went down the shaft and came back up again changed, round and hollow, as if the well were reading with him.

I felt it go down. I cannot tell you how. I felt the water of his voice running down the long stone throat into the dark, and the dark at the bottom draw back from it. Not far. A little. The way a dog draws back its lip, unsure whether to bite.

He read for a long time. When he had finished, he took the bowl in both hands and named the Maker over it, the full way, slowly, so that every woman in the court could hear each word. And he poured it into the well.

We heard it fall. A long time falling. Then the small far sound of water meeting water.

And up the shaft, after it, came a breath of air.

It was cool. That was all. Cool, and wet, and very faintly sweet, like the inside of a water-jar that has held good water a long time. It came up over the lip and spread across the court like the first breath of evening after a summer day, and every woman there felt it on her face and went still.

I knew that smell. I had smelled it every day for eight years and missed it for three.

Not all of it. Not the whole of the old quiet thing that had kept the well. That was gone, or gone wrong, and I did not know then whether it would ever come back. But something of it. A thread. Enough.

"Draw," said Hilal. "Name the Maker first. Every bucket. Every one. And the first splash of every morning goes back down the well, as your grandmothers did. Don't forget it again."

---

It was the colour that made them weep.

The first woman to step forward was a grandmother from the north side with a face like a walnut. She lowered the bucket, and named the Maker, and drew it up full, and poured the first splash back over the lip. Then she filled her jar. And as the water went into the jar, I saw her look down at the stones under her feet.

They were not grey.

Little one, I had not noticed. None of us had. The Grey had never come so far into the village as the well-court, and yet the well-court had been grey for days. Not dead grey, not the flat ash-colour of the eastern lanes, but dimmed, faded, the way a cloth fades after a summer on a line. The stones round the well. The trough. The mud walls of Saqi's house. Ever since the night Saqi lowered the bucket without the words, the colour had been seeping out of that court so slowly that nobody had seen it go.

Now it came back.

It came back from the well outward, the way a ripple goes out from a dropped stone. The well-stones went the colour of honey. The trough went blue, the old faded indigo somebody had painted it a generation ago, and the blue was so bright it hurt my eyes. The mud of the walls went warm and red-brown. A heap of green fodder by Saqi's door turned green.

The grandmother with the walnut face sat down on the lip of the well with her jar in her lap and wept.

Then they were all weeping, quietly, with their hands over their mouths. One of them kissed Hilal's sleeve, and he let her, though I could see he did not like it.

It lasted a day.

I want to tell you it lasted for ever. It did not. By the next morning the colour had begun to fade again, very slowly, from the edges in. The Grey was too close and too strong, and one old man's reading over one well could not hold it back for long. But for one whole day, in the middle of a village that was dying of grey, there was a court of honey and indigo and red-brown, and water in the jars, and children drinking.

The women never forgot it. Years later, long after everything else, I heard them still telling each other about the day the well-court came back. It is the kind of thing that is remembered, little one. A day of colour, given for nothing.

---

He taught under the ghaf tree.

There was one at the southern edge of the village, an old one, standing alone on a low rise of sand where the gardens gave out, with its crooked grey arms held out over a round of shade. The herders tied their camels to it. The children were forbidden to climb it, and climbed it. Hilal sat down in its shade on the first afternoon with his back against the trunk and a bundle in his lap, and unwrapped the bundle, and inside it was a book.

It was the first book Theyab had ever seen.

It was old and fat and battered, with a leather cover gone soft and black with handling, and the pages had come loose from the spine and been sewn back in with goat-hair thread. Some of the pages were brown at the edges, as if they had been near a fire. Hilal opened it on his knees the way you open a box of something precious, slowly, with the flat of his hand.

"Al-Hayawan," he said. "*The Book of Animals*. Written in Basra a very long time ago, by a very clever man with very bad teeth, who wanted to know everything about everything that lives. Including the things that live where you can't see them." He turned the pages. "He was wrong about a great deal. He was wrong about the elephant. But he was right about more than you'd think."

Theyab sat on the sand at the edge of the shade.

Nobody else came, at first. The village watched from the gardens and the walls, the way they had watched the reading in the yard, at a safe distance. They did not want to sit under a tree with the Laqeet. But a book is a strange thing in a place where nobody reads. By the second hour three children had crept close enough to hear, lying on their stomachs in the hot sand just outside the shade, pretending they had come to look at a beetle. One of them was Shibl, with Hilal's blue bead round his neck on its twice-knotted thread.

And at the far edge of the gardens, leaning on a wall with his back half turned, pretending to mend a rope, was Nadim.

---

"There are four kinds," said Hilal.

He held up four fingers. He did not read it from the book. I think he had it by heart, the way you have the names of your own family by heart.

"The Smokeless are made from fire without smoke, the old book says. That's what they are, all of them, down at the root. But fire goes into the world in four ways, and so do they.

"The first are the Burners. They are of the fire itself. They live in heat: in the noon, in the hearth, in the sand at the top of the day when you can't put your foot down on it. They are the strongest, and they are proud. They don't like water that has been read over. They hate it. If you have to fight one, and pray you never do, throw read-over water and run.

"The second are the Whirlers. They are of the wind. You've seen them. On a hot afternoon, out on the gravel plain, a little pillar of dust gets up and spins and walks across the sand on its own, and the camels shy from it. The herders say *iron, iron* at it, and spit. They're right to. The Whirlers carry things: sand, and voices, and sometimes children. They can't hold a shape out of the wind. When the air goes still, they come apart. The great one of them, the old men say, is the Black Wind." He did not say any more about that one. I noticed he did not.

"The third are the Drowners. They are of the sea, and the salt, and the deep water. They can't go far from wet. They stay by the coasts and the sabkha and the bottoms of old wells.

"And the fourth are the Hoofed. They are of the land. The empty sand is theirs." He turned a page. "The Hoofed can change their shape into anything. A traveller, a beggar, a camel, a beautiful woman, your own grandmother. Anything at all. Except the feet." He looked up. "They can't change the feet. Whatever face they wear, underneath it they stand on the hooves of a donkey. So when a stranger comes to your fire in the empty sand at night, look at his feet before you look at his face."

On their stomachs in the sand, the three children looked at each other.

Theyab had gone still. I knew what he was thinking. Round cups in the sand, two by two, circling his blanket in the noon. Small neat prints in the dust of the lane.

---

"Then there are the ranks," said Hilal.

He held up four fingers again.

"The old book has them in four, like steps, from the lowest to the highest. The lowest are the Dwellers. They live alongside people. In houses, in wells, in old trees, in the corners of rooms. Most of them want nothing but to be let alone and treated decently. Name the Maker before you pour hot water on the ground, so you don't scald one. Name him before you go into a dark room, so you don't tread on one. Leave them their corners. Many of them will keep your house for you, if you're kind. There was one in your well." He did not look at the well. "It was a good one, I think. For a long time."

Theyab said, "What happened to it?"

"It Turned," said Hilal. "That's the second rank. Not a rank, really. A road. Any of them can go down it. A Dweller, a Burner, anything. The Turned are the ones that have gone wicked. Sometimes by their own choice. Sometimes because something leaned on them, night after night, until they broke." He closed his hand, slowly, as if crushing something. "The Grey leans. It leans very hard."

"The third are the Breakers. They are the strong ones. The builders. The old people say that when you see stones in the desert too big for men to have lifted, the Breakers lifted them. They carry walls. They can carry whole buildings. They can bring them down." He shrugged. "I've never met one. I don't care to.

"And the fourth, the highest, are the Burners again."

Theyab frowned. "You said Burners were a kind."

"They are. The old book uses the same word twice." Hilal's mouth twitched in his beard. "The man who wrote it had never been burned by either, or he'd have taken more care. The strongest of every kind come to burn, in the end. That's the top of the steps. A Burner of the wind, a Burner of the sea. You don't want to meet any of them." He tapped the page. "Each rank comes in every kind. Remember that. The four kinds are what they're made of. The four ranks are what they've become. A Dweller can be of the sea. A Turned thing can be of the land. Four times four. Sixteen roads to the same grave."

On her stomach in the sand, one of the little girls began to cry quietly. Shibl reached over and patted her back without looking at her, his eyes fixed on the book.

"None of them is your friend," said Hilal. "Not even the good ones. A good one will help you for its own reasons, and keep you for its own reasons, and you'll never know what the reasons are. Be grateful. Be polite. Leave the first splash. But never think one of them is yours."

My hackles did not rise. He believed it.

I lay in the shade with my chin on my paws and thought of a well of sweet water, and ghaf roots like the pillars of a fort, and a gazelle with gold eyes standing in a ruin wall at noon, and I kept my face very still.

---

On the second day he talked about the Worn.

He had come from Saqi's house. He had set the arm himself the evening before, with Nadim holding his father's shoulders down and his face turned away, and Saqi screaming. He said the bones would knit crooked but they would knit. He said the ribs would mend. He did not say anything about the rest.

"Was he still in there?" said Theyab. "When it was— When he was combing Shibl's hair. When he picked him up. Was Saqi still in there?"

Hilal was a long time answering.

"He's always still in there," he said at last. "That's the cruelty of it. The thing that puts you on doesn't push you out. It hasn't room to. It simply sits down in front of you, the way a big man sits down in front of you at a wedding so you can't see. And you watch over its shoulder. You watch your hands do what it wants. You hear your mouth say what it wants. You can't stop any of it. But you're there. You see all of it." He looked at the back of his own hand. "When they come back, they remember everything. You saw that."

"Then he knew," said Theyab. "When I— When the light—"

"He knew."

Theyab said nothing more. He looked at the inside of his own arm, where the cracks were. They had faded nearly to nothing by then. In the shade you could not see them at all.

"There's another one," said Hilal. "Not a Worn. Not a thing that comes from outside. Something you were born with." He turned the pages of the book to a place near the back, where the writing was smaller and crowded together, and a page had been torn out and stuck back in with a smear of gum. "The old people say every one of us is born with a twin. Not a brother. One of them. A Smokeless child born in the same breath as you, down below, and tied to you, the way a shadow is tied to your heels. It knows your name, because it was there when you got it. It knows your voice, because it learned to talk when you did." He closed the book. "Most of them sleep. All your life, they sleep, and you never hear from them. Pray yours does."

"And if it doesn't?" said Theyab.

His voice was very quiet. I lifted my head.

Hilal looked at him.

"Then one day you'll hear it," he said. "And it'll talk to you in your own voice. And you'll think it's you."

Theyab did not say anything.

He was thinking of the dune, I knew. The hiss of sand on the night Haddaj's men came, and his name in it, one step behind him, in his own voice. He was thinking of the bottom of the well, and the white eyes, and his own secret name coming back up at him out of Saqi's mouth: *Theyab. Let go. There you are.*

He could not lie. But he could keep his mouth shut, and he did. He sat in the shade of the ghaf with his left fist closed on his knee and his right hand flat on the hirz, and said nothing at all.

Hilal looked at him a while longer. Then he looked at me.

I put my chin back on my paws.

---

He showed them the ways of keeping safe. Not in the book. In his hands.

He took Theyab's rusty khanjar out of his sash and turned it over and sniffed it, which made the children giggle. "Iron," he said. "Rust doesn't matter. It's still iron. Sleep with it under your head. Every night. Under the head, not beside it, not in your belt. Iron under the head and they can't sit on you in the night." He gave it back. "That's for all of you," he said to the children, who were not lying on their stomachs any more but sitting up cross-legged in the edge of the shade like a little majlis. "Ask your fathers for a nail, if there's no knife. A nail will do."

He showed them salt. He took a handful from his twist of cloth and walked with them to the end of the nearest eastern lane, where the Grey began, and poured it in a thin white line across the threshold of the last house before the dead ones. "Across the doorway. Every doorway that faces the bad side. It won't stop the big ones. It'll stop the small ones, and it'll make the big ones think." The line glittered in the sun.

I walked up to look at it. I don't know why. It was a line of salt. I have walked over a thousand lines of salt in my life.

I stopped in front of it.

Nothing happened. Nothing at all. It was a line of salt on a doorstep, and I am an old dog, and after a moment I stepped over it, and back, and sat down and scratched my ear, because it is mostly for show, salt, for something like me.

When I looked up, Hilal was watching me again. He did not say anything. He never did. He only watched, with his small bright reading eyes, until I looked away.

He showed them water. He took his clay bowl and filled it and read over it, low, for a long breath, and then took it to Saqi's house and made Saqi drink it lying down, with Nadim holding up his father's head. Saqi drank it all. Then he turned his face to the wall.

He showed them the words. Before you pour. Before you go into a dark room. Before you put out the lamp. He made the children say them back to him until they had them, and then made them say them again, faster, laughing, until it was a game.

And the bead. He touched the blue bead on Shibl's chest with one finger. "That catches the eye," he said. "Anything that looks at you wanting you, it looks at the bead first, and the bead takes the look. If a bead breaks, it took a hard one. Tell your brother."

"I'll tell him," said Shibl, and looked across the gardens at Nadim, who pretended not to hear.

And the hirz. He did not show them that. He only looked at the one round Theyab's neck, and nodded to himself, and moved on.

---

At dusk on the second day we walked back through the village behind him, and passed Umm Hanan's door.

It stood open. The lamp was burning in its niche, steady, upright, a small yellow tongue of flame that no draught ever found. Umm Hanan was inside, kneading. We could hear it. Slap, and turn. Two names said over the dough, and the gap, and two names again.

Hilal stopped.

He stood in the lane outside her door for a long time, leaning on his stick, looking at the lamp. Not at her. At the lamp, and at the dark of the doorway round it, the threshold, the place where I had felt something old lying across the way like a big dog in the dark.

"Hm," he said quietly. "Somebody in that house was kind to somebody, once."

"Umm Hanan," said Theyab. "She leaves bread on the wall for me."

"Does she." Hilal looked at the lamp a moment longer. "Then tell her this from me. Tell her never to stop naming the Maker before she pours. Not once. Not on the worst day of her life. There's a wall round that house, and she builds it every morning with her own mouth." He went on down the lane. "Most people don't know they're building anything."

---

It was on the third morning that Theyab asked about the sea.

We were under the ghaf again. The children had come early, and brought their sisters, and two of the smaller boys had brought their grandmother, who sat at the back with her face covered and pretended to be there only to keep an eye on them. Nadim was not at the wall. He had stopped pretending and sat on the edge of the shade with his back against a root, and his arms folded, and his face shut like a door. But he sat.

Hilal had been talking about the Drowners. About the bottoms of old wells, and the salt, and the things that live where the sea comes into the sabkha. And Theyab said, half joking, the way he never quite managed to joke:

"What about the one that called Umm Hanan's men off the boat?"

Hilal stopped.

"The divers say there's a voice," said Theyab. "On the pearl banks. It calls men over the side in voices they know. It took her husband and both her sons on the same night. The other men heard it. They say it's half a fish, and big as a dhow, and it calls from the water and the salt. They call it—" He hesitated. "They have a name for it."

"I know what they call it," said Hilal.

His face had changed. It had closed a little, the way a tent closes when the wind gets up, and the dry humour had gone out of it. He put his hand flat on the open book on his knees, as if to keep the pages from blowing.

"Divers' talk," he said. "That one's in no book."

Theyab waited.

"I've read every book there is on them, son of no one. Al-Jahiz, and al-Qazwini, and a dozen lesser men who copied both. I've read them all twice. That one isn't in any of them." He shut the book. "Men drown. The sea is cruel and the banks are deep, and divers go down too many times in a day for too little money, and their heads go strange from the pressure, and they hear things. And then their friends come home and need a reason that isn't the merchant's greed or their own. So they make one." He tied the cord round the book. "That one is in no book. Then it isn't real."

I listened to him say it.

I waited for my hackles to rise. I waited for the sour smell, the turned-milk smell, the thumb-on-the-scale smell. I lay with my nose in the air and every hair on my spine ready.

Nothing.

My hackles stayed flat. He believed it. Every word.

Remember that, little one. My nose can smell a lie. It cannot smell a mistake. An honest man who is wrong smells exactly like an honest man who is right, and there is no gift in the world, not mine and not anyone's, that can tell you the difference until the water closes over your head.

I had heard those drowned voices calling at Umm Hanan's door, only a handful of nights before, with the sea in their throats. I had heard what lent them. And I lay in the shade of the ghaf and watched the only man who had ever come to Hilat al-Sarab to help us tie up his book with his steady old hands, and tell a frightened boy that the thing in the sea wasn't there.

I could not tell him. I have never been able to tell anyone anything.

---

The rider came in the afternoon.

A boy on a lathered camel, from a hamlet two days along the Liwa crescent. A child there was sick, he said. Burning with fever and talking in voices. The mother had sent him for the mutawwa' and would he come.

Hilal's three days were up at sunset. He could have stayed; Mazyad asked him to stay, and asked him again, and nearly begged. Nobody in Hilat al-Sarab wanted him gone. But there was a sick child in a hamlet two days away, and he was a mutawwa', and the old men say that a mutawwa' who chooses between children has stopped being one.

He packed his book. He filled a skin from the well, and named the Maker, and poured the first splash back.

Then he came down to the hollow, the way he had come the first night, and stopped at the edge of it.

Theyab was sitting outside his tent. This time he stood up. He did not know why. I think his body knew, the way a body knows to stand for an old man, even when nobody has ever taught it.

"Iron under your head," said Hilal. "Every night."

"Every night," said Theyab.

"Keep the hirz on. Whatever comes. However nicely it asks."

"However nicely."

Hilal looked at him. At the thin face, the ragged shirt, the left fist.

"And the hand?" he said.

The fist closed tighter. Theyab did not move it from his side.

"Hm. Next time, then." He leaned on his stick and spoke more quietly.

"They'll call you a name," he said. "Soon. I've seen it on them. All this—" his chin went toward the village, the well, the eastern lanes, "—this fear, it needs somewhere to go. It always does. It'll go to you. They'll call you monster."

Theyab did not answer.

"When they do," said Hilal, "remember who said it first."

I saw Theyab remember. The sheikh's yard. The white eyes. The dead mother's voice coming out of a broken man tied to a post. *The monster. Ask him what he is. Monster. Monster.*

"It wasn't them," said Theyab slowly.

"It never is, first," said Hilal. "They only learn the word. Remember who taught it to them."

He turned to go. Then he turned back, and looked down at me, where I stood at Theyab's knee. He looked a long time.

"Look after him, old one," he said.

And then, quieter, so quiet that I am not certain even now that I heard it and did not dream it: "And mind where you look."

He went away up the slope toward the camel and the boy and the long road along the crescent. He did not look back. We stood in the hollow and watched until the camel was a speck on the red dunes, and then nothing, and the sun went down behind the place where it had been.

---

That night Theyab put the khanjar under his head.

He lay down in the mouth of the tent with the rusty blade under the folded blanket where his head would go, and he lay on it, and moved it, and lay on it again, because it was uncomfortable, and he was fifteen, and nobody had ever made him do anything for his own good before. Then he lay still. The hirz rose and fell on his chest.

I lay across the mouth of the tent with my nose on my paws and one ear turned east.

The night was quiet. The Grey lay where it lay. The jackals called in the dunes and stopped, and called again. Up in the village every lamp was out except one, in Umm Hanan's niche, burning steady as a star.

I kept my silver eye shut. I had kept it shut, as much as I could, for three nights.

I did not see what happened in the arish behind the well-keeper's house, a little after midnight, when the jackals stopped.

I did not see the door to the court standing open, though Nadim had barred it himself before he lay down. I did not see the moon come through the gaps in the fronds and lie in white stripes across the floor, across Nadim on his back with one arm over his face, sleeping the deep, drowned sleep of a boy who has not slept properly in a week.

I did not see the mat beside him.

I would see it later. I would see it the way you see a thing you have been dreading so long that when it finally comes it feels almost familiar. A small mat of woven palm, with a blanket thrown back from it, and a hollow in the middle of it the shape of a curled child, still faintly warm.

And nobody in it.

*End of Chapter Ten*
