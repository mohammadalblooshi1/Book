# Chapter Fourteen
## Never Trust a Lamp That Was Waiting for You

> *The bell rings for the liar, and the liar is often you.*
> — stamped round the rim of an old healer's bell, in a script no one in Liwa can read

---

Bakhit was waiting for us.

Of course he was. I'd known he would be from the moment I saw the light. The tent stood alone on the western edge of Hilat al-Sarab, past the last house, past the dung-heap and the broken well and the place where the village threw what it didn't want: a long low tent of black goat-hair, older than anything else in that village, hung inside and out with bundles of drying herbs and little bells of brass and copper on threads. When the wind went through it, it chimed. When the wind didn't, it chimed anyway, sometimes. The children said it was haunted. The children were right, but not the way they meant.

The flap was tied back. The lamp was lit. A fire of ghaf wood was smouldering in the hearth-pit, and the smoke went up straight through the gap in the roof without bending, though the night wind was blowing.

And there was tea already poured. Two glasses.

One on the rug, by the fire, in front of the place where a guest would sit. Steam still curling off it. Poured not a minute ago.

And one by the threshold, on the bare sand just inside the flap, set down carefully on a flat stone so it wouldn't tip. That one was always there. Every night. For as long as Theyab had known Bakhit, and for a great deal longer than that. Bakhit poured it for *the guest*, he said, and never said what guest, and in the morning the glass was always empty, and the sand round it was never marked.

Theyab stopped in the opening and looked at the steaming glass on the rug.

He'd walked here from the far side of the village. Bakhit had no eyes. There was no one else in the tent. And the tea had been poured for him, hot, at exactly the moment he arrived.

"Sit," said Bakhit, before Theyab had even ducked under the flap. "You're bleeding on my harmal."

---

He sat cross-legged on his worn rug at the back of the tent with his hands folded on his knees: a very old man, small and dry and brown as a date-stone, with a white beard that fell to his belt and eyes like two cups of milk. He'd been blind and old as long as anyone in Hilat al-Sarab could remember. The grandmothers said their grandmothers had brought him their sick children.

Theyab didn't sit.

He stood in the opening with the firelight on his face. The cut over his eye had closed in the night, but the blood was still dried black down his cheek. The gold cracks ran under it. In the firelight they showed brighter than they had in the dark: up his throat, along his jaw, out across both cheekbones nearly to his ears, like the veins in a leaf. His shirt was torn at the back and stiff with blood. He was shaking. Not from cold.

He was looking at the fire.

At the little flames licking up the ghaf wood. He couldn't seem to stop looking at them, and he couldn't seem to bear it, and every time one of them flared, his hand jerked toward his chest.

---

I went in first. I stepped round the guest's glass on its stone, the way I always did, with my tail held low and close so it wouldn't brush it, and I lay down across the inside of the opening with my nose on my paws.

I want to tell you about that glass. I've never told anyone.

There was something standing at that threshold. Always. Every night I'd ever lain there, and I'd lain there a great many nights, some of them before Theyab was born. With my amber eye I saw nothing. With the silver one, if I let myself look, which I mostly didn't, I saw something tall and very thin, standing just inside the flap with its back to the room, facing out into the dark. The way a man stands at the door of a house where a child is sleeping. Not moving. Not ever moving.

It was old, and getting thin, the way old cloth gets thin, so that by then I could see the night through it. It had never once looked round at me.

I lay down beside it. It's good to lie beside something that's watching the door. You can close your eyes for a while.

---

"You knew," said Theyab.

Bakhit turned his face toward the sound. For a long moment he said nothing.

Then he said, "Sit down, child. Let me see to your back. And then you can shout at me, and I promise to listen to every word."

The bells were silent. My hackles lay flat. Every word of it was true.

He sat in the end. I think he was too tired not to.

Bakhit's hands were old and dry and very gentle. He felt his way to the torn shirt and peeled it back off the wounds, slowly, where the blood had glued it. Theyab hissed through his teeth. Bakhit washed the cuts with water from a clay bowl that smelled of crushed leaves, and then packed them one by one with a brown paste out of a little jar: myrrh, and something else, bitter as wormwood, that made Theyab's eyes water and his back arch.

"Bitter," said Bakhit. "Good. The bitter ones are the ones that work. Remember that. It's true of more than herbs."

His fingers moved over the pale old scar under Theyab's left shoulder-blade, and then over the new cut, a finger's width from it.

"Close to the old one," Bakhit murmured. "The sand does like to write in the same place twice."

Then he found the lump over Theyab's eye, and the split ear, and he touched each one, and named the stone that made it, as if he'd been there. "Flint, that one. Mud, that one. That one was a piece of jar. Somebody's water-jar. Somebody was angry enough to break their own water-jar to have something to throw." He shook his head slowly. "That's a village for you. They'll go thirsty to hurt you."

He turned his blind face toward the opening where I lay.

"And you," he said. "You've finally let them see you, I hear. Walking in and out of the Grey like a man going to his own well. Barking in it, where nothing barks." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's been a long time, Ghareeb. Since I've seen you do anything at all."

I lifted my head and looked at him. My tail thumped the rug once.

Theyab twisted round. "You know him? Not just— you *know* him?"

Bakhit and I said nothing at the same moment. Which was its own kind of answer.

---

"Drink your tea," said Bakhit.

Theyab drank it. Black and bitter and smoky and green, boiled half to tar, the way Bakhit had made it every night for all the years Theyab had been coming here to sit and listen to stories. He hated it as always. It was the first thing all night that felt like home.

Remember that taste, little one. Remember that I told you to remember it.

He set the empty glass down on the rug.

"What am I?" he said.

---

The fire had burned down to a red bed. Outside, the wind was whispering along the goat-hair walls, testing the seams. The bells turned on their threads.

"You know the story," said Bakhit.

"The Phoenix-Born." Theyab's voice was flat. "The one in the cradle song. A piece of light gets put inside a child. The child carries it to the top of the highest dune in the world and lets it go, and the darkness goes back into the ground."

"Yes."

"That's a story. It's a song you sing to babies to make them sleep."

Bakhit said nothing.

"You told it to me," Theyab said. "Over and over. Since I was small. You told it to me more than any other story. The children with feathers on their chests. How they walk out into the sand and the sand knows them. How the dark comes up and they burn it back down." His voice was rising. "You told it to me while I sat right there and drank your tea. And I *liked* it. I thought it was about someone else."

"Yes," said Bakhit.

The bells were silent.

"I'm not one of them," said Theyab. "I'm not—"

He stopped.

His face twisted. His hand went to his chest, to the feather under his shirt, and pressed hard, and I saw him bite down on the inside of his cheek till it bled.

It was burning.

He'd been about to say *I'm not one of the Phoenix-Born.* He'd wanted it to be true so badly he'd nearly said it. And the feather had caught the words before they reached his lips and set them alight, and for a moment the smell of scorched skin filled the little tent, sharp and unmistakable, and every bell on every thread shivered at once without ringing.

The smell of the lie that had nearly got out of him went up my nose like a needle, and the growl came out of me before I could stop it.

He sat with his eyes squeezed shut, breathing through his nose, waiting for it to fade. When he opened them there was a thin brown mark on his shirt, over his heart, where the cloth had singed.

"There," said Bakhit softly. "You see. You can't even lie to yourself. It won't let you. That's the first thing about the ones who carry it. They can't lie." He reached out and found Theyab's hand and turned it palm up, gently, the right one, the open one, and laid it on his own knee. "Did you never wonder why?"

---

He told him, then.

Bakhit was the best teller I ever knew, and I've known some good ones. He had a way of lowering his voice before the worst part, so you leaned in, and then you were too close to get away. He didn't do that that night. He told it flat and plain, like a man reading out a list of the dead.

The Light was real. The feather on the chest was its mark, and a child was born with it or not, and nobody chose. It came, he said, from the One who made the sands. He didn't say how. He didn't pretend to know. He only knew what it did.

It healed. Theyab had seen that. It drove back the ones from below; it had thrown the thing out of Saqi. It showed the one who carried it the past and the future of whoever it touched, the way the sun shows you a road.

"And it shows you your own end," said Bakhit. "Every time you use it. A little more. You've seen it."

Theyab didn't answer. He didn't need to. Bakhit felt the hand on his knee go rigid.

"A dune," said Bakhit. "The highest. A full moon. And fire."

"How do you know that?"

"Because it's the same for all of them." He said it very gently. "All of them, child. Every one who's carried it. The dune and the moon and the fire. It's always the same."

"All of them," Theyab said. "How many?"

Bakhit didn't answer that. He turned his face a little toward me, and then away. I lay very still.

"There are carvings," he said instead, "in the glass ruins, a long way south and west of here, where the lightning fell on the sand in the old days and made it into glass. Children. Cut into the rock. With feathers on their chests. I've never seen them. I've had them described to me by a man who did. He said you could count them in the time it takes to drink a glass of tea, and that he didn't sleep for a month after."

---

"The Light won't stay in you," Bakhit said. "That's the rest of it. It can't. It isn't made to. It goes out of you in the end, all at once, like water out of a broken skin. And when it goes out it drives the dark back down into the sand for a while. A long while. A lifetime. Longer."

"When I die," said Theyab.

"When you give it."

"What's the difference?"

Bakhit was quiet a long time.

"The difference," he said, "is that nobody can take it from you. Not the Grey. Not the ones from below. Not the men who'll come for you; and they'll come, child, they've already started. Nobody can cut it out of you and nobody can burn it out of you against your will. It goes only one way. You have to *choose* it. You have to walk up that dune with your own feet and open your own arms. Freely. Or it doesn't go at all."

"And if I don't?"

Bakhit's blind face didn't change. But something in the tent did. The fire sank. The bells went still on their threads, every one, as if the wind had stopped to listen. And the thing at the threshold beside me, the thin old thing with its back to the room, for the first time in all the years I'd lain beside it, *moved*. Not much. A tremor, like a man who hears a name he knows called in a crowd.

"Then it's very bad," said Bakhit, "for a great many people. And for you."

He said it in a way that closed the subject, the way you'd shut a box. And I knew why, and I didn't know if Theyab heard it, and I lay there with my nose on my paws and thought about a white dune under a white moon three hundred years ago, and a boy running down it.

That isn't my story to tell yet either. You'll get it. Bakhit tells it himself, in the end, better than I could.

---

"Every time you heal," Bakhit said, "you'll see it more clearly. The first time, a glimpse. You saw a dune and a light. Tonight, you felt it. You felt your skin. Didn't you?"

Theyab's hand on his knee had started to shake.

"I couldn't scream," Theyab said. "That was the worst. Not the burning. That I couldn't move. That I was screaming inside and nothing came out." He swallowed. "And someone was shouting at the bottom of the dune. Shouting a name. I couldn't tell if it was mine."

"Next time you'll hear it," said Bakhit. "And the time after that you'll know the voice."

"Then I won't heal anyone."

The feather didn't burn. It wasn't a lie. He meant it, then, in that tent, with his back packed with bitter paste and his face full of gold.

Bakhit only nodded, slowly, as if Theyab had said something about the weather.

"No," he said. "No, I don't suppose you will. Not until the next one."

---

And then it came.

I'd felt it coming up in him all the long walk back across the dark village, the way you feel the ground shake before you hear the camels, and now it came up out of him all at once.

He snatched his hand off Bakhit's knee.

"You knew," he said.

His voice wasn't loud. That was the terrible thing. I'd have been glad if he'd shouted.

"You knew. All the years since I came. Eight years. Since nobody wanted me and you called me in out of the dark, *you there, you with the dog, come and drink something.* You knew what I was. You saw the feather. You heard me talk in my sleep. You told me the story over and over and *over* and you watched me sit there and drink it like it was milk." He was breathing hard. "You knew what they'd do to me. You *knew*. You knew they'd find out, someday, and you knew what a village does to a thing it's frightened of, you've lived in villages longer than anyone alive. You've seen it. You knew they'd take stones to me and drive me out like a mad dog."

He stood up. The bitter paste cracked on his back.

"You knew," he said. "And you let them stone me."

---

Bakhit lifted his head.

He turned his milk-white eyes up to where Theyab stood, as if he could see him perfectly well. And perhaps he could. I've never been sure what Bakhit saw.

"Yes," he said.

And the bell didn't ring.

---

I want you to hear that properly, little one.

There was a bell in that tent. A big one, bigger than the others: brass, old, gone brown, hanging from the main pole just inside the door on a cord of plaited hair. It didn't ring in the wind. It had never once rung in the wind. It rang for lies, and only for lies, and it had rung once, the morning before the Diggers came, when Theyab sat on that same rug and said *I'm not afraid.* It would ring for a lie whispered in the dark at the far end of the tent. It would ring for a lie you told yourself. It would ring, Bakhit said, for the lie in a man's face when he smiled at you.

He said *yes*, and the bell hung there on its pole and didn't move.

It was true. Every bit of it. He'd known. He'd let them.

And he wasn't ashamed. That was what I smelled on him, sitting there with the boy towering over him and the fire gone to embers. I put my nose up and smelled for it, the way I'd smelled for the smokeless fire on Saqi. Shame has a smell, sour and close, like a goat-pen in summer. It wasn't there. There was grief on him, old and deep, a smell like the inside of a closed chest. And something under the grief. Something hard, like a stone at the bottom of a well.

He wasn't sorry. He'd do it again.

Theyab smelled it too, I think, in his own way. His face went white under the gold.

He picked up the empty tea glass from the rug and threw it.

Not at Bakhit. At the wall. It struck the goat-hair and fell without breaking and rolled across the rug into the embers. Every bell in the tent rang at once, a wild jangling clamour of brass and copper, and for a moment the noise was so great that I couldn't hear anything else.

Except the big bell. The big bell didn't move.

Theyab stood there breathing in the noise until it faded.

"Why?" he said.

Bakhit sat a long time with his hands on his knees.

"Because the ones who know too early run," he said at last. "And there's no running from it. Only to it, the long way, with more people dead behind you." He turned his face away, toward the threshold, toward me and the thing beside me. "I know something about running, child. More than I've told you. And the night I tell you, you'll understand why I'd rather they threw stones at you than that you learned it the way I did."

The bell didn't ring for that either.

Theyab sat down. Not because he'd been answered. Because his legs gave out.

He put his face in his hands.

He didn't cry. I watched him not cry for a long time, sitting there with his shoulders up round his ears and his back cracked with bitter paste and his breath going in and out of him like a saw through wood. I came across the rug on my belly, slowly, and put my head on his foot. He didn't push me off.

Outside the wind went along the walls.

At the threshold the guest's glass sat on its flat stone, full. It had been full when we came in. I looked at it. And I swear to you, little one, as I'm lying here at your feet with my one good eye, while we'd been talking, it had gone down by half.

---

Then I heard the stick.

Far off at first, out on the gravel to the east. *Tock.* A pause. *Tock.* The sound a stick makes when an old man is using it not for walking but to feel his way: to test the ground ahead for holes and snakes and the soft places where the sand gives. And under it, so low that only a dog would hear it, a voice. A man's voice, dry and rhythmic and endless, saying something under its breath over and over as it walked. The way water says something over and over as it runs along a falaj.

The thing at the threshold heard it too.

It stepped back.

I felt it go. One pace, two, out of the opening, the way you'd step back from a door when you hear someone coming who you don't want to be seen by. Not frightened. Courteous. Then it was gone, and I couldn't see it with either eye, and the opening was only an opening with the cold dark beyond it.

The lamp guttered.

It had burned steady all night. Now it bent and shrank and spat, as if a wind had come into the tent, though nothing stirred the herbs on their strings.

And my eye began to ache.

The silver one. A dull, deep ache, like a bad tooth in cold weather, from somewhere behind it, from the bone. It had ached in the dead grove. It always ached when he was in danger. But this was different. This came with the voice. With every word of that low endless saying out there on the gravel, it throbbed once, as if each word were a small stone dropped on it from a height.

I got up. I went out of the tent.

I told myself I was going to see who it was. I knew who it was. I went out because I couldn't stand to lie there with that voice coming nearer and nearer and my eye going *tock, tock* in my skull in time with the stick.

---

He came up out of the dark from the east with the stars behind him. A thin old man, not tall, in a goat-hair cloak gone grey with dust from the road. A long stick in his right hand, a cloth bag over his shoulder. A face burned black as a pot by sixty summers of sun, with a short grey beard and two deep lines from nose to mouth, the kind a man gets from frowning at books and laughing at people.

He stopped when he saw me standing in front of the tent.

He looked at me for a long moment. At my face. At my eyes, one and then the other.

His lips went on moving. He didn't stop reciting. He never stopped, I learned, unless he was talking, and sometimes not then. But his eyes narrowed. The way a man's eyes narrow when he sees a word in a book he doesn't know, and decides to look it up later.

My eye throbbed. I didn't look away. I'm a dog. We don't.

Then he nodded to me, once, as one old man nods to another across a market, and stepped round me and stooped under the flap.

I stood out in the cold a while longer. Until the ache settled. Then I went back in and lay down across the opening where the thing had been, and kept my silver eye shut.

---

"Bakhit," said Mutawwa' Hilal.

"Hilal," said Bakhit.

They didn't get up. They didn't embrace. They looked at each other across the embers, the blind man and the reader, the way two old camels look at each other across a well: without much love, and without any surprise.

"You still read over people who don't want it," said Bakhit.

"You still pour tea for the threshold," said Hilal.

"It drinks it."

"I know." Hilal lowered himself to the rug with a grunt, and laid his stick beside him, and set his bag down. "That's what worries me."

Theyab was staring at them both. His face had gone very still.

"You know each other," he said.

"Forty years," said Hilal. "He was already old. He was already blind. I was young and stupid and I thought I could read his eyes open again." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I was there four days. I read until I couldn't speak. Nothing. Not a flicker. It was the first time in my life the words didn't do what I asked." He let his hands fall. "I've been insulted ever since."

The bell didn't ring.

"He's been coming back to try again," said Bakhit, "once every few years. For forty years. He doesn't admit it."

"I come for the tea," said Hilal.

The bell rang.

One clear note, high and sweet, like a drop of water falling into a full jar. It hung in the air of the tent. Theyab jumped. Hilal looked up at the bell, and then over at Bakhit, and the two deep lines beside his mouth deepened, and he laughed. A short dry laugh, like a stick breaking.

"I'd forgotten that thing," he said.

"It hasn't forgotten you," said Bakhit.

---

Then Hilal looked at Theyab properly.

He looked at the gold in his face, the cracks running up his throat and across his cheeks. He looked at the bitter paste on his back and the blood dried down his cheek and the split ear. He looked at the knotted cord round his neck, with the hirz on it, and his mouth tightened.

"I heard," he said. "On the road. I was a few hours out toward Liwa. A herder coming the other way told me the sky over Hilat al-Sarab lit up gold at midnight, like a lamp under a bowl. He thought it was a sign of something. He thought it might be good." He shook his head. "I turned round. Light like that, in a village like this, there's only one thing that follows it. I walked all night. I hoped I'd be wrong."

He reached out and took hold of the hirz between two fingers, and turned it to the lamp, and looked at the knot in the cord.

"Who cut this?"

"A stone," said Theyab.

"And who took it off you?"

Theyab didn't answer. He looked at me.

Hilal looked at me too. For a long time. I put my nose on my paws and looked at the fire.

"Hm," said Hilal.

He let the hirz fall back against Theyab's chest. He put out his hand, palm down, a little above Theyab's head, not touching, and closed his eyes, and his lips began to move again. Low. Quick. The way he'd read over Saqi in the sheikh's yard, but softer, a reading for a hurt child rather than a war. I won't tell you the words. I couldn't if I wanted to. I only heard the breath of them, rising and falling, like the sea that I've seen only twice in my life.

My eye ached. The lamp bent and spat. Outside, far off in the dark, I felt the thin old thing that watched the door stand at a distance with its back to us, waiting, patient, for the words to stop so it could come home.

That's the thing about the words, little one. Hilal never knew it, or wouldn't let himself. They don't ask who's at the door. They push back everything.

---

When he'd finished, he opened his eyes.

Theyab was sitting very straight. The trembling had gone out of his hands. The gold in his face was still there, but it had dimmed, as if a cloth had been laid over a lamp.

"Better?" said Hilal.

Theyab nodded. Then he looked at Bakhit. Then back at Hilal. And he said, quietly, in a voice I hadn't heard him use before, a voice with something cold at the bottom of it:

"Did you know too? What I am. When you tied the hirz on me. When you looked at me in the sheikh's yard and didn't call me a monster. Did you know?"

Hilal looked at him for a long moment.

"No," he said. "I suspected."

The bell didn't ring.

"I suspected the light," Hilal said. "Anyone would. You don't throw a Worn man across a yard with your bare arms by being an ordinary boy. But the light's not what I came back for. Bakhit can tell you about the light. He's had longer to think about it than anyone alive." He leaned forward. His face was very close to Theyab's now, and the lamplight lay in the deep lines of it. "I came back because of the other thing. The thing I asked you about, the day I left. The thing you wouldn't show me."

Theyab's left hand closed.

It had been closed all night. It had been closed, I think, all his life, the way some children suck their thumb: he'd done it so long he didn't know he was doing it. But now it closed tighter. I heard the knuckles crack. I saw the tendons stand out in his wrist.

Bakhit had gone very still.

The guest's glass sat on its stone at the threshold. Half full. The lamp burned low and steady again. Somewhere out in the dark, a long way off to the south, something that wasn't a jackal lifted its voice and let it fall.

And Hilal held out his own hand, palm up, in the firelight, and waited.

"Now," he said gently. "Show me your hand, son of no one."

---

*End of Chapter Fourteen*
