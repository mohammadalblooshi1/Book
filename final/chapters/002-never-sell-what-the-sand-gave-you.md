# Chapter Two
## Never Sell What the Sand Gave You

> *"A trader's scale has two pans, and his thumb is the third."*
> — a saying of the Liwa market

---

He didn't sleep again after the words.

He sat with his back against the tent pole and his knees drawn up, and he said them over to himself in a whisper, trying to catch them before they went, the way you try to hold a dream in your hands after waking. Five or six sounds with water running through them. By the time the light came grey under the tent flap he had two of them left. By the time the doves started in the palms he had none.

"It was nothing," he told me. "A dream-word. Everyone says dream-words."

I lay with my chin on my paws and kept my hackles exactly where they were, which was flat, and he looked at them for a while, because he'd learned long ago to read my back the way the herders read the sky. Flat meant he believed it. Flat didn't mean it was true. He knew the difference. He just didn't want to know it that morning.

Then he took the thaler out of his fist, where it had sweated all night, and looked at it in the grey light, and his face changed into the face of a boy who has found silver, and the words were gone for good.

That's what silver is for, little one. Making you forget the thing you should be remembering.

---

Ghanim's stall stood at the corner of the square where the lane from the well came in, so that every bucket of water and every bundle of firewood and every goat bound for the coast had to pass under his eye. It was a roof of palm fronds on four poles, a mat, a strongbox with a lock the size of a fist, and a pair of brass scales that hung from a crossbar and shone like the only honest thing in the village. They weren't. They had a thumb in them. Everyone knew it. Ghanim had been getting slowly rich on that thumb for twenty years, and the village paid it the way you pay the heat: with a curse under your breath and nothing else.

He was a narrow man with a well-oiled beard and very clean hands. That was the first thing anyone noticed about Ghanim, in a village where everyone's hands were cracked from rope and dough and goat. His hands were soft. He rubbed them with sesame oil at night, and they smelled of it, and under the sesame they smelled of silver, which is a smell like a cold tongue.

Theyab put his sack on the mat.

He'd been saving it for weeks. Bent copper from the ruins, a bronze ring green as a lizard, the brass lip of some old lamp, a handful of beads, a strip of something that might once have been the edge of a tray. He laid it out piece by piece. He always laid it out. He thought if Ghanim could see it all at once he might be ashamed to cheat it.

Ghanim didn't look at the copper. He looked at Theyab's left hand, which was closed.

"What's in the fist, Laqeet?"

Theyab could have said *nothing*. That's what any other boy in Hilat al-Sarab would have said, any boy in the world. *Nothing.* And opened his hand later, at home, alone, and kept what the sand had given him.

He couldn't.

You need to know how it was with him, and you need to know it now, because it's the hinge of everything. He could not lie. Not *would* not. *Could* not. When a lie got as far as his tongue, the mark on his chest, the small gold feather that sat just under his collarbone where his shirt hid it, would burn. Not a little. Like a coal from the coffee hearth pressed into the skin and held. Deep enough that he could smell it. Once, at nine, he'd tried to force a lie past the pain to save himself a beating, and he'd woken face down in the dust with the beating already done and a blister on his chest the exact shape of a feather.

So he didn't say *nothing*.

He said, "Silver," and opened his fingers just enough to show the edge of it.

Ghanim's eyebrows went up. They were very expressive eyebrows. They did half his lying for him.

"Let me see."

Theyab put the thaler on the mat. Ghanim picked it up between two oiled fingers, turned it, bit it, rang it on the edge of the strongbox, held it up to the light. The fat-chinned queen looked out at the square with her foreign indifference.

"Well," said Ghanim. "Well, well."

He put it down on his side of the mat. Not on Theyab's.

Then he weighed the copper. He hung the pieces in the left pan one by one, and the beam swung, and his hand rested on the crossbar the way a man's hand rests on his knee, and the beam settled where his hand wanted it to settle. I watched his thumb. I always watched his thumb. It moved about the width of a grain of barley, and the copper lost a third of what it weighed.

My hackles went up.

Not for the scales. Scales don't lie; they only do what they're told. For what came next.

"Copper's poor this month," said Ghanim. "Too many scavengers."

There was exactly one scavenger in Hilat al-Sarab, and he was standing in front of Ghanim's mat.

The lie came off the trader sour and quick, like breath off a man who has eaten onions. The growl came up in my chest. Ghanim's eyes went to me, and away.

"Keep that animal off my mat."

"He won't touch it," Theyab said, which was true. He put his hand on my neck anyway. The growl went back down where it came from, but it didn't go far.

Ghanim counted out annas. Worn, small, the coppers the Indian traders brought up from the coast. He counted half of what the copper was worth, and then a little less than half, and then he said, "And the silver I'll keep for you."

Theyab looked at him.

"For safekeeping," said Ghanim kindly. "A boy like you, with a thaler? Somebody'll say you stole it. Somebody'll beat you for it and take it anyway, and then where are you? I'll keep it safe. When you need it, you come to me. It's yours. It's always yours."

My hackles rose so hard they hurt. Every word of that last part stank. I growled out loud. I couldn't help it. A woman crossing the square with a water-jar on her hip looked over, and looked away.

Theyab knew. Of course he knew. He'd lived next to my back for eight years. He looked at my hackles and he looked at Ghanim's oiled face and he said, very quietly, the only true thing he could say:

"Will you give it back?"

"Of course," said Ghanim.

I barked. Once, sharp, right in his face. Ghanim jerked back and the scales swung.

"I said keep that thing off my mat!"

And Theyab said nothing. That was the other thing about him, and it was nearly as bad as the first. He couldn't lie, and he couldn't fight a grown man in the square for a coin, not and stay in Hilat al-Sarab afterward. He had nowhere else. Ghanim knew that better than anyone. Ghanim had built his whole life out of knowing what people had nowhere else to go.

"Hold out your hand," said Ghanim, and lifted the annas. "Come on. Both hands, I'm not dropping my coppers in the dirt."

Theyab held out his right hand.

"Both," said Ghanim. "Make a bowl."

Theyab hesitated. I felt him hesitate; I was leaning on his leg. Then he did what any boy would do when a grown man tells him to make a bowl of his hands for money. He brought his left hand up beside his right. And to make the bowl, he opened it.

Ghanim poured the annas.

He poured them slowly. Too slowly. The coins went chink, chink, chink into the cup of the boy's hands, and Ghanim's eyes weren't on the coins. They were on the left palm, where the line ran under the copper. One straight crease from one edge of the hand to the other, unbroken, the kind of line a midwife sees and goes quiet about.

I saw his eyes find it. I saw them stop.

He said nothing.

He finished pouring. He dusted his clean oiled fingers together. And then he smiled. It was a small smile, and it didn't go anywhere near his eyes, and it smelled of nothing at all, which was the worst thing about it. A lie I can smell. A smile like that is just a door closing quietly in a house where you didn't know anyone was home.

"Go on, then," said Ghanim, and he took a date from the bowl at his elbow, a good fat one, and held it out. "For the walk."

Theyab stared at the date. In eight years Ghanim had never once given him anything.

He took it. He was hungry. He was always hungry. And he walked away across the square with the annas in one fist and the date in the other, and he didn't look back, and so he didn't see what I saw, which was Ghanim picking up the thaler and holding it, not to the light this time, but to his nose. Sniffing it. The way you'd sniff a cut of meat to see where it had been.

I bared my teeth at him across the square.

He smiled again, and put the silver in his sleeve.

---

He sold the sand's gift, and the sand saw him do it. I'll tell you what the old people say about that, little one, since nobody else will. They say the sand keeps a tally. They say when it gives you something out of its own belly, a coin or a ring or a pot of old gold, it's watching what you do with it. Give it away and the sand forgets. Sell it, and the sand remembers who bought.

That's not a rule. It's just what old people say. I've noticed it's true more often than rules are.

---

Ghanim followed us that noon.

I knew it before we were halfway to the ruins. I smelled sesame oil on the hot wind, and silver, and the sweat of a man who doesn't walk much. When I looked back I saw a shape duck behind the shoulder of the first dune: a man in a clean white dishdasha in the full blaze of noon, where no grown man in Hilat al-Sarab would go if you paid him. Ghanim had a box of thalers and a strong opinion about the Noon Mare. He'd decided that the first was worth the second.

I nudged Theyab's hand. He looked back. He saw nothing; Ghanim was lying flat behind the crest like a lizard. Theyab shrugged and went on.

I let him. I wanted to see what the trader would do. That was a mistake, and I'll own it.

Theyab went down into the room where he'd found the thaler and began to dig again, in the same corner, with the same patience, sifting every handful through his fingers. I lay at the top of the steps. The heat stood over the ruins like a held breath. The walls ticked. And after a while, behind me, very softly, came the crunch of Ghanim's sandals on the old falaj channel.

Then he stopped crunching.

Then he said, under his breath, "Oh, you filthy thing."

I was up and round the corner of the wall before Theyab had lifted his head.

Ghanim was standing in the dry channel with his back pressed to the stone and a stick in his hands, a long dead branch of arak, and in front of him, coiled in the bed of the channel where a little blown sand had gathered, there was a snake.

It was pale. That was the first thing. Not the sand-coloured, horned, sideways-crawling viper that every child in Liwa learns to fear, the one whose bite turns your leg black. This was pale the way bone is pale, the way milk is pale, smooth and long and lying in a loose coil with its head lifted a hand's width off the sand. It wasn't threatening. It wasn't hissing. It was simply looking at him, with its head on one side, the way you'd look at a man who'd come into your tent without asking.

Its eyes were the colour of honey held to a lamp.

My hackles didn't move. Not one hair.

"Get back, Laqeet," said Ghanim, as Theyab came round the wall behind me. "It's a viper. Get back. I'll break its back." He lifted the stick.

"It's not a viper."

"Every snake's a viper." Ghanim was sweating. Silver sweat, sour. He'd come out here at noon to find where the thalers came from, and the sand had sent him a snake instead, and he didn't like being looked at. "They're all from below. Everyone knows that. Kill the snake you see, or it'll come back for you at night."

He swung.

Theyab stepped in between.

He didn't think about it. I'm sure of that. He simply moved, the way a boy moves to catch a falling cup, and the stick that was meant for the snake's back came down on his shoulder instead. It made a sound like a stick hitting a sack of grain. He staggered. He didn't go down.

Ghanim stared at him. "Are you mad?"

Theyab didn't answer him. He turned his back on the trader and crouched down in the channel, face to face with the pale snake, close enough that if it had wanted his eye it could have had it. His shoulder was bleeding a little through his shirt. He didn't look at it.

And he did what the old people do.

You won't know this, little one. Most of your generation won't. When the old people of the sands find a snake in the house, or at the well, or coiled in the shade of the tent wall where the baby sleeps, they don't kill it first. Not straight away. Because you never know, they say. You never know who's wearing the snake. So you speak to it. You tell it to go. You tell it three times, politely, the way you'd tell a guest who's outstayed his welcome, and if it goes, it goes, and no blood is between you. If it stays after the third time, then you may do what you must, and whatever was wearing it has no complaint.

I don't know who taught him. Nobody in Hilat al-Sarab ever taught him anything. Perhaps he'd heard an old woman do it once. Perhaps he'd always known.

"Go from this place," he said to the snake. Low and clear. "We don't want harm with you. Go, and go safely."

The snake looked at him.

Ghanim made a disgusted sound. "Talking to vermin. Like calls to like, I suppose."

"Go from this place," Theyab said again. "We don't want harm with you. Go, and go safely."

The pale head swayed. Its tongue came out, black, and tasted the air between them, very near his face. I stood behind him and I didn't growl and I didn't move, and I'll tell you a thing I've never told anyone: I couldn't have. Not because I was afraid. Because I was holding my breath, the way the old man tells the children to hold theirs, and for the same reason. Something was being decided.

"Go from this place," Theyab said a third time. "We don't want harm with you. Go, and go safely."

And it went.

Not fast. Not like a snake flees. It uncoiled the way silk is drawn off a spool, and it slid down the channel along the foot of the wall, pale against the pale stone, and at the corner where the old falaj ran under a fallen lintel it stopped. It turned its head. It looked back at him. Not at Ghanim. Not at me. At him. For a long moment.

Then it was gone into the dark under the lintel, and the noon closed over the place where it had been as if it had never been there at all.

The air smelled, very faintly, of milk.

I sat down very suddenly in the hot sand. My legs didn't want to hold me.

"Mad," said Ghanim. He threw the stick down. "Mad as a goat in a well. You'll be bitten in your sleep and good riddance." He looked round at the ruins, at the shut rooms and the half-buried walls and the dark under the lintel where the snake had gone. Then he looked at Theyab, crouched in the channel with his bleeding shoulder, and the look changed. "Is this where you found it? The silver?"

Theyab stood up.

He could have said nothing. He could have walked away. But Ghanim had asked him a straight question, and Theyab's mouth only had one kind of answer in it.

"Yes," he said.

Ghanim smiled.

He didn't stay. He looked at the ruins for a long time, the way a man looks at a woman he means to marry for her father's camels, and then he went back across the dunes toward the village with his clean dishdasha dark with sweat, walking fast, the way you walk when you have somewhere to be and someone to tell.

He never came back to dig. I wondered about that, at the time. A man like Ghanim, shown where silver lies, and he never once came back with a spade?

I don't wonder now. He'd found something better than silver that morning, and he'd found it in a boy's open hand. He didn't need the ruins. He only needed a buyer.

---

The bundle was on the wall that evening.

There was a low mud wall at the back of the lane, behind the last houses, where nobody went except the goats and the boy and the dog. Every few days something appeared on top of it. A square of faded indigo cloth knotted at the corners. Inside, flatbread, thin and blistered, still warm from the baking plate. And on the good days, a twist of waxed paper with four dates in it, soaked dark and sticky in sidr honey from the mountains.

It was a good day.

He sat on the wall with his bleeding shoulder stiffening and held the dates in his open palm for a long time without eating them. Dates in honey were the finest thing he knew of in the world. He always saved them for the hardest days, as if sweetness could be stored the way you store water against a drought. He ate the bread. He tore it in two first and gave me half. He always did that. I never once asked.

He didn't know who left the bundles. When he was small he'd decided it must be the One who made the sands, who had made a mistake with him and left bread to say sorry. When he was older he stopped deciding and just ate.

I knew.

I'd followed the smell of that bread back to its oven the very first morning, eight years ago, while he was still crying in a language no one spoke.

---

Umm Hanan baked before the doves woke.

Her house was the last on the lane behind the well, mud brick gone soft at the corners, with an indigo door that her husband had painted the year they married, the deep blue that keeps the eye off a house. Behind it, in a yard no bigger than a tent, she had a clay oven and an iron plate on three stones, and every morning in the dark she lit the fire and knelt in front of a wide basin of dough and worked.

She was old. Older than she looked, and she looked very old. Small, bent from the waist, with a face as creased as a dry wadi bed and a black abaya gone brown at the hem with flour and dust. She lived alone. She'd lived alone for a long time.

Years before the boy ever came up out of the noon, her husband had gone down to the coast for the pearling season, and her two sons with him, the elder as a diver, the younger to haul the rope. In the autumn the boats came home, sails down, men singing, little leather bags of pearls tied at their belts. Her men's boat came home too. Her men were not on it. The rest of the crew were, every one, with their eyes on the deck. Nobody ever told her properly what happened. Or rather, somebody did, and she never repeated it, and I'll come to what it was.

Every morning since, kneading the dough, she said their names.

Three names. In the same order, every morning, under her breath, in time with the heels of her hands. Her husband's. Her elder son's. Her younger son's. Pushed down into the dough, folded in, pushed down again, the way you'd push salt in. As if, so long as she put them into the bread, they were still being fed. As if bread was a kind of rope you could let down into the sea.

I won't tell you their names. I heard them every morning for eight years, lying in the dark lane outside her yard, and I could say them now, in my head, in her voice. I won't give them to you. Names are not mine to give. And I've seen what the wind does with names that people say carelessly. I won't be the one.

She kneaded. She said them. She shaped the loaves and slapped them onto the plate, and when the first water boiled in her blackened pot for the morning's tea, she did a thing I've never seen another person in Hilat al-Sarab do so faithfully. Before she poured the boiling water out on the ground to rinse the pot, she bent and spoke over it. She named the Creator, quietly, and told whatever might be lying under the earth there to move aside. Every morning. Every single time. In case anything was sleeping under her yard that a splash of boiling water would hurt.

I used to wonder why she bothered. I don't now. I'll tell you why soon enough.

Then, every few days, she'd wrap a loaf in a square of indigo cloth. And she'd go to the shelf where she kept the honey from the mountains, which cost her more than she had, and take four dates out of the jar with her fingers. And she'd go out the back way, in the dark, before the lane woke, with her abaya pulled close, and put the bundle on the wall where the foundling went. She never once spoke to him in the square. She never stood up for him when the boys threw stones. She was old and alone and frightened, and in Hilat al-Sarab kindness out loud could cost a widow everything she had left. So she was kind quietly. Before the doves. For eight years. With nobody ever thanking her.

She did it the way you drop a date-stone in wet ground and walk on, not even thinking of a tree.

The world is held together by people like Umm Hanan, little one. Too frightened to be brave. Good anyway, in small ways, in the dark.

---

The well stood in the middle of the square, a ring of old stone with a wooden frame over it and a pulley that shrieked. Saqi the well-keeper sat beside it on a palm-rib stool from first light to dusk with a stick across his knees, and every bucket that came up out of that well came up on his rope, and every family in Hilat al-Sarab paid him for it in dates or coffee or coppers or favours, and the ones who couldn't pay waited till he wasn't looking.

Theyab couldn't pay. Theyab waited.

We waited in the shade of the wall at the corner of the square, that evening after the bread, with the dates still saved in his shirt. The sun was going down red behind the palms. The women had come and gone with their jars. Saqi had shouted at a boy for spilling, and cuffed him, and shouted again. He was a big, sour man with a beard going grey in streaks and a voice like the pulley. His wife had died in childbirth seven years before, and since then he'd had two sons and a rope, and he treated all three about the same.

The two sons were at the well now.

Nadim was the elder. Fifteen, like Theyab, near enough, and broad where Theyab was narrow, quick in the tongue where Theyab was slow. The well-keeper's son. In a village where every drop came up on your father's rope, that made you nearly a sheikh's son, and Nadim had grown up knowing it. He sat on the stone lip of the well with his legs dangling and three other boys round him like flies round a date.

And the younger one, Shibl, was sitting in the dust at his feet.

Seven years old. Small for it, with a head too big for his neck and enormous dark eyes, and ears that stuck out like the handles on a water-jar. He had a piece of string and was doing something very serious with it. Lion cub, his name means. He looked about as much like a lion as a dove does.

He looked up and saw me.

Not Theyab. Me. And his face did a thing that faces almost never did when they saw me: it opened. He dropped the string. He got up. He came across the square toward us in that straight, unbothered way small children walk toward things they want, and he stopped an arm's length from me and stared.

At my eye. The silver one.

"It's like money," he said. He said it to Theyab, but he didn't take his eyes off me. "His eye. It's like a coin. Like a coin in the well."

Theyab didn't say anything. People didn't usually talk to him. He didn't know where to put his face.

"Can I touch him?"

"He doesn't bite," said Theyab. True. Mostly.

Shibl put out his hand. It was sticky, and it smelled of dates and string and goat. He put it on my head, between my ears, very carefully, the way you'd put your hand on a sleeping baby, and then he leaned in close until his nose was nearly touching mine and looked straight into the silver.

I let him.

I don't know why. I never let anyone look into that eye. But he looked, and I let him, and I felt his small breath on my face, and I thought: *this one.* I didn't know what I meant by it. I still don't, entirely. I only knew that something in me lay down and turned over and showed its belly to a seven-year-old boy with jug ears, and I've never got it back up.

"There's something in it," Shibl whispered. "Like a lamp. Far down."

My silver eye gave one small throb.

"Shibl!"

Nadim was off the well and across the square. He grabbed his brother by the back of the shirt and hauled him away so hard the boy's feet left the ground.

"Don't touch that. Don't you touch that. It's *his*." He put Shibl behind him. "You want a curse on you? You want to wake up with your eyes gone silver?"

"It's just a dog," said Shibl, struggling.

"It's not *just* anything." Nadim looked at Theyab, and his face changed. It got easier, the way a man's face gets easier when he's found a thing he knows how to do. "Look who's come to the well. Thirsty, Laqeet?"

Theyab said, "Yes."

The boys at the well laughed. That was how it started. It always started like that. Nadim had discovered, years before, the thing about Theyab that the rest of the village never bothered to notice: that he couldn't lie. And Nadim had made a sport of it.

"Got any money for my father's water?"

"No."

"Did Ghanim cheat you again?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Half."

They laughed harder. Saqi, on his stool, looked up and didn't stop them.

"Do you cry at night, Laqeet?"

Theyab's jaw went tight. I felt it, the struggle in him, the way you feel a camel straining against its hobble. He wanted to say *no*. I watched his hand go to his chest, flat, over the shirt, over the mark. The burn starting.

"Yes," he said.

The boys howled.

"What do you cry about?"

"I don't know."

"Who loves you, Laqeet?" Nadim was grinning now, enjoying it, his face flushed. "Go on. Somebody must. Who loves you?"

And Theyab looked at me.

He didn't say it. He didn't have to. He looked down at me, sitting by his leg in the dust, and the boys followed his eyes, and the laugh that went up off the well was the worst I'd heard in eight years. One of them barked. Another got down on his hands and knees and whined. Even Saqi snorted into his beard.

Shibl didn't laugh. Shibl stood behind his brother and looked at Theyab with his enormous eyes, and something in his small face was working, slowly, like a boy doing sums.

Nadim held up a hand. The boys quieted. He had one more. I could see it coming the way you see weather.

"Last one," he said. "And you have to tell the truth. You always have to tell the truth." He stepped close. "Are you a monster?"

The square went quiet.

I want you to see it. The red light going down behind the palms. The well-frame black against the sky. The women in the doorways, who had stopped pretending not to listen. Saqi with his stick across his knees. Shibl with his mouth open. And Theyab, with one hand on his chest and the other, the left one, closed tight at his side, trying to find the true answer to that question so he could say it. Because he had to. Because he couldn't say anything else.

He didn't say *no*.

He couldn't. Do you understand? He didn't know. He lay awake on the dark nights with the feather glowing faintly under his shirt and pulsing in time with something that wasn't his heart. He talked in his sleep in a language no one in the world spoke. He had come out of the noon like the Noon Mare came out of the noon. He didn't know what he was. And so the answer that came up in his mouth, the only true one, was the one that would hurt him most.

"I don't know," he said.

Nobody laughed at that one.

Nadim's face went odd. He'd wanted *no*. He'd wanted to laugh at *no*. *I don't know* was a different kind of animal, and it had come into the square and lain down among them, and nobody quite wanted to look at it.

"Monster," said Nadim, but quietly, and not to anyone.

Then Saqi was off his stool and across the square, and he cuffed Nadim hard across the back of the head, so hard the boy staggered. "Leave the foundling be and fetch the goats," he snapped. "I'm not feeding you to stand about." He hit him again, the flat of his hand across the ear, for nothing, for being there. "Go on."

Nadim went. He didn't look back. But I saw his face as he passed us, red down one side where his father's hand had been, and his eyes were wet, and he was looking at the ground the way Theyab looked at the ground. The same exact way.

I smelled no lie on him. Only a boy, and a bruise.

People are rarely only one thing, little one. Remember that. You'll need it later, when you want to hate him.

Saqi went back to his stool. After a while, without looking at us, without a word, he lowered the bucket, and drew it up, and set it on the stone lip of the well, full, and turned his back on it.

Theyab drank.

---

He went up the big dune after dark.

It was the highest one near the village, a long red ridge to the south, and on clear nights he went up it to sit alone and look at the sands. He'd done it since he was small. He said the sky was bigger from up there. I think the truth was that the village was smaller.

The cold had come. It comes fast in the sands, the minute the sun is gone. The stars were out so thick that the sky looked like a black cloth someone had spilled salt on. Suhail was not yet up; it was still too early in the year. The Pleiades were climbing in the east, the little knot of stars the herders call al-Thurayya, the sisters.

He sat on the crest with his knees up and his arms round them and the four honey-dates still in his shirt, saved. His shoulder ached where Ghanim's stick had caught it. He didn't touch it. He was looking south and east over the dark sands, toward nothing, toward everywhere.

He'd been called *Laqeet* since the noon he came up. Foundling. He'd never had another name from anyone. You don't name a thing you're waiting to give back.

So he'd given himself one.

He was seven, the first year. Sitting on the lip of the well at dusk, alone, after the village had gone in. And a word came into his mouth. It came the way the strange words came in his sleep, already shaped, as if someone had left it there for him to find. *Theyab.* Wolves. The plural, not the singular. Not *a wolf*. A pack of them. As if a boy who had nobody could make himself into many.

He never told anyone. A name was the only thing in the world that was his, and he'd already learned that the people of Hilat al-Sarab took things.

He told Old Bakhit, years later. And me, of course. Everyone tells the dog.

He sat on the crest in the cold, and he looked out at the dark, and because he was fifteen and lonely and had been called a monster in the square by a boy with wet eyes, and had answered *I don't know*, and because the only thing in the world that was truly his was a name nobody ever said, he said it.

Very quietly. To the wind. The way you'd say it to someone you wanted to know you.

"Theyab."

The wind took it.

Gently. Too gently, the way a thief's hand is gentle. It lifted the word off his lips and carried it away south and east over the cold ridges, toward the deep sands, toward the dark.

I was at the foot of the dune. I smelled the wind change.

It came back. Over the crest from the south, much colder than any night in the sands has a right to be. And it smelled of nothing. Wind out there always smells of something: camel, salt, the smoke of somebody's far fire, the dust of the next valley. This smelled of nothing at all. It was like putting your nose into an empty jar.

It brushed past his ear.

And in it, very faint, very far, something said:

*"Theyab."*

In his voice. His own exact voice. Only emptied out, the way a water-skin is empty, the way a tent is empty after the funeral meal has been cleared away. Said slowly, carefully, the way you'd say a word in a language you don't speak, to see how it sits in the mouth.

He went still. All of him.

Then he saw the line.

Far out, where the dark gold of the dunes met the black of the sky, there was a band of grey. Low and flat, like the mist that lies on the coastal salt flats on winter mornings, except that the salt flats were four days' ride away, and it wasn't winter, and it wasn't morning. It hadn't been there the night before. It hadn't been there in eight years of nights. It wasn't moving fast.

It didn't have an edge. The dunes ran into it and went on being dunes for a while, paler, flatter, as if they were forgetting how.

And above it, the stars were going out.

Not dimming. Going out. One at a time, low on the sky, the way lamps go out in a house when someone walks from room to room pinching the wicks. Al-Thurayya was rising, and as he watched, one of the sisters went out. And then another.

He understood, with the cold sure understanding you only have in nightmares, that the grey wasn't coming toward the village.

It was coming toward the place where his name had been said.

The feather on his chest began, faintly, to burn. He put his hand over it. And behind my eye, the silver one, the ache I'd carried home from the ruins woke up and turned over and began, slowly, to push.

He didn't see me at the foot of the dune. He didn't see me standing with my legs braced and every hair on my body lying flat. Not a hackle. Not one. Everything about that moment was true, and I've never hated my own nose so much.

I put my claw to the cold sand.

I scratched a stroke. And another. One for each of them, the way I always do, the way I've done for longer than there have been forts on the coast. One for each child I've walked beside up a dune under a full moon. The marks went down in a row in the starlight, small and neat, and when I'd finished there were twelve.

Twelve.

I counted them again. I've always been good at counting. Better than you'd think, for a dog.

Then I lay down on top of them, before he could come down the slope and see, and I put my chin on my paws, and I looked out at the grey with my silver eye aching, and I didn't move until he came.
