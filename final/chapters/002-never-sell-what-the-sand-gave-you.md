# Chapter Two
## Never Sell What the Sand Gave You

> *"A trader's scale has two pans, and his thumb is the third."*
> — a saying of the Liwa market

---

He didn't sleep again after the words.

He sat against the tent pole and said them over in a whisper, trying to hold them the way you try to hold a dream after waking. By the time the light came grey under the flap he had two of them left. By the time the doves started in the palms he had none.

"It was nothing," he told me. "A dream-word. Everyone says dream-words."

I kept my hackles flat. He looked at them a while; he read my back the way the herders read the sky. Flat meant he believed it. Flat didn't mean it was true. He knew the difference. He just didn't want to know it that morning.

Then he took the thaler out of his fist and looked at it in the grey light, and the words were gone for good.

That's what silver is for, little one. Making you forget the thing you should be remembering.

---

Ghanim's stall stood at the corner of the square where the lane from the well came in: a roof of palm fronds on four poles, a mat, a strongbox with a lock the size of a fist, and a pair of brass scales that shone like the only honest thing in the village. They weren't. They had a thumb in them. Ghanim had been getting slowly rich on that thumb for twenty years.

He was a narrow man with a well-oiled beard and very clean hands, in a village where everyone's hands were cracked from rope and dough and goat. He rubbed them with sesame oil at night, and under the sesame they smelled of silver, which is a smell like a cold tongue.

Theyab put his sack on the mat.

Weeks of it. Bent copper from the ruins, a bronze ring green as a lizard, the brass lip of an old lamp, a handful of beads. He laid it out piece by piece. He thought if Ghanim could see it all at once he might be ashamed to cheat it.

Ghanim didn't look at the copper. He looked at Theyab's left hand, which was closed.

"What's in the fist, Laqeet?"

Any other boy in the world would have said *nothing*, and kept what the sand had given him.

He couldn't.

You need to know this now, because it's the hinge of everything. He could not lie. Not *would* not. *Could* not. When a lie got as far as his tongue, the mark on his chest, the small gold feather under his collarbone, would burn like a coal from the coffee hearth pressed into the skin and held. Once, at nine, he'd tried to force a lie past the pain to save himself a beating, and he'd woken face down in the dust with the beating already done and a blister on his chest the exact shape of a feather.

So he didn't say *nothing*.

He said, "Silver," and opened his fingers just enough to show the edge of it.

Ghanim's eyebrows went up. They did half his lying for him.

"Let me see."

Theyab put the thaler on the mat. Ghanim picked it up between two oiled fingers, turned it, bit it, rang it on the edge of the strongbox, held it up to the light. The fat-chinned queen looked out at the square with her foreign indifference.

"Well," said Ghanim. "Well, well."

He put it down on his own side of the mat.

Then he weighed the copper. His hand rested on the crossbar the way a man's hand rests on his knee, and his thumb moved about the width of a grain of barley, and the copper lost a third of what it weighed.

"Copper's poor this month," said Ghanim. "Too many scavengers."

There was exactly one scavenger in Hilat al-Sarab, and he was standing in front of Ghanim's mat.

The lie came off the trader sour and quick, like breath off a man who has eaten onions. The growl came up in my chest. Ghanim's eyes went to me, and away.

"Keep that animal off my mat."

"He won't touch it," Theyab said, which was true. He put his hand on my neck anyway. The growl went back down where it came from, but it didn't go far.

Ghanim counted out annas, half of what the copper was worth, and then a little less than half, and then he said, "And the silver I'll keep for you."

Theyab looked at him.

"For safekeeping," said Ghanim kindly. "A boy like you, with a thaler? Somebody'll say you stole it. Somebody'll beat you for it and take it anyway, and then where are you? I'll keep it safe. When you need it, you come to me. It's yours. It's always yours."

My hackles rose so hard they hurt. Every word of that last part stank. I growled out loud.

Theyab looked at my hackles and at Ghanim's oiled face and said, very quietly, the only thing he could say:

"Will you give it back?"

"Of course," said Ghanim.

I barked. Once, sharp, right in his face. Ghanim jerked back and the scales swung.

"I said keep that thing off my mat!"

And Theyab said nothing. He couldn't fight a grown man in the square for a coin and stay in Hilat al-Sarab afterward. He had nowhere else. Ghanim had built his whole life out of knowing when people had nowhere else to go.

"Hold out your hand," said Ghanim, and lifted the annas. "Come on. Both hands, I'm not dropping my coppers in the dirt."

Theyab held out his right hand.

"Both," said Ghanim. "Make a bowl."

Theyab hesitated. I felt it; I was leaning on his leg. Then he brought his left hand up beside his right. And to make the bowl, he opened it.

Ghanim poured the annas.

He poured them too slowly, chink, chink, chink, and his eyes weren't on the coins. They were on the left palm, where the line ran under the copper. One straight crease from one edge of the hand to the other, unbroken, the kind of line a midwife sees and goes quiet about.

I saw his eyes find it. I saw them stop.

He said nothing.

He finished pouring. And then he smiled. A small smile that smelled of nothing at all, which was the worst thing about it. A lie I can smell. A smile like that is just a door closing quietly in a house where you didn't know anyone was home.

"Go on, then," said Ghanim, and he took a date from the bowl at his elbow, a good fat one, and held it out. "For the walk."

Theyab stared at the date. In eight years Ghanim had never once given him anything.

He took it. He was always hungry. And he walked away across the square and didn't look back, and so he didn't see what I saw, which was Ghanim picking up the thaler and holding it, not to the light this time, but to his nose. Sniffing it. The way you'd sniff a cut of meat to see where it had been.

I bared my teeth at him across the square.

He smiled again, and put the silver in his sleeve.

---

Ghanim followed us that noon.

I smelled him before we were halfway to the ruins: sesame oil on the hot wind, and silver, and the sweat of a man who doesn't walk much. A man in a clean white dishdasha in the full blaze of noon, where no grown man in Hilat al-Sarab would go if you paid him. Ghanim had a box of thalers and a strong opinion about the Noon Mare. He'd decided the first was worth the second.

I let him come. I wanted to see what the trader would do. That was a mistake, and I'll own it.

Theyab went down into the room where he'd found the thaler and began to dig again. I lay at the top of the steps. After a while, behind me, came the crunch of Ghanim's sandals on the old falaj channel.

Then he stopped crunching.

Then he said, under his breath, "Oh, you filthy thing."

I was up and round the corner of the wall before Theyab had lifted his head.

Ghanim was in the dry channel with his back to the stone and a dead arak branch in his hands, and in front of him, coiled in the blown sand, there was a snake.

It was pale. Not the sand-coloured horned viper that every child in Liwa learns to fear. Pale the way milk is pale, smooth and long, in a loose coil with its head lifted a hand's width off the sand. It wasn't hissing. It was simply looking at him, with its head on one side, the way you'd look at a man who'd come into your tent without asking.

Its eyes were the colour of honey held to a lamp.

My hackles didn't move. Not one hair.

"Get back, Laqeet," said Ghanim, as Theyab came round the wall behind me. "It's a viper. Get back. I'll break its back." He lifted the stick.

"It's not a viper."

"Every snake's a viper." Ghanim was sweating. Silver sweat, sour. He'd come out here at noon to find where the thalers came from, and the sand had sent him a snake instead, and he didn't like being looked at. "They're all from below. Everyone knows that. Kill the snake you see, or it'll come back for you at night."

He swung.

Theyab stepped in between.

He didn't think. He moved the way a boy moves to catch a falling cup, and the stick that was meant for the snake's back came down on his shoulder instead. It made a sound like a stick hitting a sack of grain. He staggered. He didn't go down.

Ghanim stared. "Are you mad?"

Theyab turned his back on him and crouched in the channel face to face with the snake, close enough that if it had wanted his eye it could have had it. His shoulder was bleeding through his shirt. He didn't look at it.

And he did what the old people do.

When the old people of the sands find a snake in the house, they don't kill it first. You never know, they say, who's wearing the snake. So you tell it to go, three times, politely, the way you'd tell a guest who's outstayed his welcome. If it goes, no blood is between you. If it stays after the third time, you may do what you must, and whatever was wearing it has no complaint.

Nobody in Hilat al-Sarab ever taught him that. Perhaps he'd always known.

"Go from this place," he said to the snake. Low and clear. "We don't want harm with you. Go, and go safely."

The snake looked at him.

Ghanim made a disgusted sound. "Talking to vermin. Like calls to like, I suppose."

"Go from this place," Theyab said again. "We don't want harm with you. Go, and go safely."

The pale head swayed. Its tongue tasted the air very near his face. I stood behind him and didn't move. I was holding my breath, the way the old man tells the children to hold theirs, and for the same reason. Something was being decided.

"Go from this place," Theyab said a third time. "We don't want harm with you. Go, and go safely."

And it went.

Not like a snake flees. It uncoiled the way silk is drawn off a spool and slid down the channel, and at the corner where the old falaj ran under a fallen lintel it stopped, and turned its head, and looked back. Not at Ghanim. Not at me. At him. For a long moment.

Then it was gone into the dark under the lintel, and the noon closed over the place where it had been as if it had never been there at all.

The air smelled, very faintly, of milk.

I sat down very suddenly in the hot sand. My legs didn't want to hold me.

"Mad," said Ghanim. He threw the stick down. "Mad as a goat in a well. You'll be bitten in your sleep and good riddance." He looked round at the ruins, at the shut rooms and the half-buried walls and the dark under the lintel where the snake had gone. Then he looked at Theyab, crouched in the channel with his bleeding shoulder, and the look changed. "Is this where you found it? The silver?"

Theyab stood up.

He could have said nothing. He could have walked away. But Ghanim had asked him a straight question, and Theyab's mouth only had one kind of answer in it.

"Yes," he said.

Ghanim smiled.

He went back across the dunes with his dishdasha dark with sweat, walking fast, the way you walk when you have someone to tell.

He never came back to dig. A man like Ghanim, shown where silver lies, and never once with a spade? He'd found something better than silver that morning, in a boy's open hand. He didn't need the ruins. He only needed a buyer.

---

The bundle was on the wall that evening.

A low mud wall at the back of the lane, where nobody went but goats and the boy and the dog. Every few days a square of faded indigo cloth appeared on it, knotted at the corners, with flatbread inside still warm from the baking plate, and on good days a twist of waxed paper with four dates soaked dark in sidr honey from the mountains.

It was a good day. He tore the bread in two and gave me half. He always did that. The dates he saved for the hardest days, as if sweetness could be stored the way you store water against a drought.

He didn't know who left the bundles. When he was small he'd decided it must be the One who made the sands, who had made a mistake with him and left bread to say sorry.

I knew. I'd followed the smell of that bread back to its oven the very first morning, while he was still crying in a language no one spoke.

---

Umm Hanan baked before the doves woke.

Her house was the last on the lane behind the well, with an indigo door her husband had painted the year they married, the deep blue that keeps the eye off a house. In a yard no bigger than a tent she had a clay oven and an iron plate on three stones, and every morning in the dark she knelt in front of a wide basin of dough and worked. She was small and bent, with a face as creased as a dry wadi bed and a black abaya gone brown at the hem with flour. She'd lived alone a long time.

Years before the boy ever came up out of the noon, her husband had gone down to the coast for the pearling season, and her two sons with him, the elder as a diver, the younger to haul the rope. In the autumn the boats came home, sails down, men singing, little leather bags of pearls tied at their belts. Her men's boat came home too. Her men were not on it. The rest of the crew were, every one, with their eyes on the deck. Nobody ever told her properly what happened. Or rather, somebody did, and she never repeated it, and I'll come to what it was.

Every morning since, kneading the dough, she said their names.

Three names, in the same order, under her breath, in time with the heels of her hands. Pushed down into the dough the way you'd push salt in. As if, so long as she put them into the bread, they were still being fed. As if bread were a rope you could let down into the sea.

I won't tell you their names. I heard them every morning for eight years, lying in the dark lane outside her yard, and I could say them now, in my head, in her voice. I won't give them to you. Names are not mine to give. And I've seen what the wind does with names that people say carelessly. I won't be the one.

She kneaded. She said them. She shaped the loaves and slapped them onto the plate, and when the first water boiled in her blackened pot for the morning's tea, she did a thing I've never seen another person in Hilat al-Sarab do so faithfully. Before she poured the boiling water out on the ground to rinse the pot, she bent and spoke over it. She named the Creator, quietly, and told whatever might be lying under the earth there to move aside. Every morning. Every single time. In case anything was sleeping under her yard that a splash of boiling water would hurt.

I used to wonder why she bothered. I don't now. I'll tell you why soon enough.

Then, every few days, she'd wrap a loaf in indigo cloth, and take four dates out of the honey jar that cost her more than she had, and go out the back way in the dark and put the bundle on the wall where the foundling went. She never once spoke to him in the square or stood up for him when the boys threw stones. She was old and alone and frightened, and in Hilat al-Sarab kindness out loud could cost a widow everything she had left. So she was kind quietly. Before the doves. For eight years. With nobody ever thanking her.

She did it the way you drop a date-stone in wet ground and walk on, not even thinking of a tree.

The world is held together by people like Umm Hanan, little one. Too frightened to be brave. Good anyway, in small ways, in the dark.

---

The well stood in the middle of the square, a ring of old stone under a wooden frame with a pulley that shrieked. Saqi the well-keeper sat beside it on a palm-rib stool from first light to dusk with a stick across his knees, and every family paid him for every bucket, and the ones who couldn't pay waited till he wasn't looking.

We waited in the shade at the corner of the square that evening, with the dates saved in Theyab's shirt. The sun was going down red behind the palms. Saqi was a big, sour man with a beard going grey in streaks and a voice like the pulley. His wife had died in childbirth seven years before, and since then he'd had two sons and a rope, and he treated all three about the same.

The two sons were at the well now.

Nadim was the elder. Fifteen, like Theyab, and broad where Theyab was narrow, quick in the tongue where Theyab was slow. In a village where every drop came up on your father's rope, that made you nearly a sheikh's son. He sat on the stone lip of the well with his legs dangling and three other boys round him like flies round a date.

And the younger one, Shibl, was sitting in the dust at his feet.

Seven years old. Small for it, with a head too big for his neck and enormous dark eyes, and ears that stuck out like the handles on a water-jar. He had a piece of string and was doing something very serious with it. Lion cub, his name means. He looked about as much like a lion as a dove does.

He saw me.

Not Theyab. Me. And his face did a thing faces almost never did when they saw me: it opened. He came across the square in that straight, unbothered way small children walk toward things they want, and stopped an arm's length from me and stared.

At my eye. The silver one.

"It's like money," he said. "His eye. It's like a coin. Like a coin in the well."

Theyab didn't say anything. People didn't usually talk to him. He didn't know where to put his face.

"Can I touch him?"

"He doesn't bite," said Theyab. True. Mostly.

Shibl put out his hand, sticky, smelling of dates and goat, and laid it between my ears the way you'd touch a sleeping baby, and leaned in until his nose nearly touched mine and looked straight into the silver.

I let him.

I never let anyone look into that eye. But I felt his small breath on my face, and something in me lay down and turned over and showed its belly to a seven-year-old boy with jug ears, and I've never got it back up.

"There's something in it," Shibl whispered. "Like a lamp. Far down."

My silver eye gave one small throb.

"Shibl!"

Nadim was off the well and across the square. He grabbed his brother by the back of the shirt and hauled him away so hard the boy's feet left the ground.

"Don't touch that. Don't you touch that. It's *his*." He put Shibl behind him. "You want a curse on you? You want to wake up with your eyes gone silver?"

"It's just a dog," said Shibl, struggling.

"It's not *just* anything." Nadim looked at Theyab, and his face changed. It got easier, the way a man's face gets easier when he's found a thing he knows how to do. "Look who's come to the well. Thirsty, Laqeet?"

Theyab said, "Yes."

The boys at the well laughed. That was how it started. It always started like that. Nadim had discovered, years before, the thing about Theyab that the rest of the village never bothered to notice: that he couldn't lie. And Nadim had made a sport of it.

"Got any money for my father's water?"

"No."

"Did Ghanim cheat you again?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Half."

They laughed harder. Saqi didn't stop them.

"Do you cry at night, Laqeet?"

Theyab's jaw went tight. I felt it, the struggle in him, the way you feel a camel straining against its hobble. He wanted to say *no*. I watched his hand go to his chest, flat, over the shirt, over the mark. The burn starting.

"Yes," he said.

The boys howled.

"What do you cry about?"

"I don't know."

"Who loves you, Laqeet?" Nadim was grinning now, enjoying it, his face flushed. "Go on. Somebody must. Who loves you?"

And Theyab looked at me.

He didn't say it. He didn't have to. He looked down at me, sitting by his leg in the dust, and the boys followed his eyes, and the laugh that went up off the well was the worst I'd heard in eight years. One of them barked. Another got down on his hands and knees and whined. Even Saqi snorted into his beard.

Shibl didn't laugh. Shibl stood behind his brother and looked at Theyab with his enormous eyes, and something in his small face was working, slowly, like a boy doing sums.

Nadim held up a hand. The boys quieted. He had one more. I could see it coming the way you see weather.

"Last one," he said. "And you have to tell the truth. You always have to tell the truth." He stepped close. "Are you a monster?"

The square went quiet.

The red light behind the palms. The women in the doorways, who had stopped pretending not to listen. And Theyab, with one hand on his chest and the left one closed tight at his side, looking for the true answer so he could say it. Because he couldn't say anything else.

He didn't say *no*.

He couldn't. He lay awake on dark nights with the feather glowing under his shirt, pulsing in time with something that wasn't his heart. He talked in his sleep in a language no one spoke. He had come out of the noon the way the Noon Mare comes out of the noon. And so the only true answer was the one that would hurt him most.

"I don't know," he said.

Nobody laughed at that one.

Nadim's face went odd. He'd wanted to laugh at *no*. *I don't know* was a different animal, and it had come into the square and lain down among them, and nobody wanted to look at it.

"Monster," said Nadim, but quietly, and not to anyone.

Then Saqi was off his stool and cuffed Nadim across the back of the head so hard the boy staggered. "Fetch the goats. I'm not feeding you to stand about." He hit him again, across the ear, for nothing, for being there. "Go on."

Nadim went. I saw his face as he passed us, red down one side, his eyes wet, looking at the ground exactly the way Theyab looked at the ground.

I smelled no lie on him. Only a boy, and a bruise.

People are rarely only one thing, little one. Remember that. You'll need it later, when you want to hate him.

Saqi went back to his stool. After a while, without a word, he drew a bucket and set it on the lip of the well, full, and turned his back on it.

Theyab drank.

---

He went up the big dune after dark.

It was the highest one near the village, a long red ridge to the south. He said the sky was bigger from up there. I think the truth was that the village was smaller.

The cold comes fast in the sands, the minute the sun is gone. The stars were out so thick the sky looked like black cloth spilled with salt, and al-Thurayya was climbing in the east, the little knot the herders call the sisters. He sat with his arms round his knees and the honey-dates still in his shirt, his shoulder aching where the stick had caught it.

Nobody had ever given him a name but *Laqeet*. So he'd given himself one.

He was seven, that first year, sitting alone on the lip of the well at dusk, and a word came into his mouth the way the strange words came in his sleep, already shaped, as if someone had left it there for him to find. *Theyab.* Wolves. The plural, not the singular. Not *a wolf*. A pack of them. As if a boy who had nobody could make himself into many.

He told no one but Old Bakhit, years later. And me. Everyone tells the dog.

And because he was fifteen and lonely and had answered *I don't know* in the square, and because the only thing in the world that was truly his was a name nobody ever said, he said it.

Very quietly. To the wind. The way you'd say it to someone you wanted to know you.

"Theyab."

The wind took it.

Gently. Too gently, the way a thief's hand is gentle. It lifted the word off his lips and carried it away south and east over the cold ridges, toward the deep sands, toward the dark.

I was at the foot of the dune. I smelled the wind change.

It came back over the crest from the south, much colder than any night in the sands has a right to be. And it smelled of nothing. Wind out there always smells of something: camel, salt, somebody's far fire. This was like putting your nose into an empty jar.

It brushed past his ear.

And in it, very faint, very far, something said:

*"Theyab."*

In his voice. His own exact voice. Only emptied out, the way a water-skin is empty, the way a tent is empty after the funeral meal has been cleared away. Said slowly, carefully, the way you'd say a word in a language you don't speak, to see how it sits in the mouth.

He went still. All of him.

Then he saw the line.

Far out, where the dunes met the black of the sky, there was a band of grey. Low and flat, like the mist on the coastal salt flats on winter mornings, except that the salt flats were four days' ride away. It hadn't been there in eight years of nights. It wasn't moving fast.

It didn't have an edge. The dunes ran into it and went on being dunes for a while, paler, flatter, as if they were forgetting how.

And above it, the stars were going out.

Not dimming. Going out. One at a time, low on the sky, the way lamps go out in a house when someone walks from room to room pinching the wicks. Al-Thurayya was rising, and as he watched, one of the sisters went out. And then another.

He understood, with the cold sure understanding you only have in nightmares, that the grey wasn't coming toward the village.

It was coming toward the place where his name had been said.

The feather on his chest began, faintly, to burn. He put his hand over it. And behind my eye, the silver one, the ache I'd carried home from the ruins woke up and turned over and began, slowly, to push.

He didn't see me at the foot of the dune with every hair on my body lying flat. Everything about that moment was true, and I've never hated my own nose so much.

I put my claw to the cold sand.

I scratched a stroke. And another. One for each child I've walked beside up a dune under a full moon, the way I've done for longer than there have been forts on the coast. The marks went down in a row in the starlight, small and neat, and when I'd finished there were twelve.

Twelve.

I counted them again. I've always been good at counting. Better than you'd think, for a dog.

Then I lay down on top of them, before he could come down the slope and see, and I put my chin on my paws, and I looked out at the grey with my silver eye aching, and I didn't move until he came.

*End of Chapter Two*
