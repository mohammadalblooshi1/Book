# *Never Say Your Name to the Wind*: The 150-Chapter Outline

*They say it truly happened.*

This outline follows `architecture-150.md` exactly: ten parts of 15 chapters, with every twist landing in the part the architecture gives it. It draws on `final-direction.md`, `../desert-jinn/jinn-additions.md`, `../desert-jinn/folklore-additions.md`, `../desert-jinn/direction.md` and `../research/book-study.md`. The best scenes of the 20-chapter Desert Jinn Edition are reused and marked *(base Ch N)*.

## How to read this outline
- **Title:** a warning. All 150 titles are different. The 20 titles from `final-direction.md` sit at the matching story points.
- **Epigraph:** an original saying, from a Bedouin, a grandmother or a guide. No religious text.
- **Beats:** 4–7 per chapter. **Hook:** the wound or hook the chapter closes on.
- **Tags:** [TALE] a tale within the tale · [FIRE] a frame scene (italic, 300–700 words) · [TWIST-Tn seed/reveal] · [UNREAL] an unreliable-reality device · [ACTION] a chapter driven by action.
- **The narrator:** Ghareeb, the saluki, speaking to "little one". When a chapter is reconstructed from Theyab's own later account, the beats say so.
- **The hidden message** (kindness returns, and so do cruelty and greed) is never stated. The beats mark each kindness with ⟲ when it is done and ⟲⟲ when it comes back, for the writers only. Those marks never appear in the prose.

## Standing devices added from the author's late notes
- **Sirat Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan** (the author's reference book #1). (1) Old Nadim's fire openings use the rhythms of the epic reciter: formulas like "The teller of this tale said…" and "And so it was, O listeners…", a rhymed line now and then, and a cliffhanger break ("and we will come to that, if the fire allows"). (2) **Jinn bound to objects:** an *'amir* house-jinn bound in Old Bakhit's truth bell (that is why it rings at lies), the jinn sister of Bahla bound by a sorcerer's ring, and the Ifrit in the brass vessel. (3) **A jinn princess ally:** the Younger Sister of Bahla, the jinn who dug its falaj. She never tells her name (Ch 50–53, 131, 139). (4) **A mother who tries to kill her son:** Jasir's mother (Ch 33), told in the epic's manner.
- **Classical jinn taxonomy** (a placeholder until `research/book-study.md` Part 4 exists; it did not exist when this outline was written). These terms sit alongside the four elemental kinds: the ***'amir*** (a house- and object-dwelling jinn: Bakhit's bell, the well at Bir al-Ghina'); the ***marid*** (the strongest and oldest: **Bu Darya** is the marid of the drowned sea under the sabkha); the ***ifrit*** (the thing under Ubar); the ***shaytan*** (the whisperer: the qareen's nature); from al-Qazwini, the ***ghul***, the ***si'lat*** (a kin of Hmarat al-Qayla and Umm al-Duwais), the ***nasnas*** (half-men in Wadi al-Sada and the ruins of Ubar) and the ***shiqq*** (the half-jinn that fathers the nasnas); the ***hatif*** (an unseen voice that calls in the empty desert); and, after Ibn Shuhayd, the **companion jinn of poets** (the Sand Singer's tale and Hazza''s verses). Hilal explains these in Ch 10 and Ch 96. Each line marked ⟨P4⟩ is to be checked against Part 4.
- **Umm al-Duwais and Bu Darya are major recurring presences.** Umm al-Duwais appears in Ch 29, 60, 65, 68, 69 [TALE], 112, 115, 127, 130, 139 and 150. Bu Darya appears in Ch 2–3 (Umm Hanan's drowned men), 7, 32–33, 77, 80 [TALE], 84, 117, 128 and 139. Hmarat al-Qayla appears in Ch 1, 3 [TALE], 11 and 109. Abu Fanous appears in Ch 21 and 104. Umm al-Sibyan appears in Ch 148–149.

# PART I: THE MIRAGE VILLAGE (Chapters 1–15)

### 1. Never Walk at Noon
*"Come in from the sun, or Hmarat al-Qayla will take you." — what the mothers of Hilat al-Sarab call at noon*
- [FIRE] An italic prologue at a winter camp in Liwa (*base Ch 1*). An old man with a stammer pours the coffee himself and spills it: "You'll say it's a tale. I tell you it happened. I was there." An ancient saluki lies at his feet. One eye is amber. The other lid is sewn shut by old scar tissue, and the children say something under it catches the light. He will tell it "the way the dog would tell it." There are eleven children.
- The flash-forward: a boy burning on Tal Moreeb under the full moon, smelling of honey, hot metal and hair. "But that is the end."
- Ghareeb introduces himself: a saluki, impossibly old, who cannot speak to humans and growls at lies. He has watched over eleven children before this one. (He believes that number.)
- Noon in Hilat al-Sarab. Every mother calls her children in by Hmarat al-Qayla's name. No one calls Theyab, the foundling they call Laqeet. He scavenges the half-buried ruins in the white heat, and Ghareeb shadows him.
- In the ruins Theyab finds a Maria Theresa thaler. He keeps his left fist closed the way he always has, over the line across his palm. Ghareeb's silver eye aches. He doesn't know why. It always does when Theyab is in danger.
- **Hook:** In the noon-empty lane, round cups pressed into the sand two by two, hoofprints, circle the hollow where Theyab sleeps and stop at his blanket.

### 2. Never Sell What the Sand Gave You
*"A trader's scale has two pans, and his thumb is the third." — a saying of the Liwa market*
- Ghanim the trader weighs Theyab's scraps on crooked scales and pays him half. He pockets the thaler "for safekeeping." ⟲ (His greed will come back to him.)
- Umm Hanan's secret kindness: bread and honey-dates left on the wall where only a boy who looks for them would find them ⟲. She lost her husband and two sons when the pearling boats came home without them. She kneads their three names into her dough every morning.
- Nadim, Saqi the well-keeper's son, bullies Theyab in front of the well. His little brother Shibl, seven years old, is fascinated by the dog with the coin-coloured eye.
- Theyab cannot lie. A small cruel scene shows it: Nadim makes him answer questions in front of the other boys, and every true answer is a humiliation.
- That night, alone on the highest dune, Theyab whispers the name he gave himself, *Theyab*, "wolves", the one name nobody ever gave him.
- **Hook:** The wind says it back to him. On the horizon a flat grey line rises, and the stars above it go out one by one. Ghareeb scratches *twelve* into the sand and lies down on the marks.

### 3. Don't Let the Old Women Frighten the Children
*"The noon has teeth, and the teeth have hooves." — the grandmothers of Liwa*
- [TALE] Umm Hanan tells Shibl and two other children the tale of Hmarat al-Qayla to keep them in at noon. Theyab listens through the palm-frond wall. It is her own story: the daughter called Hanan, the name she still answers to, whom she let play outside one noon forty years ago. She found only small hoofprints and one sandal, warm as bread.
- The tale's horror lies in its rules. The donkey-woman speaks in your mother's voice, she only takes the children no one is calling, and she never takes them far, so you can hear them for days.
- Theyab understands the tale is about him. He is the child no one calls.
- Umm Hanan's sea-grief: her men were "called off the boat by a voice." The first, glancing mention of Bu Darya.
- **Hook:** The next noon Shibl is missing from his mat. Theyab finds him in the empty lane, smiling, walking toward a woman standing in the heat-shimmer with her arms out. Under her abaya the legs are wrong. Theyab carries Shibl home, and no one ever believes how it happened.

### 4. Never Tell the Wind Who You Are
*"Say your name to the wind and the wind will find someone to sell it to." — a herders' saying*
- Before dawn Theyab bangs on Sheikh Mazyad's door (*base Ch 2*). He gets "Sea fog. Dust." Ghareeb smells fear coming off the sheikh, the lie's older brother. "Go back to your tent, Laqeet."
- Old Bakhit, the blind healer at the western edge, calls Theyab into his tent, which is hung with herbs and bells. He already knows. His brass bell rings once when Theyab says "I'm not afraid."
- Bakhit warns him of the four things never to do: walk at noon, answer the grey, follow a light, say your name to the wind. "Which have you done?" Theyab can't lie.
- Bakhit touches the feather mark on Theyab's chest and goes grey in the face. He says nothing more. He sends Theyab away with a bitter green tea Theyab hates and drinks to the dregs.
- **Hook:** Walking home, Theyab hears his name in the hiss of the dunes, in his own voice, a step behind him. Ghareeb turns, and there is nothing, but the hackles along his spine have risen for something he can't see.

### 5. Never Forget the Names You Knead Into Bread
*"What you say every morning is a wall. Stop saying it and see what comes in." — Umm Hanan*
- The grey comes the way cold comes into a tent (*base Ch 2*). Umm Hanan's indigo door goes the colour of ash. Kneading, she says her husband's name and her eldest son's name. For the third: "——".
- The device starts here. The blank is printed in the text, and Ghareeb tries and fails to say the name.
- By noon the eastern lanes have lost colour, sound and smell. A goat stands bleating with no sound. The red cloths on the drying lines are grey.
- The village argues in the majlis about whether the strange dog is a jinn (Frankenstein in Baghdad). Mazyad will not decide. Ghanim says the dog should be sold to the coast.
- **Hook:** At dusk the first emptied villager stands at the well, smiling, repeating his last words: *"Bring the bucket, the bucket, bring the bucket."* Then the woman beside him starts saying them too.

### 6. Never Answer When the Grey Calls
*"If the dark calls you by name, it learned it from someone you trusted." — a grandmother's warning*
- Night. The grey calls from the eastern lanes in the voices of the dead and the absent. It uses the drowned voices of Umm Hanan's sons, as if the sea and the grey were sharing them (Bu Darya's voices carried inland).
- Umm Hanan steps toward her younger son's voice. Theyab holds her back with both arms. She hits him, and then she thanks him without words, with her hand flat on his cheek.
- A herdsman answers his dead wife, walks into the grey and comes back emptied. The grey now speaks in his voice too: a monster made of the community.
- Ghareeb walks into the grey on purpose to draw it off, and walks out again. The whole village sees. "The dog goes in and out." It settles nothing, and it makes everything worse.
- The first list of the emptied's last words: eleven lines, one per villager, in Ghareeb's flat voice.
- **Hook:** At the well, Saqi the well-keeper is lowering a bucket after dark. He hums to the water, and the water hums back.

### 7. Never Lower a Bucket After Dark
*"Name the Maker before you pour, or something under the ground will thank you for the drink." — what the women say at the well*
- From Nadim's side, as Ghareeb watches: Saqi has drawn water at night without naming the Creator. Something from the well, an *'amir* of wells ⟨P4⟩, comes up the rope.
- Saqi turns gentle and strange. He is kind to his sons for the first time in years, and that is the horror: Nadim loves the jinn's version of his father.
- Shibl tells Theyab at the wall: "Baba talks to the water now. It talks back with Mama's voice." Their mother is dead.
- Ghareeb smells it on Saqi: smokeless fire and wet stone. His silver eye throbs.
- **Hook:** At midnight Saqi stands over Shibl's sleeping mat. His eyes have rolled white, and he says, in a doubled voice, "This one is light enough to carry."

### 9. Always Let the Mutawwa' In
*"A guest who comes reading is better than a son who comes armed." — a saying of the majlis*

### 10. Never Name a Jinn You Haven't Met
*"There are four winds, four waters and four kinds of the hidden ones, and none of them is your friend." — Mutawwa' Hilal*
- Hilal stays three days, the guest-right. He teaches Theyab under the ghaf tree: the four kinds (fire, wind, sea, land), their powers and weaknesses, and the classical names ⟨P4⟩ (*'amir, marid, ifrit, shaytan, ghul, si'lat, nasnas*, the *hatif* voice).
- He explains the malboos ("the victim is still in there") and the qareen, the companion jinn born with every person. "Most sleep. Pray yours does."
- Hilal reads over the village's water. For a day colour returns to the well-court ⟲, and the village loves him for it and goes on hating Theyab.
- Hilal shows Theyab how to steady his breath. He also tells him the iron rule: never sleep without iron. Theyab has no iron.
- **Hook:** Hilal must leave for a sick child in Liwa. At the edge of the village he turns back and says, "When they call you monster, boy, remember who said it first." Then he is gone. That same night, Shibl's mat is empty again.

### 15. Never Show Anyone Your Palm
*"Hide the child's hand in your sleeve; there are men who read palms with knives." — what the Liwa mothers whisper over a marked baby*
- Theyab opens his fist. An unbroken line runs straight across the palm. Hilal names it: **zahwi**. **[TWIST-T1 reveal]**
- Hilal explains: sorcerers and jinn crave a zahwi's blood to open sealed and guarded places. Treasure-hunters kidnap such children. "Your mother hid this hand. Someone did."
- Bakhit adds the worst part. The Nameless, who burn their own names to serve the thing under Ubar, will want him twice: for the Light, and as the blood that opens the last seal.
- As Hilal's thumb traces the line, Ghareeb's silver eye burns like a coal. He hides his face in his paws.
- Theyab decides to go into the desert, toward Ghidd al-Zujaj where Bakhit says the old carvings are, rather than bring the hunters to the people who stoned him.

### 17. Never Refuse the Fire Twice
*"Run from your death and it will wait for you at your son's door." — a saying of the old caravan guides*
- [TALE: The Blind One, the ninth bearer] Bakhit's own tale, three hundred years ago. He was a fourteen-year-old herd-boy of the Hajar with the feather on his chest. A shepherd boy with a dog befriended him on the road. The boy was Ghadban's shape, already the Ifrit's mouth. He made running sound like courage.
- Bakhit refused on the dune. The Ifrit "rewarded" him: it took his eyes, and with them every face he loved, and bound him to live until the bearer who would not refuse came. He has outlived three wives and his children's children.
- His horror detail: he still sees one thing in the dark, the inside of the fire he didn't enter.
- The dog that ran beside him then was Ghareeb. Bakhit says Ghareeb did his best and it didn't matter.
- Theyab asks whether refusing was wrong. Bakhit's answer is the only one the bell does not judge: "I don't know. I only know what it cost."
- **Hook:** As Theyab sleeps, Bakhit whispers to the dog: "It saw me through something, that night. Did you ever ask yourself what?" Ghareeb leaves the tent.

### 20. Never Walk With a Stranger Whose Feet You Can't See
*"Strike a ghoul once. If you strike it twice, you've given it back its life." — a saying of the Bedu, told over coffee*
