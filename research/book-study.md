# Part 1: Ten Books

## 1. Frankenstein in Baghdad — Ahmed Saadawi
- **Author / year / origin:** Ahmed Saadawi, Iraq. Arabic original published by Manshurat al-Jamal, Beirut, 2013. English translation by Jonathan Wright (Oneworld, 2018) [S1][S2].
- **Premise:** In US-occupied Baghdad (around 2005), Hadi, a junk dealer, gathers body parts left by bombings and sews them into one corpse so it can be buried properly. The corpse comes alive as "the Whatsitsname" and starts taking revenge for the people it is made from. Each killing uses up a part, so it needs new flesh, and the line between victim and killer blurs [S3].
- **Style & structure:** Many intersecting points of view: Elishva, an old Assyrian Christian widow waiting for a son lost in war; Hadi; Brigadier Majid of a secret "Tracking and Pursuit Department" that uses astrologers; and Mahmoud, a journalist [S3]. The novel is framed by a "Final Report" from a committee that investigates the department and confiscates a writer's manuscript, the book you are reading [S4]. Tone mixes black comedy, satire and horror.
- **Themes:** Collective guilt; violence feeding on itself; justice turned into vengeance; rumour and myth-making; grief.
- **Why it reached the world (verified):** Won the 2014 International Prize for Arabic Fiction (the first Iraqi winner). Shortlisted for the 2018 Man Booker International Prize, the only Arabic title on that list. The French translation won the foreign-novel category of the Grand Prix de l'Imaginaire 2017 [S1][S2][S5].
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **A monster made of the community.** The Whatsitsname is literally the town's dead. Our Ifrit's "grey" could be shown as wearing the faces, voices and last words of the emptied, so every victim becomes part of the threat.
  2. **A documentary frame that casts doubt.** Saadawi's "official report" makes the tale feel real and suspect at once. Our elder's "I was there" can be backed by small "evidence" objects (a burnt name-tablet, a thaler, the saluki itself).
  3. **Folk horror explained by politics.** Saadawi never says if the monster is real; rumour and fear do the work. Let villagers argue about whether Ghareeb is jinn-touched, and never settle it.

## 2. The Ocean at the End of the Lane — Neil Gaiman
- **Author / year / origin:** Neil Gaiman, UK. Published 2013 (Headline UK / William Morrow US) [S6].
- **Premise:** A middle-aged man goes back to his childhood home for a funeral, sits by a farm pond, and remembers what happened when he was seven: an ancient being entered his family's life, and the Hempstock women at the end of the lane fought it. A girl, Lettie, sacrifices herself for him [S7].
- **Style & structure:** Prologue and epilogue in the adult present; eleven untitled chapters of first-person memory between them. Childhood perception is told in a plain, quiet adult voice. The epilogue suggests he has returned and forgotten before [S7].
- **Themes:** Memory and forgetting; a child's helplessness among adults; sacrifice that saves someone who may not deserve it; old powers older than the world.
- **Why it reached the world (verified):** Named Book of the Year at the 2013 British (National) Book Awards; reported sales of more than 1.2 million copies. The National Theatre staged it (Dorfman Theatre, December 2019; adapted by Joel Horwood, directed by Katy Rudd). It moved to the West End in 2021, toured the UK and Ireland, returned to the West End in 2023, and was nominated for the Olivier for Best New Play [S6][S8].
- **Caution for marketing:** In January 2025, after sexual-assault allegations (which Gaiman denies), Dark Horse Comics dropped him and other partners distanced themselves [S9]. Using him as a "comp title" in a pitch is now risky; name the book's techniques, not the author.
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **Frame of an old man remembering childhood.** This is almost exactly our elder frame. Gaiman keeps the frame very short and lets the frame's sadness (what was lost) colour the whole tale. Keep our prologue and epilogue short.
  2. **A sacrifice the survivor must live with.** The narrator owes his life to someone else's sacrifice, which matches Nadim and Theyab. Let old Nadim's guilt show in small physical ways in the frame (he can't look at the fire, he touches the dog).
  3. **Forgetting as horror.** The man has forgotten and keeps re-forgetting. Our curse eats names and memories, so the frame can echo it: the elder must tell the tale aloud or it will be lost.

## 3. The Alchemist — Paulo Coelho
- **Author / year / origin:** Paulo Coelho, Brazil. *O Alquimista*, 1988, first issued by Rocco, a small Brazilian house, with a tiny first print run (sources say about 900 copies) [S10]. First English edition 1993 (HarperCollins / HarperSanFrancisco) [S10].
- **Premise:** Santiago, a young shepherd, dreams of treasure at the Egyptian pyramids, crosses the desert with a caravan, meets an alchemist and learns to read signs, and finds the treasure was where he started.
- **Style & structure:** A fable. Short, simple sentences, a straight quest line, wise-mentor figures, repeated sayings and aphorisms. The circular ending (treasure back at home) is borrowed from an old Nights-type tale of two dreamers (source link **unverified** in this search).
- **Themes:** Following your calling; the world conspiring to help; the desert as teacher; omens.
- **Why it reached the world (verified):** Guinness World Record for most translated book by a living author (counts of 67 languages around 2009, 80 by 2015). Sales are usually cited as 65 million+, with Penguin Random House's author page giving a higher figure [S11][S12].
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **Circular structure.** "The end is the beginning" is our title; Coelho shows how a circle feels like fate. Plant the newborn/cradle in Ch 1 so that Ch 20's return lands as a circle.
  2. **Short, repeatable sayings.** Coelho's lines get quoted and shared. Our existing sayings ("Never say your name to the wind", "May the sand remember you") can be used the same way, repeated at turning points.
  3. **Readable desert for a global reader.** The desert, caravan and oasis are explained through action, never footnotes. Keep jargon light and let context explain *falaj*, *ghaf*, *sabkha*.
  - (Caution: Coelho states his lesson openly. Our hidden message must stay hidden, so borrow the clarity, not the preaching.)

## 4. The Road to Ubar — Nicholas Clapp
- **Author / year / origin:** Nicholas Clapp, US documentary filmmaker. *The Road to Ubar: Finding the Atlantis of the Sands*, Houghton Mifflin, 1998 [S13].
- **Premise:** Non-fiction. Clapp's decades-long hunt for Ubar, the frankincense city that the Quran links to the people of 'Ad and to "Iram of the pillars." He studied old maps and manuscripts (Kirkus notes his first break came from an error in Ptolemy's map) and earlier explorers' notes (Bertram Thomas reported an old track), then brought in NASA radar imagery [S13][S14].
- **Style & structure:** First-person detective story: archive research, dead ends, expedition, dig. Alternates between legend and fieldwork.
- **Themes:** Myth vs. evidence; obsession; the desert hiding and revealing.
- **Why it succeeded (verified):** The 1992 find made world news (NASA/JPL release; NYT; Time) [S15][S16]. The book is still in print (Open Road e-book edition) [S13].

### What was actually found at Shisr (useful real detail)
- **The team:** Clapp, lawyer George Hedges, explorer Ranulph Fiennes and archaeologist Juris Zarins, with JPL scientists Ronald Blom and Charles Elachi [S15][S17].
- **The satellite role:** Space Shuttle radar and Landsat/SPOT images showed old caravan tracks converging on the area, which radar can trace through dry sand. Fiennes said the imagery didn't locate the city itself; it showed they'd been searching in the wrong place [S15][S17].
- **The site:** At Shisr (Shisur), a known Bedouin well, the dig (late 1991 to 1992) found a walled fortress around the well: a limestone wall 2.5–3 m high and about 1 m thick, linking eight or more towers [S17]. It worked as a caravan watering and processing stop for frankincense from the Dhofar (Qara) mountains [S18].
- **The collapse (great for fiction):** Part of the fortress fell into a **sinkhole**: the well sat over a limestone cavern, and the ground under the walls gave way. Clapp suggested an earthquake; other hypotheses blame water being drawn down from the cavern. Dates given range from about AD 100 to 300–500 [S19][S20]. In legend, the city was swallowed by the sands for its people's pride.
- **Disputes:** Many scholars say Shisr is too small and too late to be the legendary city; Zarins himself noted "Ubar" in old sources meant a region and people (the Iobaritae), not one city [S17][S18].
- **Today:** Shisr ("Wubar") is part of UNESCO's **Land of Frankincense** World Heritage Site (inscribed 2000) with Wadi Dawkah (frankincense trees), and the ports of Khor Rori and Al-Baleed [S21].
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **Real archaeology under the legend.** Use real details: a well over a cavern, towers in a ring, a city that dropped into the earth, caravan tracks visible only from above. Our Ubar "with endless tombs beneath" can sit over exactly that sort of limestone void.
  2. **The search story.** Clapp's "slip of the pen in old maps" idea suits Ch 17: Jasir's secret way into Ubar could come from a false or deliberately wrong old route.
  3. **Frankincense as atmosphere.** Smell of burning resin, the trade and the wealth: Ubar's lamps on the Night of Lamps could burn frankincense.

## 5. The Sand Fish — Maha Gargash
- **Author / year / origin:** Maha Gargash, Emirati (born in Dubai). *The Sand Fish: A Novel from Dubai*, HarperCollins, 2009, written in English [S22][S23].
- **Premise:** 1950s, in the area that is now the UAE. Noora, a spirited young woman from a poor mountain family, becomes the third wife of a much older pearl merchant on the coast and fights for freedom inside his household [S22].
- **Style & structure:** Linear, close third-person historical fiction; rich sensory detail of pre-oil life (mountains, coast, pearling).
- **Themes:** A woman's freedom; tradition vs. desire; the hard life before oil.
- **Why it succeeded (verified):** Rare English-language novel by an Emirati published by a major US house. Publishers Weekly reported it held #1 on Magrudy's (UAE bookshop) list for 23 weeks. Kirkus found the plot predictable but the setting original [S23][S24].
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **Pre-oil daily life as the hook.** Gargash's selling point is lived detail. Our 1920s setting is earlier; lean into chores, food, water, smell.
  2. **Women's inner lives inside a male world.** Umm Hanan, Umm Shamma and Hessa can carry this; their kindnesses drive the hidden message.
  3. **Writing in English from the Gulf.** Shows a market for English-language Gulf fiction; useful for pitching.

## 6. A Small Death — Mohammed Hasan Alwan
- **Author / year / origin:** Mohammed Hasan Alwan, Saudi Arabia. *Mawt Saghir*, 2016. English translation by William M. Hutchins as *Ibn Arabi's Small Death* (University of Texas, 2021) [S25][S26].
- **Premise:** A fictional autobiography of the Sufi master Muhyiddin Ibn 'Arabi, from his birth in Muslim Spain (12th century) through travels across North Africa, Arabia and Anatolia to his death in Damascus [S25].
- **Style & structure:** Mainly Ibn 'Arabi's own first-person voice, a long life-journey (about 600 pages in Arabic) [S27]. Reviewers describe the book as slow and contemplative; one ArabLit critic questioned its pacing [S27]. (I could not verify the detail, commonly mentioned, that it also follows the manuscript's journey through later centuries: **unverified**.)
- **Themes:** Spiritual seeking; love; exile; a life as a pilgrimage; "small deaths" of the self.
- **Why it reached the world (verified):** Won the International Prize for Arabic Fiction in 2017 (not 2018) [S25]. A bookseller listing says the English edition was a nominee for the National Book Award for Translated Literature (2022) (**not verified** against the National Book Foundation).
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **A spiritual journey told as an adventure.** Theyab's Light is a gift that must be given up; treat each stage as a "small death."
  2. **Real historical texture around a legendary figure.** Real places and routes make the mystical believable.
  3. **A voice that has lived very long.** Ibn 'Arabi narrates with the weight of old age; Ghareeb, impossibly old, can too.

## 7. The Bleeding of the Stone and Gold Dust — Ibrahim al-Koni
- **Author / year / origin:** Ibrahim al-Koni, Libyan Tuareg; born in the desert and learned Arabic at about twelve [S28]. *The Bleeding of the Stone* (Arabic 1990; English by May Jayyusi and Christopher Tingley, Interlink, about 2002) [S29]. *Gold Dust* (*al-Tibr*, 1990; English by Elliott Colla, 2008, reissued by Hoopoe 2020) [S30].
- **Premises:**
  - *Bleeding of the Stone*: Asouf, a lone herder in the mountains of southern Libya near ancient rock paintings, protects the sacred waddan (mouflon / Barbary sheep) from hunters who want to eat it [S29].
  - *Gold Dust*: Ukhayyad, a young Tuareg, and his piebald Mahri camel. He trades away his wife and gets a bag of gold dust, and the dishonour and blood that follow [S30].
- **Style & structure:** Short, mythic novels. Desert as a spiritual space; animals as soul-companions; Sufi and pre-Islamic Tuareg belief; scenes of talking animals and magic in plain prose [S29].
- **Themes:** The bond between a man and an animal; greed and the outside world destroying the desert; sacrifice; the desert as both paradise and hell.
- **Why it reached the world (verified):** Al-Koni won the Sheikh Zayed Book Award in 2008 [S30]. The Japanese translation of *Gold Dust* won a translation award in 1997 [S30]. He was a finalist for the 2015 Man Booker International Prize (for his body of work) [S31]. (Swiss State Prize and exact language counts: **not verified** here.)
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **The animal as the soul of the story.** Ukhayyad and his camel are like Ghareeb and the bearers. Al-Koni writes the animal with total physical realism and still lets it be mystical.
  2. **Rock art as a door to the past.** Asouf lives beside ancient paintings that seem to come alive. Our Ghidd al-Zujaj rock carvings of the eleven bearers can do the same.
  3. **Greed as the true monster.** Hunters and outsiders, not jinn, do the worst harm. Ghanim the trader and the Nameless can mirror this.

## 8. One Thousand and One Nights (Alf Layla wa-Layla)
- **Origin:** Anonymous collection of Persian, Indian, Arab and Egyptian tales, gathered over centuries. The oldest substantial manuscript is the 14th-century Syrian (Galland) manuscript; Antoine Galland's French version (1704–17) brought it to Europe [S32].
- **Frame structure:** King Shahryar, betrayed, marries a new bride every night and kills her at dawn. Scheherazade tells a story each night and stops at the best moment, so he must keep her alive. Stories nest inside stories (a character inside a tale tells another tale).
- **"The Fisherman and the Jinni":** An old fisherman casts his net four times a day; the fourth catch is a brass jar sealed with the seal of Solomon. Smoke pours out and forms an Ifrit, who says he will kill his rescuer. During his imprisonment he had vowed riches to whoever freed him, but as the centuries passed his vows soured, until he swore death to his rescuer. The fisherman tricks him back into the jar by doubting he could ever fit inside. Nested within it are other tales (e.g., King Yunan and the Sage Duban, a story about ingratitude) [S32].
- **"The City of Brass":** The caliph 'Abd al-Malik hears of jinn sealed by Solomon in brass bottles and sends Musa ibn Nusayr's expedition into the western deserts. They find a jinn chained in a black stone pillar, then a dead city of brass whose walls bear verses about mortality. Men who climb the wall laugh, leap in and die; inside, everyone is dead, including an embalmed queen with wakeful-seeming eyes. It is a meditation on vanity and death [S33].
- **Why it lasts:** Translated into hundreds of editions; the source of "Aladdin", "Sindbad" and "Ali Baba" in world culture.
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **Stories nested in the frame.** Our elder at the fire can, once or twice, have Ghareeb tell a short "tale within the tale" (Ghadban's first refusal, the founding of Ubar), told the way the Nights would.
  2. **The ifrit's turning vow.** A jinn whose gratitude rots into hatred over centuries of imprisonment is a perfect backstory for our Ifrit under Ubar, and an exact mirror of our hidden message (good returns, but so does neglect).
  3. **The City of Brass as Ubar.** A dead city that kills those who look in, warning inscriptions about pride, a preserved ruler: strong images for Ch 17–19 and Sheikh Mutlaq.

## 9. Arabian Nights and Days — Naguib Mahfouz
- **Author / year / origin:** Naguib Mahfouz, Egypt (Nobel Prize in Literature 1988, the first Arabic-language winner) [S34]. *Layali alf layla*, written about 1979, published 1982; English by Denys Johnson-Davies (AUC Press 1993 / Doubleday 1995) [S35].
- **Premise:** Starts where the Nights end: Shahryar has spared Scheherazade. In his city, jinn (Singam, Qumqam, Sakhrabout, Zarmabaha) test ordinary people. A merchant is ordered by a jinni to kill the governor; police chief Gamasa al-Bulti frees a jinni and is reborn as a porter; Aladdin and Sindbad appear [S35][S36].
- **Style & structure:** 17 interlinked stories; characters cross from one tale to another; the city is the real hero [S36].
- **Themes:** Power and corruption; free will (the jinn tempt, but humans choose); justice; the ruler's repentance.
- **Why it reached the world:** Mahfouz's Nobel opened his work to translation; this book reached a wide readership as a modern take on a world-famous classic.
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **Jinn that test, not just attack.** Mahfouz's jinn give people choices, and the choice reveals who they are. Use this for the Ifrit's offers (to Ghadban, to Nadim).
  2. **Linked tales in one community.** Several chapters can show the same village through different people's acts, so the hidden "return" of good and evil feels like a web.
  3. **Moral consequence without sermon.** Mahfouz shows rewards and punishments landing on characters, never states the moral. This is exactly our rule for the hidden message.

## 10. Season of Migration to the North — Tayeb Salih
- **Author / year / origin:** Tayeb Salih, Sudan. Published 1966 (first in the Beirut journal *Hiwar*). English by Denys Johnson-Davies, 1969 (Heinemann) [S37].
- **Premise:** An unnamed narrator returns from study in England to his village on the Nile and meets Mustafa Sa'eed, a stranger with a hidden past in London. Sa'eed's story is told to the narrator in pieces, and after Sa'eed probably drowns in the Nile, the narrator is left with his family and his secret [S37][S38].
- **Style & structure:** A story told to a listener (Sa'eed confides in the narrator, who now tells us). Layers of memory, confession, village gossip; the reader assembles the truth. It is a mirror of *Heart of Darkness* in reverse.
- **Themes:** Colonialism; identity between two worlds; violence and desire; the stranger in the village.
- **Why it reached the world (verified):** In 2001 the Arab Literary Academy (Damascus) named it the most important Arabic novel of the 20th century; translated into more than 30 languages [S37].
- **Techniques to borrow:**
  1. **A tale handed from one witness to another.** Sa'eed → narrator → reader matches Ghareeb → old Nadim → children → reader. Let each layer leave a mark (Nadim's own hesitations).
  2. **The stranger who comes to the village.** "Ghareeb" literally means "stranger". Salih shows how a village reacts to someone who doesn't belong: suspicion, gossip, rumour. Use this in Ch 1–3 for Theyab.
  3. **The river/water as the end.** The Nile's pull and drowning; our equivalent is the well, the sabkha or the sinkhole under Ubar.

# Part 2: Gulf Folklore Told to Children

### How parents used these figures
Across the Gulf, most of these creatures were "scare stories" used for a practical reason: keep children indoors at the dangerous midday heat (fear of sunstroke), keep them home after dark, stop them going near the sea, wells or palm groves alone [S39][S40]. Emirati author and folklorist Abdulaziz Almusallam says Gulf ghost stories were meant first to educate, and that in many Arabic stories the hero is defeated in the end, unlike American ghost tales [S41]. A Sharjah Institute for Heritage encyclopedia of mythical creatures in Emirati heritage (Almusallam, 2017) lists around 37 beings; it is the best single source to check names [S42][S43].

The UAE word for these tales is **khurrafa / khrareef** (خراريف, singular خروفة), "fanciful tales" [S43][S44]. In Kuwait and Qatar the word is **hazawi / haza'a** (حزاوي) (**uncertain**: commonly used, but I found no source that defines it). In Najd (Saudi), storytelling went with coffee at gatherings, recently revived through the *rawi* tradition [S45].

---

### A. Well attested

**1. Umm al-Duwais (أم الدويس), UAE (especially Ajman), Kuwait, Qatar**
- **What she is:** A female jinn. A beautiful woman with long black hair and a strong, intoxicating perfume, sometimes with cat-like eyes. One account gives her blades for hands [S46][S47]. (Commonly said but **unverified** here: that *duwais* refers to a sickle-like blade, and that she hides a donkey's hoof under her robe.)
- **The story:** She appears to men alone at night, lures them with scent and beauty, and when they follow her she turns hideous and kills them with her blade. Some versions say she kills only unfaithful men [S46].
- **Lesson:** Warns men against being tempted by strangers; warns everyone not to wander at night [S46]. Her name is still used as a joke for women in heavy perfume and make-up.
- **Used on children:** To keep them indoors at night, especially in Ajman [S43][S48]. A 2013 horror film, *Djinn*, drew on her [S46].

**2. Bu Darya / Baba Darya (بو دريا / بابا دريا), "Father of the Sea", UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait**
- **What he is:** A sea jinn: a giant, half-human, half-amphibian or fishlike figure. "Darya" is Persian for "sea" [S49].
- **The story:** He rises in the darkest nights, calls to pearl divers and sailors with a human voice, then drags them overboard and eats them, or steals the day's pearls from sleeping crews. Survivors were thought to bring bad luck, so they kept quiet [S49][S50].
- **Lesson / custom:** Crews kept a night watch; sailors were warned not to answer voices from the water. In some versions he can be scared off by making noise or showing a blade [S49] (that last detail **uncertain**).
- **Used on children:** To keep them off the beach and away from the sea at night.

**3. Hmarat al-Qayla / Humarat al-Gayla (حمارة القايلة), "Donkey of the Midday", whole Gulf, Najd**
- **What she is:** A half-woman, half-donkey (sometimes donkey legs on a human body, or a donkey face). Also called **Umm Hmar** ("Donkey Lady") in Qatar [S39][S51][S52]. *Qayla/gayla* means the hot noon hour; *qayloula* is the midday nap [S53].
- **The story:** She roams the empty lanes and palm gardens in the noon heat and eats, or carries off, any child she finds outside [S51].
- **Lesson:** Stay indoors and rest at noon. The real danger was sunstroke [S40].
- **Used on children:** Exactly that, at the midday nap. In Abu Dhabi there is even a children's game named after her; in Al Kuwais (Al Ain) the version used a camel [S53]. An Abu Dhabi heritage record notes the tale left lasting fear in many who heard it [S52].

**4. Khattaf Rafai / Khattaf Raffay (خطاف رفاي), "the snatcher who…", UAE coast**
- **What he is:** A mirage spirit of the sea, sometimes a "snatcher of women" [S43][S48].
- **The story:** He shows fishermen gold, pearls or whatever they want; as they row toward it, it moves away until they are lost at sea. One telling has him moving to Sharjah town with the last pearl divers in the 1940s and blocking roads instead [S48].
- **Lesson:** Greed leads you to ruin; trust what is real, not what shimmers.
- **Use for us:** A perfect partner to the desert mirage (Hilat al-Sarab means "the mirage").

**5. Abu Fanous / "Father of the Lantern" (أبو فانوس), Saudi, Gulf deserts**
- **What it is:** A light seen at night in the desert that moves like a lantern [S54] (secondary source).
- **The story:** It leads travellers deep into the desert, then vanishes, leaving them lost to die.
- **Lesson:** Don't follow strange lights; stay with the caravan.

**6. Umm al-Sa'af wa al-Leef (أم السعف والليف), "Mother of palm fronds and palm fibre", UAE, Saudi**
- **What she is:** A witch or jinn living in palm trees, dressed in fronds and fibre [S54][S44].
- **The story:** She waits in the palm grove to snatch children. One explanation: palms rustle and moan in the wind, their shadows move at night [S44].
- **Lesson:** Stay out of the palm gardens alone, especially at night.

**7. Ghoul / Ghula (غول / غولة), plural Ghilan (غيلان), across Arabia**
- **What it is:** A pre-Islamic desert demon, a kind of jinn. Lives in wastelands and graveyards, shape-shifts (often into a beautiful woman, or a hyena) to lure travellers off the path and eat them. Often given donkey hooves as a tell [S55][S56].
- **Lesson:** Don't leave the road; don't trust a stranger in the empty desert.
- **Folk detail (uncertain):** In some old Arabic accounts a ghoul must be killed with a single blow, as a second blow revives it. This was **not confirmed** by my sources; check classical sources before relying on it.
- **Used on children:** "Al-Ghula will eat you" is a general bogey across the Gulf; listed among famous UAE khrareef [S44].

**8. Nasnas (نسناس), Arabian / Yemen / Hadramawt tradition**
- **What it is:** Half a person: half a head, half a body, one arm, one leg, hopping very fast. Said to be the child of a jinn called the shiqq and a human [S57][S58]. Linked to wilderness and ruins.
- **Uncertain:** Classical geographers placed it in Yemen/Hadramawt, but my searches found no source confirming the region. Not clearly a Gulf children's bogey.

**9. Bahla, Oman: "the city of jinn"**
- **The legend:** Bahla's great mud-brick wall (about 12 km long) was raised by jinn in one night. One version: two jinn sisters, one built the wall, the other dug the *falaj* water channels. Bahla has long had a reputation for sorcery and magic; a man accused of witchcraft is said to have been killed inside the fort [S59][S60].
- **Why the legend grew (theory):** Historians suggest rulers spread jinn tales to keep people within the walls and out of the dangerous desert [S60].
- **Today:** Many residents dislike discussing it; younger Omanis call it grandparents' stories [S59]. Bahla Fort is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **Use for us:** Real Omani interior, real 1920s place, fits Ch 6–10 travel and our wall-and-tower Ubar.

**10. Umm al-Sibyan (أم الصبيان), "Mother of Children", wider Arab world, UAE tale**
- **What she is:** A female demon who attacks babies and mothers in childbirth. Protective charms against her appear in Arabic magic manuscripts [S61]. Also a title in Dubai Abulhoul's Emirati khrareef series [S43].
- **Lesson / custom:** Amulets, prayers and vigilance around newborns and mothers.
- **Use for us:** Our story ends at a cradle. She is the natural threat around a newborn.

### B. Partly attested (names confirmed, details thin)

**11. Bu Salasil (بو السلاسل), "Father of the Chains", UAE.** Named in Emirati heritage lists and in Dubai Abulhoul's 2021 children's series (Kalimat) [S43][S48]. Usually described as a creature who drags chains, heard at night (**description uncertain**; I found no detailed account).

**12. Umm al-Hailan (أم الهيلان), UAE.** Listed among famous Emirati khrareef and a title in the same series [S43][S44]. **Description not found.**

**13. Al-Si'luwa / Sa'luwa (السعلوة; classical *si'lat*), Gulf, Iraq, Najd.** A female ogre related to the ghoul. One Saudi source treats her as close to the Hmarat al-Qayla, with a human body and a donkey's face and legs [S62]. Iraqi and Gulf versions often make her a river or palm-grove witch who takes men as husbands (**uncertain**). A related Bedouin figure, the **Salu'ah**, is a wolf-like demon woman [S63].

**14. Naqat al-Eid (ناقة العيد), "the Eid camel", UAE.** Eats greedy children who overeat at Eid [S48].

**15. Tantal (طنطل), Kuwait and Iraq.** A tall shape-shifting ghost that kidnaps children [S64].

**16. Al-Seolu (Kuwait).** Described as a revived monster with huge teeth who eats children [S64] (spelling likely a variant of Si'luwa; **uncertain**).

**Not found / not verified:** "al-Mughaira", "Khatfa", "Fattamah", "Shaqiyah" as named Gulf creatures. I found no reliable source; they may be local or misremembered names. Leave them out or invent openly.

### C. Bedouin and Gulf beliefs about jinn
(Kept to beliefs and customs; our book rules forbid quoting religious texts.)
- **Times:** Dusk (*maghrib*) is the dangerous hour when jinn spread out; children are gathered in, doors shut, vessels covered and water-skins tied at nightfall (prophetic tradition, widely practiced) [S65]. Noon is the second danger time in folk belief (Hmarat al-Qayla) [S40].
- **Places:** Ruins and abandoned settlements, graveyards, caves, wells, wadis, rubbish and unclean places, lone trees [S65][S66]. Jinn are said to gather in deserted places.
- **Protection:** Saying the name of the Creator before acts (entering, pouring, eating); reciting protective verses; amulets (silver cases with written verses, blue beads against the evil eye); **iron** is widely believed to keep jinn away [S67]. Salt is used in folk practice but is not part of formal religion [S68].
- **Asking permission:** Many Gulf people ask leave (*dastoor*) before pouring hot water on the ground, so as not to scald unseen jinn (**not found in written sources**; widely reported orally; treat as uncertain).
- **Conflict:** In one Bedouin ethnography (Negev), jinn are believed to feed on conflict and uncontrolled anger, so their appearance signals trouble in the community [S69]. Strong link to our hidden message.
- **Dogs (not sourced in this search; check):** In Arab tradition black dogs are sometimes linked to devils; a jinn-touched dog is a recognizable idea, but the saluki is the honoured, "clean" hunting dog of the Bedouin (supports Ghareeb's double nature).
- **Shape-shifting:** Jinn are said to take the form of snakes, black dogs, cats, whirlwinds (the zawba'a dust devil).

### D. Storytelling traditions
- **Grandmothers' tales:** In the UAE, *khrareef* were told mostly by grandmothers and mothers at bedtime, on roofs on summer nights, or in the family courtyard [S43][S44]. Collectors say the tradition faded with television and modern life; Zayed University students compiled the collection *Khrareef* when they found younger siblings didn't know the characters [S43].
- **Revival:** Sharjah Radio narrator Mariam Al Shehhi ("Umm Salim"); Abdulaziz Almusallam's collections (*Hikayat Khurafiya*, 2011) and his TV series *Kharayef* (2002), which he himself had Sharjah TV pull after six episodes (reason not confirmed) [S41][S43]; Dubai Abulhoul's five-book series (Kalimat, 2021: *Qawm al-Dasis*, *Umm al-Sibyan*, *Khattaf Rafai*, *Bu al-Salasil*, *Umm al-Hailan*) [S43].
- **Delivery:** Saudi storyteller Nawaf Al-Huwaimel explains fear depends on the teller's voice and on setting the scene to match the listeners' own surroundings [S54]. (Ideal for our elder at the fire.)
- **Men's gatherings:** In Najd and among Bedouin, men told tales and poetry over coffee in the majlis or by the fire at night [S45].

# Part 3: Recommendations for Our Book

1. **Put Hmarat al-Qayla in Ch 1–3.** Village mothers in Hilat al-Sarab call children indoors at noon with her name. Theyab, an outcast, has no one to call him in, so he is the only child out in the noon heat. A small, real custom that shows his loneliness at once and gives the elder frame a familiar shiver.

2. **Use Umm al-Duwais for the Nameless's lure (Ch 12–14).** Nadim's betrayal can begin with a perfumed woman met at night on the road, a cult agent wearing the legend as a disguise. Readers who know the tale will feel dread before anything happens.

3. **Make the ghilan of Ch 4–8 follow the classic rules:** they take shapes of travellers, have donkey-hoof tracks Ghareeb can smell and see, and lure people off the path. Add Abu Fanous lights over the dunes in the Rub' al Khali crossing. Give Jasir (Ch 7) a mirage story borrowed from Khattaf Rafai: he followed shimmering wealth and lost his caravan.

4. **Model the Ifrit's backstory on "The Fisherman and the Jinni".** Sealed under Ubar for centuries, it first swore to reward its freer, then swore to punish. This makes the hidden message structural: even the monster is a kindness/cruelty that "returned."

5. **Build Ubar from the real Shisr plus the City of Brass.** Ring of towers around a well; a city that sank into a limestone cavern; ancient caravan tracks only visible from the high dunes; frankincense lamps for the Night of Lamps. Borrow City of Brass images: warning words on the walls, men who look in and cannot stop themselves, a perfectly preserved dead ruler.

6. **Bring in Umm al-Sibyan at the cradle (Ch 20 and epilogue).** Hessa's newborn is guarded with an amulet and an iron blade under the pillow, as real Gulf mothers did. The grey's last attempt on the baby wears her shape; Ghareeb is the one who won't be frightened away (already a planned "return").

7. **Use Bahla as a travel stop (Ch 9–10) if the route allows.** The jinn-built wall and the town's sorcery reputation give real-world texture; a sorcerer of Bahla could be an Ubar informant, or Old Bakhit's teacher.

8. **Keep the frame very short (Gaiman, Salih).** Prologue and epilogue under 600 words each. Let old Nadim falter once mid-book (a single italic interruption around Ch 14, the betrayal), as Sa'eed's confession breaks in Salih. Use the storyteller habit of matching the scene to the listeners' fire and wind.

9. **One nested "tale within a tale" in Nights style.** In Ch 10 or 16, Old Bakhit tells the story of Ghadban, the first bearer, as a fireside tale with its own opening formula ("It was, or it was not…" style, in our own words). This echoes the frame and gives readers the Ifrit's origin in folklore voice.

10. **Epigraphs.** Use short epigraphs from public-domain sources only: the Nights (Burton or Lane translations, pre-1927), Bertram Thomas's *Arabia Felix* (1932: check copyright in your markets), or original "old Bedouin sayings" written by you. Avoid quoting religious texts, per the direction file. Avoid modern novels unless permission is cleared.

11. **Add a glossary and a short "Notes on the Folklore" at the back.** Glossary of 30–40 terms (*falaj, ghaf, sabkha, majlis, khanjar, thaler, qayla, ghilan, zawba'a, khrareef*). Notes on the folklore: one paragraph each on the real creatures, plus a short note on the real 1992 Ubar find. This helps global readers and signals authenticity to Gulf readers (like the endnotes in translated Arab fiction).

12. **Marketing globally.** Pitch it as "Arabian folk horror" next to *Frankenstein in Baghdad* (horror + Arab folklore + literary prizes) and the evergreen desert-fable appeal of *The Alchemist*; avoid Gaiman as a lead comp (see note in Book 2). Target Arab-region outlets (Sharjah International Book Fair, Abu Dhabi International Book Fair, Emirates LitFest), plus UK/US horror lists. Consider an Arabic edition early: the IPAF and Sheikh Zayed Book Award are for Arabic-language works, and the success of Saadawi and al-Koni came through translation. The UNESCO "Land of Frankincense" and Ubar story is a ready-made press hook.
